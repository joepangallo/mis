Objective 6.1 · Part 1

Data as a strategic asset

A customer pays for the last item shown online. The warehouse cannot find it. The payment succeeded, but the business failed to keep its promise. Chapter 6 starts with this dependency: reliable transactions require reliable data, and the same records can help managers improve the next decision.

A transaction leaves evidence

A transaction is a business event, such as a purchase, refund, payment, or inventory adjustment. It consumes existing data and creates new data. Checkout reads a product price and an available quantity, records the order, and changes what can be promised to the next customer. A customer-service interaction can also generate useful transaction records.

A data-driven organization incorporates data into business processes and uses the resulting evidence to inform decisions. Collecting information is only the beginning. Somebody must know what a record means, whether it is current enough, and which action should follow from it. A dashboard nobody acts on creates little value.

The chapter separates three levels of decisions. Keep the decision horizon in view:

  • Operational decisions concern today's work, such as reserving available stock for an order or assigning a courier to a delivery.
  • Tactical decisions allocate resources over a medium horizon, such as adjusting staffing across stores using several weeks of demand.
  • Strategic decisions concern longer-term direction, such as entering a new market after studying demand, capabilities, and competitive conditions.

  • Choose whether a new service business fits the firm’s capabilities. — The question changes what business the organization will pursue.

Timely and accurate are separate tests

Accurate data correctly represents the event or entity. Timely data reaches a decision before the opportunity to act disappears. Yesterday's inventory can be perfectly accurate about yesterday and unsuitable for a promise made now. A live sensor can transmit quickly while reporting the wrong temperature because it is miscalibrated.

The chapter identifies four operational benefits when both tests are met. These are outcomes to measure, rather than features to buy:

  • Reduce errors by capturing a barcode once and validating the result, instead of asking several employees to retype the same product code.
  • Accelerate decisions by delivering reliable stock or delivery status while a worker can still change the outcome.
  • Improve efficiency by finding bottlenecks and scheduling labor, inventory, or equipment where actual demand requires it.
  • Improve customer satisfaction by making credible promises and resolving problems with enough context to avoid repeated explanations.

These benefits can reinforce one another. Fewer inventory errors can mean fewer refunds, less support work, and more customers willing to return. They can also conflict: an extra verification step may slow checkout while preventing an expensive mistake. Evaluate the whole process and the cost of being wrong.

Connect the record to competitive performance

Consider the value chain before choosing a system. Receiving stock belongs to inbound logistics; getting an order to its customer belongs to outbound logistics. Sharing a reliable quantity connects those activities. Handling the cancellation belongs to service. A single data defect can therefore create work in several places.

Porter's five forces describe competitive pressure. Existing sellers offering more reliable pickup are evidence about rivalry. Buyers comparing alternatives easily can strengthen buyer power. An inventory database may improve the retailer's response to those pressures, but installing one does not establish that the industry has become less competitive.

Ask what a rival would need to copy the improvement. Widely available software alone may produce parity. Reliable historical records, well-designed processes, trained staff, and relationships that improve the data may be harder to reproduce together. The business capability matters more than the number of records stored.

Use a baseline that can survive a comparison

A baseline describes performance before a change. Record both the result and the amount of work behind it. Twenty corrections out of two hundred records means something different from twenty out of twenty thousand. Compare similar periods and explain changes in product mix, staffing, or demand that could influence the result.

A pilot should connect the data improvement to a specific action. Faster inventory updates help only if the ordering process uses them before promising stock. A manager can test that connection by tracing canceled orders and checking whether the available quantity had reached checkout when each promise was made.

Separate two useful checks during that review:

  • Freshness check compares the time of the stock event with the time its updated quantity reached the ordering process.
  • Outcome check follows the resulting promise to fulfillment, cancellation, or correction so a technical improvement can be connected to customer experience.

Follow one event from capture to action. Identify the decision, the required freshness, the potential error, and a measurable business consequence before describing the data as an asset.

Objective 6.1 · Part 2

Data flows and responsible stewardship

A delivery address can pass through a checkout form, an order system, a warehouse application, a carrier, and a support screen. Understanding each handoff helps explain why a correct address at checkout can become an incorrect label at dispatch.

Trace the handoffs

Data flows are the movement and transformation of data within and between systems. Sources include point-of-sale terminals, transaction processing systems, web forms, mobile applications, and Internet of Things sensors. A useful map records what moves, who owns each step, how quickly it moves, and how errors are reported.

The chapter's flow has four broad stages. A business can revisit these stages repeatedly as data is reused:

  • Capture records the event at its source, including the identifier, time, and context needed to interpret it later.
  • Transform extracts records, cleans errors, validates values, standardizes formats, and may aggregate or enrich the result.
  • Store places the data in a repository suited to transactions, reporting, flexible documents, or raw analytical material.
  • Use supplies a transaction, report, recommendation, or other decision whose value depends on the earlier stages.

Three failures that look similar

A data silo exists when useful data is isolated in a department or system and cannot readily support other work. Data duplication is unnecessary repetition of the same fact. Data inconsistency occurs when records, formats, or meanings conflict. Copying every silo into one storage location can preserve all three problems.

Suppose dispatch writes an address correction in a spreadsheet while support reads the original order. The inaccessible correction is a silo problem. Maintaining the address in both places creates duplication. Showing two destinations for the same shipment creates inconsistency. Diagnosis matters because each symptom calls for a different part of the remedy.

Use concrete controls at the handoff rather than assuming the next team will notice:

  • Shared identifiers let systems agree which order, customer, or product a record describes before combining its attributes.
  • Validation rules reject or flag implausible values; their meaning must fit the event, because a planned delivery can legitimately be in the future.
  • Documented transformations preserve the meaning of dates, units, status codes, and missing values when formats change.
  • Exception ownership gives a named team responsibility for repairing rejected records and communicating the correction downstream.

Ethics follows the data

The chapter treats ethical data handling as an organizational responsibility. Its five pillars are ownership, transparency, privacy, intention, and outcomes. These ask whose interests are involved, whether people understand the flow, how exposure is limited, why data is collected, and what consequences its use actually creates.

Collecting more does not automatically make a decision better. Precise location histories may be excessive when neighborhood-level demand would answer a planning question. Public posts may lack context or originate from bots; a screenshot can preserve a disclosure after deletion. Assess origin and meaning before treating social activity as evidence of customer demand.

Objective 6.2 · Part 1

Tables, keys, and relationships

A customer places several orders, and each order contains several products. A useful database must represent those connections without confusing a person, a purchase, and a product. Designing that structure is a business reasoning task before it becomes a software task.

Name the things and their properties

A database is an organized collection of related data. A relational database management system (RDBMS) is the software used to define, store, organize, and retrieve data in related tables. The stored records are the database; the software managing them is the RDBMS. A website can query those records to populate a reusable product-page template.

A data model describes the entities and relationships the organization needs. The model should answer ordinary questions: what counts as an order, can a customer place more than one, and can one product appear on several orders? Work through these questions with the people who perform the process.

The relational vocabulary separates four levels that are easy to mix up:

  • Entity means a kind of thing being described, such as a customer, product, order, or employee.
  • Table organizes records about an entity or relationship into rows and columns with defined meanings.
  • Record means one row, such as the information describing order 501 rather than every order in the company.
  • Attribute means a property represented by a column, such as an order date, quantity, or customer identifier.

Choose types and document meaning

A data type specifies the kind of value a field holds, such as text, a number, or a date. A quantity can be used in arithmetic; a postal code is an identifier even if it contains digits. Treating a postal code as a number can discard a meaningful leading zero.

A data dictionary, also called a metadata repository, explains field names, types, valid values, business meanings, and uses. Metadata is data describing other data. Document whether a price includes tax, which currency it uses, and whether a timestamp represents ordering or shipping before people compare totals.

Business rules describe permitted business behavior and can guide validation. An order line may require a positive quantity; a completed shipment cannot precede the order. State exactly what event a field records. Applying a blanket ban on future dates would incorrectly reject planned delivery appointments.

Identify and connect rows

A primary key identifies each row uniquely and cannot be null. It can contain one column or a combination. Names make poor identifiers when different people can share a name or one person can change it. A stable customer_id lets the business retain the relationship while correcting descriptive details.

A foreign key references a key in a related table. Orders.customer_id can reference Customers.customer_id. Repeated customer identifiers in Orders are useful: the same customer can place many orders. An enforced relationship prevents an order from pointing to a nonexistent customer.

An entity-relationship diagram (ERD) draws entities as boxes and relationships as lines. Read the relationship in both directions:

  • One-to-one associates at most one related record on each side, such as an employee and an assigned locker when the stated policy permits one each.
  • One-to-many lets one customer have many orders, while each order in this model belongs to one customer.
  • Many-to-many lets an order contain many products and a product appear in many orders; an OrderLines table records each pairing.

Read an unfamiliar model with a business user

For each table, finish the sentence “one row represents…” before choosing its key. One row might represent an order, an order line, or a delivery attempt. Those are different events. Counting delivery attempts as orders could overstate demand whenever a courier tries a destination more than once.

Then ask whether a relationship is required. Can an order exist before a customer is assigned, and can a product appear on no order yet? The answers determine additional rules. Start with the business relationship, give each row a dependable identity, and place each fact where its meaning belongs. Related tables remain tables; joining them does not create a literal third spatial dimension.

Objective 6.2 · Part 2

Normalization and master data

An instructor changes a phone number. If that number appears beside every student's grade, a single correction becomes hundreds of edits. The chapter uses this kind of repetition to explain normalization, then extends the problem across departments through master data management.

Store a fact where it belongs

Normalization organizes relational tables so attributes describe the appropriate entity or relationship and unnecessary duplication is reduced. Separating Students, Courses, Instructors, Teaching, and Grades lets a phone number live with the instructor while a student's grade belongs with the relevant enrollment.

The purpose is reliable maintenance. A table that repeats an instructor's contact information on every grade row can show several different phone numbers after an incomplete update. It can also make adding a new instructor awkward before anyone has enrolled. Removing the last enrollment should not erase the instructor's existence.

The same pattern appears in a business order file. Separate current master records from facts specific to the transaction:

  • Customers stores the current customer name and contact details once per customer identifier, so corrections have a clear home.
  • Products stores shared product descriptions and current catalog information once per product identifier, rather than beside every sale.
  • Orders records order-level facts such as the date and the customer reference, which apply to the purchase as a whole.
  • OrderLines records quantity and agreed unit price for each line, preserving what happened in that particular transaction.

Repetition is not always a mistake

Repeated foreign-key values are how relationships work. Two orders from customer C1 should both reference C1. A price agreed last month is a different fact from today's catalog price. A shipping address preserved on a completed order may be a historical snapshot, while the customer table holds the current contact address.

Ask whether two fields represent the same fact at the same time and for the same purpose. If they do, independently editing both is risky. If they describe different events or times, merging them can destroy meaning. Normalization is not an instruction to delete every repeated string.

The database approach brings several related advantages over applications that each maintain separate files:

  • Consistency and quality improve when shared rules and authoritative records reduce opportunities for conflicting updates.
  • Access and standards become easier to coordinate when fields have common definitions and permissions are managed deliberately.
  • Program–data independence separates application logic from data management, reducing some maintenance work when applications evolve.
  • Development productivity improves when new applications can reuse established structures and validation instead of inventing another private file.

These are potential advantages, not automatic guarantees. A centralized database can contain bad data, and incompatible schema changes can still break applications. Governance, testing, and appropriate access remain necessary. The key benefit is a shared place to manage the problem consistently.

Agree on the actors across systems

Master data describes core actors and things shared across business processes, such as customers, suppliers, products, and employees. Objective 6.2 · Part 3

Queries that answer business questions

Which orders arrived late, and which supplier supplied the affected products? The answer is stored across related records. This section turns a business question into a query you can run, then asks whether the resulting evidence supports the decision.

Capture, retrieve, present

A form provides fields through which a person can enter or change data. Good forms show required information and understandable validation messages. Much data also arrives automatically from checkout systems and sensors. Whichever channel captures it, the same business meanings and quality controls should apply.

A query requests data from a database. Structured Query Language (SQL) expresses that request in a language used with relational systems. A report organizes results for people to read. A report generator helps create that presentation, sometimes through a visual interface. Changing a chart cannot repair a query that selected the wrong records.

Use a precise question before writing SQL. “Find problems” leaves the analyst to invent a definition. “Show order identifiers and delay days for completed orders delayed by more than two days, largest delay first” specifies the fields, population, condition, and ordering. That is enough to check whether the result is correct.

Four clauses handle the first kind of request. Read them as instructions about a table:

  • SELECT names the columns or expressions to return, so the result includes the information needed for the decision.
  • FROM identifies the table containing the records, such as Orders rather than the separate Customers table.
  • WHERE limits the rows to those meeting a condition, such as delay_days greater than two.
  • ORDER BY arranges the results, with DESC placing the largest numeric values first for a priority list.

For example, SELECT order_id, delay_days FROM orders WHERE delay_days > 2 ORDER BY delay_days DESC returns the late-order priority list from the practice data below. Select only the fields a recipient needs. The query does not need customer contact details to show which order identifiers require investigation.

Join for context and group for a pattern

A JOIN combines related rows through matching keys. The practice tables link each order's supplier_id to the supplier record. Prefixing columns with table aliases, such as o.supplier_id and s.supplier_id, makes the relationship explicit. Matching unrelated numeric columns can produce plausible-looking but meaningless results.

GROUP BY groups records for a summary. COUNT(*) counts rows in each group; SUM totals a numeric expression; AVG computes an average. Name calculated results with AS so a reader can tell what each column means. Always establish what one row represents before counting it.

Objective 6.2 · Part 4

From transactions to data warehouses

Checkout needs to reserve one customer's items now. A regional manager needs to compare sales, returns, and promotions over two years. Both use business data, but they place different demands on its organization, freshness, and processing.

Run the business and study the business

Operational systems support the current state of business activity. Online transaction processing (OLTP) handles many concurrent transactions, often involving small, defined reads and updates. Processing an order, issuing a receipt, or updating a delivery address are examples. Fast responses and reliable changes matter to the person waiting.

Informational systems support analysis and decision making using historical or point-in-time data. A manager may compare branches, investigate customer groups, or evaluate a promotion. Online analytical processing (OLAP) supports examining summarized data from different perspectives. The question often spans many records rather than changing one current transaction.

The chapter contrasts their design priorities. Use the workload, rather than the job title of the person asking, to classify the need:

  • Operational work reads and changes current records through narrow requests, such as confirming that the last available item can be reserved.
  • Informational work integrates and compares records across time or business areas, such as finding which promotions produced profitable repeat purchases.
  • Freshness requirements depend on the decision; yesterday's data may support trend analysis while being too old to stop an active fraudulent payment.
  • Resource contention can arise when broad analysis shares resources with checkout; separating workloads is one way to protect responsiveness.

Objective 6.3

Big data, document stores, and data lakes

A delivery fleet produces order records, vehicle locations, sensor messages, repair notes, and camera footage. The information does not all have the same structure or arrive at the same speed. Choosing how to manage it begins with the question the organization wants to answer.

Distinguish the three Vs

Big data refers to data whose scale, diversity, or speed creates substantial management and analysis challenges. Chapter 6 emphasizes three characteristics: volume, variety, and velocity. They describe different constraints. A large archive is not necessarily fast-moving, and a small stream can require an extremely fast response.

The fleet provides an example of each characteristic. Identify which requirement is doing the work:

  • Volume concerns the amount accumulated, such as retaining years of recordings across a large number of vehicles.
  • Variety concerns different forms and meanings, such as combining numeric readings, nested event messages, text, images, and audio.
  • Velocity concerns the rate of arrival and required use, such as reacting to a sensor alert before equipment damage occurs.

These characteristics do not guarantee useful insight. More data can include more noise, gaps, duplicated events, or biased coverage. An analyst must ask how the data was generated and whether it represents the people or situations the decision concerns. Large datasets can produce very precise answers to poorly chosen questions.

Recognize the structure

Semistructured data has identifiable elements without requiring every record to contain the same fields; a JSON event is an example. Unstructured data, such as free text or a recording, does not fit a predefined tabular business schema.

Unstructured does not mean meaningless or devoid of all internal structure. An image has a file format, and a support note has language. The point is that their business meaning needs additional interpretation before a conventional query can compare them as quantities, dates, or categories.

Use flexible documents deliberately

Non-relational databases, often called NoSQL or “not only SQL,” use models beyond conventional relational tables. A document data store holds records as documents, often using JSON-like structures. A customer order document can nest contact information, shipping details, and an array of items.

A later document might add delivery instructions without requiring that field in every older document. Flexibility helps when record structures evolve, but it does not remove the need for data modeling. Applications and database capabilities must still enforce required values, valid meanings, appropriate access, and consistency where the business needs them.

Choose a model by its access patterns and requirements. Relational systems are not inherently unable to scale or handle flexible data, and NoSQL products are not interchangeable. The chapter's useful comparison concerns design tradeoffs; it should not become a rule that large means NoSQL or that tables mean small.

Data through the five forces and value chain

A well-managed database can improve a business, but the strategic explanation must say how. Porter's five forces examine industry pressure on profitability. The primary activities follow the offering and the customer. Their roles remain useful in service businesses even where no physical factory exists:

  • Inbound logistics handles received inputs, while operations transforms inputs into the product or performs the core service work.
  • Outbound logistics delivers completed output, while marketing and sales helps customers discover, choose, and purchase the offering.
  • Service sustains the offering's value after purchase, including support, repair, and handling a return with the relevant history available.

The support activities enable work across that primary chain. They are more than background overhead:

  • Firm infrastructure includes general management, planning, and finance; the phrase does not simply mean an organization's computer hardware.
  • Human resource management recruits, develops, and manages people, while technology development improves products, processes, and know-how.
  • Procurement sources and purchases inputs. Selecting a supplier belongs here; receiving that supplier's shipment belongs to inbound logistics.

A useful analysis separates observed conditions from proposed actions and hoped-for effects. “Supplier power is high because one proprietary feed is essential and replacement takes months” describes dependence. “Document and test an alternative data feed” proposes a response. “Reduce migration time” names an outcome to measure. Read the conditions before choosing a tool

Imagine a regional seller of replacement equipment parts. Its customers compare several similar sellers and often need immediate availability. Repairing an existing part can sometimes replace buying another. New sellers can rent storefront software, but acquiring reliable catalog mappings and a stocked local range takes time.

The seller also depends on one supplier for a hard-to-replace product range. Its own duplicate product identifiers cause receiving discrepancies and customer-support investigations. Those are internal data failures. They affect its response to competitive pressure, but they do not themselves establish how much bargaining power a supplier has.

Begin with an evidence statement for every force. The hypothetical case supports these starting interpretations:

  • Rivalry is high because several comparable sellers compete on availability and price; service reliability is a plausible way to respond.
  • Buyer power is high because customers can compare alternatives and move purchases readily when availability claims fail.
  • Supplier power is high for the specialized range because credible replacement sources are scarce, regardless of whether the current supplier delivers reliably.
  • Entry pressure is moderate because software is accessible but local stock and trustworthy catalog mappings require additional effort.
  • Substitution pressure is moderate because repair meets some customers' needs, though it cannot replace every required part purchase.

These are reasoned ratings within the assumed conditions, not universal facts about parts retailing. A real analysis would investigate margins, customer concentration, switching behavior, entry costs, and repair alternatives. 6.1data-driven organization
An organization that incorporates data into processes and uses evidence to inform decisions.For example: A retailer uses observed demand to allocate stock and evaluate a new location.
6.1data flows
The movement and transformation of data within and between systems.For example: An order address moves from checkout to a carrier label.
6.1data silo
An isolated store of data that cannot readily support other organizational work.For example: Support cannot access a dispatch correction log.
6.1data duplication
Unnecessary repetition of the same fact across records or systems.For example: Five applications separately maintain a supplier’s current phone number.
6.1data inconsistency
A conflict between data representations, values, or business meanings.For example: Two systems show different current addresses for the same customer.
6.2database
An organized collection of related data supporting retrieval and use.For example: Product, customer, and order records are stored together with their relationships.
6.2relational database management system (RDBMS)
Software that defines and manages data organized in related tables.For example: The system executes a query joining customers to orders.
6.2entity
A kind of person, object, event, or concept about which data is collected.For example: Customer and Order are different things the business describes.
6.2table
A structure organizing records into rows and attributes into columns.For example: Products contains one row per product in the model.
6.2record
One row describing a particular entity or relationship instance.For example: The row for order 501 stores that order’s date and customer reference.
6.2attribute
A property represented by a column in a relational table.For example: order_date describes when an order was placed.
6.2data model
A representation of entities, relationships, and relevant structural rules.For example: The model links Customers to Orders and Orders to OrderLines.
6.2data type
The category of value a field holds, influencing valid operations and storage.For example: Quantity is numeric, while a postal code is text.
6.2data dictionary
Documentation of field names, types, meanings, valid values, and uses.For example: A price definition specifies the currency and whether tax is included.
6.2metadata
Information describing other data and its context.For example: A sensor dataset’s units and collection time describe its readings.
6.2business rules
Policies defining valid business behavior and acceptable records.For example: An order-line quantity must be greater than zero.
6.2primary key
A chosen column or combination that uniquely identifies each row and cannot be null.For example: order_id distinguishes each order even when two were placed at the same time.
6.2foreign key
A column or combination referencing a key in a related table.For example: Orders.customer_id refers to a customer in Customers.
6.2entity-relationship diagram (ERD)
A diagram showing entities and the relationships between them.For example: Boxes and connecting lines show one customer associated with many orders.
6.2one-to-one
A relationship allowing at most one related instance on each side.For example: A policy assigns each employee at most one dedicated locker and each locker to at most one employee.
6.2one-to-many
A relationship in which one instance can relate to several instances on the other side.For example: One customer can place many orders; each order has one customer in this model.
6.2many-to-many
A relationship permitting multiple related instances in both directions.For example: Orders contain products and products occur in orders, connected through order lines.
6.2normalization
Organization of relational tables to reduce unnecessary duplication and inappropriate dependencies.For example: Instructor contact details are separated from individual student grades.
6.2program–data independence
Separation of application logic from data management that reduces certain maintenance dependencies.For example: Several applications reuse a centrally managed data structure.
6.2master data
Core shared descriptions of business actors and things.For example: Customer, supplier, product, and employee records are shared across processes.
6.2master data management (MDM)
Organizational coordination of shared data identity, definitions, ownership, and quality.For example: Two divisions agree which records describe the same supplier.
6.2form
An interface with fields for entering or changing data.For example: A customer updates a delivery address using labeled input fields.
6.2query
A request that retrieves or operates on data according to specified criteria.For example: A request selects orders delayed by more than two days.
6.2Structured Query Language (SQL)
A language used to define, query, and manipulate relational data.For example: SELECT retrieves columns from rows that satisfy a WHERE condition.
6.2report
An organized presentation of data for a user.For example: A manager reads a summary of late deliveries by branch.
6.2report generator
A tool for creating organized presentations of query results.For example: A visual designer builds a recurring branch performance report.
6.2scheduled reports
Presentations produced at predefined intervals.For example: The standard fulfillment summary arrives each morning.
6.2key-indicator reports
Presentations of metrics considered critical to a goal.For example: A management panel shows on-time delivery and cost per shipment.
6.2exception reports
Presentations highlighting observations outside a defined acceptable range.For example: A list contains only orders delayed more than two days.
6.2drill-down reports
Presentations that expose more detail beneath a summary.For example: Selecting a branch reveals the late orders behind its total.
6.2ad hoc queries
Requests created to answer an unplanned information need.For example: A manager investigates orders affected by an unexpected road closure.
6.2operational systems
Systems supporting the current state and daily transactions of the business.For example: An order system reserves inventory during checkout.
6.2online transaction processing (OLTP)
Processing of concurrent business transactions through defined reads and updates.For example: The system records a purchase and produces a receipt.
6.2informational systems
Systems supporting analysis and decisions with historical or point-in-time information.For example: A manager compares two years of promotions and returns.
6.2online analytical processing (OLAP)
Analysis of summarized data from different perspectives to support decisions.For example: An analyst compares sales by product, region, and period.
6.2data warehouse
An integrated repository designed for historical analysis and reporting across sources.For example: Orders and refunds from several divisions support a consistent profitability analysis.
6.2extraction, transformation, and loading (ETL)
A process retrieving source data, preparing it, and placing the result in a destination.For example: Orders are extracted, identifiers reconciled, and accepted records loaded for reporting.
6.2data cleansing
Detection and correction or documented removal of inaccurate or inconsistent data.For example: Known date formats are standardized while ambiguous dates are flagged.
6.2denormalization
Deliberate combination or repetition of selected data to serve a workload.For example: An analytical table includes commonly queried customer and order attributes.
6.2data mart
An analytical collection limited to a business area or user group.For example: A finance team uses a selected subset of warehouse data.
6.3big data
Data whose scale, diversity, or speed creates substantial management challenges.For example: A fleet combines years of footage with rapidly arriving sensor messages.
6.3volume
The amount of data accumulated or processed.For example: An archive grows to billions of events.
6.3variety
The diversity of data structures, formats, and meanings.For example: An investigation combines invoice tables, event documents, notes, and video.
6.3velocity
The rate at which data arrives and the speed required to use it.For example: A sensor alert must trigger a response within seconds.
6.3structured data
Information following a defined, consistent organization.For example: Order rows contain typed identifiers, dates, and quantities.
6.3semistructured data
Information with identifiable elements and a flexible record structure.For example: A JSON event includes nested readings and optional device context.
6.3unstructured data
Information that does not follow a predefined tabular business schema.For example: Repair notes and camera recordings require interpretation for many analyses.
6.3non-relational databases
Systems using data models beyond conventional related tables.For example: A document store retrieves nested event records as units.
6.3NoSQL
A category name meaning not only SQL, commonly applied to non-relational databases.For example: A system uses flexible documents for evolving event records.
6.3document data store
A database organizing records as documents with potentially nested and optional fields.For example: One order document contains an array of purchased items.
6.3distributed computing
Division of processing or storage tasks among multiple computers.For example: A large processing job distributes work across a cluster.
6.3data lake
A repository preserving varied data in original formats for later processing.For example: An organization retains raw event documents, tables, and recordings with a catalog.
6.3data swamp
An inadequately governed repository whose data is hard to discover, interpret, or trust.For example: Sensor files have no documented owners, units, or collection context.

  • A. One-to-one, because every student has a unique ID. — A unique identifier does not restrict how many courses the student can take.
  • B. One-to-many, with no relationship in the reverse direction. — Both sides permit multiple related instances, so the relationship is many-to-many.
  • C. Many-to-many, recorded through an enrollment table. — correct. Enrollment records represent each student-course pairing and can hold relationship facts such as a grade.
  • D. No relationship, because the entities have different names. — Different entity names do not prevent the business from relating their records.

7. 6.2 Several invoices belong to customer C7. Which repetition is expected in a sound model?

  • A. C7 appears as a foreign key on several invoices. — correct. Many invoices can legitimately reference the same customer through a repeated foreign-key value.
  • B. C7’s current address is edited independently on every invoice. — Independent current-address copies invite inconsistency; historical invoice addresses would have a different stated meaning.
  • C. Two customer rows share C7 as their declared primary key. — A declared primary key must distinguish rows rather than identify two customer records identically.
  • D. An invoice uses a random customer identifier with no match. — An enforced required relationship should not accept a reference to a nonexistent customer.

8. 6.2 A shipping system drops the leading zero from postal code 02110. Which design choice is most appropriate?

  • A. Store it as a rounded measurement with two decimals. — A postal code identifies a place and is not a measurement requiring rounding.
  • B. Remove the code because numeric fields cannot contain zeros. — Zero is valid in both text and numbers; the issue is preserving identifier formatting.
  • C. Use the number of deliveries as the postal-code field. — Delivery counts describe a different fact and cannot substitute for a location identifier.
  • D. Represent the code as text rather than an arithmetic quantity. — correct. Text preserves meaningful leading zeros because no arithmetic is required on the identifier.

9. 6.2 An organization writes quantity must be positive in a data dictionary. What additional step makes the rule operational?

  • A. Print the dictionary with a larger heading. — Typography may help readers, but it does not cause invalid records to be rejected.
  • B. Enforce validation in the relevant data-entry paths. — correct. A documented policy needs actual validation where records enter or change in the system.
  • C. Copy negative quantities into a separate product name. — Moving an invalid quantity into a name obscures the error and changes field meaning.
  • D. Remove the quantity field from all business reports. — Hiding a field in reports leaves invalid stored data available to other processes.

10. 6.2 Each enrollment row repeats the instructor’s current office, and some rows now disagree. Which change addresses the update problem?

  • A. Create a second editable copy of the entire enrollment file. — Another editable copy adds places where office changes can become inconsistent again.
  • B. Sort enrollment rows by office before every report. — Sorting changes presentation without changing how many copies require a coordinated update.
  • C. Store current instructor details once and reference the instructor. — correct. Normalization gives the current office a clear home while relationships preserve enrollment context.
  • D. Replace instructor IDs with office numbers in all enrollments. — An office is a mutable attribute and may not uniquely identify one instructor.

11. 6.2 Two divisions disagree whether the same supplier appears in their purchasing systems. Who must lead resolution of its shared meaning?

  • A. Accountable business owners supported by the data team. — correct. MDM requires agreement about identity and responsibility that technical matching alone cannot supply.
  • B. The storage vendor acting without business input. — A vendor cannot determine business identity and definitions without accountable organizational input.
  • C. Each division independently editing the other division’s data. — Uncoordinated cross-editing can create new conflicts and does not establish authoritative ownership.
  • D. The report designer choosing the shortest supplier name. — Name length is not evidence that two records represent the same supplier.

12. 6.2 An analyst needs customer names alongside orders. What makes the join meaningful?

  • A. Matching whatever numeric columns happen to exist. — Unrelated numeric values can coincide without representing a legitimate business relationship.
  • B. Combining every customer with every order to show coverage. — Every possible pairing invents customer-order relationships and inflates results beyond the source records.
  • C. Sorting both tables alphabetically before combining rows. — Alphabetical position is unstable and does not identify which customer placed an order.
  • D. Matching the order’s customer reference to the customer key. — correct. The defined key relationship connects the correct order to its customer’s descriptive details.

13. 6.2 A manager wants only service cases open more than seven days. Which SQL clause expresses that filter?

  • A. ORDER BY to rename the selected fields. — ORDER BY arranges results and does not by itself restrict which cases are returned.
  • B. WHERE to restrict rows using the age condition. — correct. WHERE selects the rows meeting the stated condition before they are presented.
  • C. FROM to multiply the age of every case. — FROM identifies the source relation and does not perform the requested age filter.
  • D. SELECT to change stored case-closing dates. — A SELECT retrieval does not change the stored dates of the source cases.

14. 6.2 A dashboard shows a high return rate; the manager opens the transactions behind that rate. What is this?

  • A. A new transaction reserving inventory for an order. — The manager is investigating existing results rather than executing a new inventory transaction.
  • B. A scheduled report defined only by its delivery time. — The defining action is opening detail, not receiving a report at a predefined interval.
  • C. A drill-down from the summary into supporting detail. — correct. Drill-down exposes the records or lower-level detail behind a summarized performance measure.
  • D. A data-type conversion that removes all return records. — The action neither changes field types nor removes source records from the system.

15. 6.2 A travel site confirms a booking while many customers reserve other trips. What workload best fits?

  • A. OLTP handling concurrent current transactions. — correct. The site makes defined reads and updates to current booking records for concurrent users.
  • B. Historical OLAP examining ten years of travel trends. — Long-term travel analysis differs from processing the current reservation the customer is making.
  • C. MDM negotiating what the company calls a traveler. — Definition agreement supports good systems but is not the booking-processing workload itself.
  • D. A data lake cataloging raw photographs for research. — Photograph storage is unrelated to committing this current reservation and its availability change.

16. 6.2 A warehouse load maps two source date formats to an agreed format before insertion. Which ETL stage is that?

  • A. Extraction that retrieves the unchanged source rows. — Extraction obtains source records; the described change happens after retrieving them.
  • B. Loading that appends the already prepared result. — Loading writes prepared data to the destination rather than defining the conversion described.
  • C. Presentation that draws a chart from the results. — Chart presentation occurs after preparation and does not describe this source-format mapping.
  • D. Transformation that standardizes the meaning and format. — correct. Transformation prepares data for the destination, including agreed conversions and quality checks.

17. 6.2 Finance needs a limited analytical subset of a company’s integrated history. Which concept fits?

  • A. A live form for replacing every customer address. — A data-entry form supports record changes rather than providing a scoped analytical collection.
  • B. A data mart for the finance decision-support needs. — correct. A mart limits analytical scope to the relevant users and business questions.
  • C. An ungoverned archive with no defined business users. — An undefined archive lacks the intended scope and governance of the requested resource.
  • D. A duplicate primary key shared by all financial records. — Shared duplicate primary keys would undermine identity instead of organizing finance’s analytical data.

18. 6.3 A camera archive is large, but the urgent problem is reacting to alerts within one second. Which V names the deadline?

  • A. Variety of formats in the saved footage. — Different formats concern variety, whereas the question highlights how quickly an action must occur.
  • B. Volume of the archive accumulated over years. — Archive size concerns volume, while the urgent constraint is the response time.
  • C. Velocity of arrival and required use. — correct. Velocity includes the speed required to process and use data before the decision expires.
  • D. The count of entities in the relational model. — Entity counts describe model structure rather than the three-V response-time characteristic.

19. 6.3 An event document has named fields, a nested sensor object, and optional comments. Which description fits best?

  • A. Semistructured data with identifiable but flexible elements. — correct. Named elements and nesting provide structure while optional fields permit variation across records.
  • B. A rigid row requiring every future event to match exactly. — The described optional and nested fields do not require a fixed relational row shape.
  • C. Meaningless data because its fields can be optional. — Flexibility does not eliminate meaning, though the field definitions still need governance.
  • D. A scheduled report defined by a weekly delivery time. — The question describes a record format rather than when a report is delivered.

20. 6.3 A team chooses a document store for evolving order documents. What responsibility remains?

  • A. Prohibit any change in optional fields after launch. — The reason for the design includes controlled evolution rather than banning every optional-field change.
  • B. Treat every nested record as a separate physical server. — Nested structure describes document organization and does not imply a separate machine per element.
  • C. Assume the product label proves every query will be faster. — Performance depends on the workload and implementation rather than the database category alone.
  • D. Define required fields, business rules, and access. — correct. Flexible documents still need coherent meanings, validation, and appropriate protection for their uses.

21. 6.3 A team wants raw recordings, event messages, and tables for future approved analyses. Which option fits?

  • A. An undocumented folder granting unrestricted access to everyone. — An undocumented unrestricted collection loses interpretability and increases exposure rather than meeting the need responsibly.
  • B. A governed data lake retaining the original formats. — correct. A lake can preserve different original formats while a catalog and access controls maintain usability.
  • C. A form that overwrites each old value with the latest one. — Overwriting old values removes the historical material the team wants to preserve.
  • D. A rule that discards all records without numeric columns. — Discarding nonnumeric content defeats the request to retain recordings and event messages.

22. 6.3 Thousands of raw datasets have no owners, units, or source descriptions. What is the primary concern?

  • A. The warehouse has become too thoroughly normalized. — The problem is missing governance and context, not evidence of excessive relational normalization.
  • B. The organization has solved its master-data conflicts. — Missing owners and descriptions indicate unresolved management problems rather than completed coordination.
  • C. The lake is becoming difficult to interpret and trust. — correct. A data swamp emerges when people cannot reliably discover and interpret the stored material.
  • D. The data must be accurate because its volume is high. — A large number of records cannot establish their meaning, origin, or accuracy.

23. 6.4 Several firms already sell comparable subscriptions in the defined market and cut prices repeatedly. Which force is most directly evidenced?

  • A. Rivalry among existing competitors. — correct. The observed behavior concerns price competition among firms already participating in the industry.
  • B. The threat of future entrants only. — Future entry concerns firms not yet competing and the barriers they would face.
  • C. The bargaining power of input suppliers. — Input supplier leverage requires evidence about providers of resources to the focal firms.
  • D. Technology development within the firm. — Technology development is a value-chain activity rather than one of the five outside forces.

24. 6.4 A tutoring company loses demand because learners use self-study materials. Within a paid tutoring industry boundary, what does this illustrate?

  • A. A second branch of the same tutor is a supplier. — A branch is not identified as an input provider merely because demand changes.
  • B. The company’s employee records are a new entrant. — Employee records are internal resources rather than firms entering the tutoring industry.
  • C. Self-study is necessarily another identical tutoring firm. — Self-study changes how the need is met rather than providing another identical tutor.
  • D. A substitute meets the underlying need differently. — correct. Learners can meet part of the learning need through a different approach.

25. 6.4 A small group of clients controls most sales and can switch providers cheaply. Which pressure does this support?

  • A. Low rivalry proved by the presence of customer records. — Customer records do not establish how intensely existing providers compete with one another.
  • B. High buyer power supported by concentration and alternatives. — correct. Concentrated buyers with credible low-cost alternatives have leverage over price and terms.
  • C. High supplier power because clients purchase the final service. — These clients buy the focal firm’s offering; input suppliers occupy a different role.
  • D. Low entry threat because client names are stored in tables. — The storage model for names does not establish barriers to entering the industry.

26. 6.4 A specialist data provider is hard to replace because converting historical records takes months. Which response targets the dependence?

  • A. Add a second copy of its logo to reports. — Branding changes do not affect whether the firm can move away from the provider.
  • B. Judge the relationship only by last week’s uptime. — Uptime measures service performance but does not measure replacement feasibility or switching cost.
  • C. Document exports and test migration to a credible alternative. — correct. Tested portability and a viable alternative directly address the costly switching condition described.
  • D. Classify the provider as a buyer of the firm’s service. — The provider supplies an essential input rather than buying the focal firm’s offering.

27. 6.4 An analyst claims a purchased database prevents new firms entering. What evidence is most needed?

  • A. Barriers competitors cannot readily reproduce or acquire. — correct. Entry barriers concern the difficulty of assembling a viable competing business, not merely owning software.
  • B. The number of colors in the database vendor’s dashboard. — Dashboard appearance does not establish difficulty or cost for an entrant to compete.
  • C. The count of internal reports printed last month. — Internal report counts do not demonstrate an obstacle confronting prospective competing firms.
  • D. A list of every employee who has a database login. — Login counts describe use within the company rather than barriers facing new firms.

28. 6.4 Supplier selection uses price and quality histories; warehouse receiving scans the delivered items. Which activities are these?

  • A. Both are marketing and sales because products are involved. — Product involvement does not turn purchasing and receiving work into customer acquisition activities.
  • B. Both are service because suppliers provide a service. — Service in the value chain concerns maintaining customer value rather than every purchased service.
  • C. Inbound logistics first and procurement second. — Selection and purchasing precede physical receipt, so these two classifications are reversed.
  • D. Procurement first and inbound logistics second. — correct. Procurement sources inputs, while inbound logistics handles their receipt and movement into the business.

29. 6.5 A project releases $4,000 of estimated staff time per month and costs $1,000 monthly to operate. What is the careful interpretation?

  • A. It guarantees $4,000 of additional cash every month. — Staff time becomes cash benefit only when costs fall or additional valuable output results.
  • B. It estimates $3,000 net capacity value, subject to realization. — correct. Subtracting operating cost gives the estimate, while actual economic value still requires evidence.
  • C. It eliminates the need to examine any one-time setup cost. — A recurring measure does not eliminate separate implementation costs from the investment decision.
  • D. It proves that the industry’s competitive rivalry has disappeared. — An internal efficiency estimate does not establish a change in industry competitive structure.

30. 6.5 A pilot reduced total support hours during a month with fewer orders. What comparison best tests improvement?

  • A. Number of stored records in the new database. — Stored record counts do not adjust for workload or establish that service became more efficient.
  • B. Number of meetings held during the implementation. — Meeting counts describe activity rather than a measured improvement in the business process.
  • C. Support hours per comparable order, with accuracy checked. — correct. A consistent denominator and an accuracy guardrail help evaluate whether the process actually improved.
  • D. The vendor’s forecast repeated as the observed result. — A forecast remains an expectation until the pilot’s outcomes provide supporting evidence.

31. 6.5 Two proposed benefits are supplier selection and supplier negotiation. What should an analyst notice when asked for two distinct value-chain areas?

  • A. They both belong to procurement in the stated example. — correct. Both tasks concern sourcing and purchasing inputs; two tools do not create two activity categories.
  • B. They must be two activities because two reports are used. — The count of reports does not determine how the underlying business work is classified.
  • C. They belong to service because a supplier serves the firm. — After-sale service to the firm’s customers differs from sourcing inputs from its suppliers.
  • D. They cannot use data because purchasing is a support activity. — Support activities can use data and create value just as primary activities can.

32. 6.5 A pilot improves processing speed but increases incorrect shipments. Which rollout decision fits an explicit accuracy guardrail?

  • A. Expand immediately because a faster process is the stated goal. — Speed is not sufficient when the agreed guardrail requires maintaining correct shipment outcomes.
  • B. Stop recording shipment errors until the pilot period finishes. — Stopping measurement hides a material adverse outcome and prevents an informed rollout decision.
  • C. Count errors as a marketing opportunity without investigation. — Reframing errors does not resolve the operational failure or its cost to customers.
  • D. Investigate the errors and correct the cause before expansion. — correct. A breached guardrail requires investigation and correction before extending the affected process.
