Two frameworks, two different questions

Both of these are tools for deciding where to spend money. They are not rival theories, and they are not interchangeable. One looks outward at the industry you are stuck in. The other looks inward at the work you actually do. Used together they answer a question a manager can act on: given the pressure we are under, which piece of our own work should we fix first?

Outside in

Porter’s Five Forces

A model of why an industry is profitable or brutal, built from five sources of pressure: rivals, new entrants, buyers, suppliers, and substitutes. Rate each one, and the strongest is the pressure your strategy has to answer.

The question it answers: where are we competing, and what is squeezing us hardest?

What it never tells you: what to build. It names a condition, not a product.

Inside out

The Value Chain Model

A map of every activity inside the organization that adds value, from receiving supplies through to looking after the customer after the sale, plus the support bands that serve all of them. Cost each activity, and you can see where a system would pay.

The question it answers: which of our own activities is worth improving?

What it never tells you: whether the improvement matters to the market.

How the two connect

Run them in order and each one supplies what the other is missing. A force names the pressure; a value chain activity is where you can actually do something about it; the initiative is the system you build there; the measure is what makes the claim checkable later.

  1. Diagnose the pressure. Rate the five forces from observable evidence, and name the strongest one.
  2. Locate the work. Walk the value chain and find the activities that touch that pressure, with their costs.
  3. Name one initiative. A concrete system in a named activity, not “improve our digital capabilities.”
  4. Attach a measure and a date. The number you expect to move, and when you will look.
Why the order matters A vendor shortlist assembled before the force is named is a set of products hunting for a problem. The diagnosis is what makes one product better than another rather than just newer.

Five Forces tells you what you are up against. The value chain tells you what you can do about it with the resources you already own. A recommendation that skips either half is a preference, not an analysis.

Reading the model without misreading it

  • Strong force means low profit, not lost cause. Strong pressure means the industry gives its profit away — to buyers as discounts, to suppliers as costs, to entrants as lost share. Firms still win inside brutal industries; they just have to earn it somewhere specific.
  • Substitutes come from outside your industry. Another coffee shop is a rival. An energy drink, a home espresso machine, and simply staying at your desk are substitutes. The distinction matters because price cuts do not win back a customer who left the category.
  • The answer expires. The internet changed all five forces at once: it widened buyer choice, lowered entry barriers, opened supplier access, and made substitution easy. Any rating is a photograph of one industry on one day.
  • Rate from evidence you could show someone. “Rivalry feels intense” is an opinion. “Every promotion we ran last quarter was matched within a week while volume stayed flat” is a finding.

Worked example — a hypothetical regional parcel-delivery firm

Read how each rating is argued. Notice that every line names something observable, and that the conclusion changes what the firm should build.

ForceRatingThe evidence behind the rating
RivalryStrongFour established carriers quote the same lanes; the last three contract renewals were decided by price alone, and each discount was matched inside a fortnight.
New entrantsModerateA van and an app get somebody started, but overnight coverage of the whole region needs depots and night sorting that a newcomer cannot rent quickly.
Buyer powerStrongTwo retail chains supply 46 percent of revenue, both run annual tenders, and both have moved volume away mid-contract before.
Supplier powerModerateFuel and vehicles are commodity purchases, but a single telematics vendor holds the tracking data and raised its per-vehicle fee twice.
SubstitutesWeakDigital delivery has already taken what it can take — documents. Physical goods still have to physically move.
What the diagnosis produced Two forces are strong, and they point the same way: revenue is concentrated in buyers who can leave, in a market where the only argument anyone makes is price. So the initiative has to give those accounts something they would lose by leaving, and it has to be something a matched discount cannot copy — delivery data the buyer's own systems come to depend on, not a lower quote. A rating exercise that stopped at “rivalry is strong” would have recommended cutting prices, which is the one move every rival can match by Friday.

Where the sliders come from

Each force is rated from four drivers, and every driver is phrased so that higher always means more pressure on you. That is the discipline the model asks for: you are not scoring how much you like the industry, you are counting conditions.

  • 0–1, weak — the condition is mostly absent. This force is not what is squeezing the firm.
  • 2–3, moderate — present and worth watching, but the firm still sets its own terms most of the time.
  • 4–5, strong — the condition decides outcomes. Terms are set by somebody other than you.
A caution about averages A force can be strong on one decisive driver and mild on the other three. If a single buyer holds half your revenue, buyer power is strong no matter how the other sliders sit. Use the average as a first read, then argue with it — the verdict panel flags any single driver you rated at the top.

Value chain analysis in three steps

The model is a drawing. The analysis is what you do with the drawing, and it runs in a fixed order for a reason: each step is impossible without the one before it.

  1. Draw the chain. Write out every activity, function and process where value is or should be added. Nobody improves a step that was never written down.
  2. Cost each area and find what drives the cost. A cost with no known driver can be complained about but not managed. “Delivery costs $340,000” is a number; “delivery costs $340,000 and 60 percent of it is drivers idling between stops” is a target.
  3. Decide what to optimise. Only now is naming a system reasonable, because only now can you say what it is supposed to change and by how much.
The most common mistake Starting at step three. A proposal that opens with a product name has skipped the drawing and the costing, so nothing in it can be checked — there is no baseline to compare against later.

It was built for factories and still fits a service business

The names come from manufacturing, so a hotel, a clinic or a software firm has to translate them. The translation is part of the analysis, not a distraction from it. Here is the same chain read three ways.

ActivityA furniture makerA hotelA subscription software firm
Inbound logisticsTimber and fittings arriving at the yardSupplies arriving, and reservations being taken and heldCustomer data and content being imported at sign-up

Some organisations have no inbound or outbound logistics worth the name and classify activities differently. The support bands, however, look much the same everywhere. The chain is a tool for analysis, not a law — but if you rename an activity you must say what you moved into it.

Worked example — costing a hypothetical regional bakery supplier

Annual figures, drawn from one year of accounts. The right-hand column is what turns the chain from a diagram into a decision: the driver is the thing a system could actually change.

ActivityAnnual costShareWhat drives it
Inbound logistics$410,00017%Flour and butter bought in small emergency lots at premium prices, because nobody knows stock levels until a baker runs out
Operations$980,00041%Overtime on night shifts, and batches scrapped when an order changes after mixing has begun
Outbound logistics$340,00014%Routes planned by hand each evening; drivers idle between stops and two vans run half empty
Sales and marketing$260,00011%Field reps re-entering orders taken on paper, with no record of what a café ordered last month
Service$150,0006%Credits issued for short or late deliveries, most of them unexplained by the time anyone looks
Support bands (all four)$260,00011%Administration, hiring and training, systems upkeep, and the buying function itself
Reading the table like an analyst The biggest number is not automatically the target. Operations is 41 percent of cost, but part of that is the product itself — bread has to be baked. Outbound logistics is less than half the size and almost all of it is waste that a routing system could take out. The question is never “where is the most money spent?” It is “where is the most money spent on things a customer would not miss?”

The test your portfolio just took

Business/IT alignment is the name for it: an organisation funds the systems that match what it is trying to be, not the systems that are most impressive. Money and time are finite, so a firm competing on low cost normally refuses a system that differentiates on quality, however good that system is — and a firm competing on service refuses the one that only shaves pennies.

  • Strategic necessity is the exception. Some investments must be made regardless of fit, because the firm cannot operate without them. Calling something a necessity is a strong claim and should be argued, not assumed.
  • The system is not the change. Any significant implementation needs matching change in processes, roles and habits. Routing software that drivers ignore saves nothing.
  • A bad business model survives good software. An information system can only be as effective as the model it serves.

    What the checklist is actually looking for

    • A force named exactly. One of the five, in the model’s own words. Not “competition,” which could mean any of three of them.
    • Evidence carrying a number. A share, a count, a frequency, a trend. Without one the rating cannot be argued with, which sounds like a strength and is a weakness.
    • Two different areas. Two support bands are usually the same idea twice; a primary activity and a support band, or two ends of the flow, are a real scan.
    • One initiative. Three initiatives is a wish list. One is a decision, and a decision is the only thing a board can approve.
    • A measure and a date. A measure with no date can be defended forever. A date with no measure has nothing to check.

    The one-page version

    1. Five Forces, outside in. Rivalry, new entrants, buyers, suppliers, substitutes. Rate each from observable evidence; name the strongest.
    2. Value chain, inside out. Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, sales and marketing, service, over four support bands: firm infrastructure, human resources, technology development, procurement. Draw it, cost it, then choose.
    3. Join them. The force says what is squeezing you; the activity says where you can push back; the initiative is one concrete system in that activity.
    4. Make it checkable. A measure and a date, or it is a preference.
