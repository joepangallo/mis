Section 5–1a

Why collaboration became a strategic problem

Nobody needs a textbook to tell them that people at work talk to each other. What is worth a chapter is why that talking became a competitive matter: the right people for a problem are almost never in the same building, and assembling them anyway is now something a firm either does well or loses money on.

The problem is assembling, not talking

To stay competitive an organization has to bring together the right combination of people — the knowledge, the skills, the information and the authority to actually decide — quickly and repeatedly.

The traditional answer was the task force: a temporary work group with a finite task and a life cycle, pulled together to solve something the existing departments could not. Task forces still exist, and they are slow. Structure and logistics get in the way of people trying to get anything done.

So organizations increasingly build virtual teams instead: members drawn from different geographic areas, assembled as needed for a particular project. Membership is fluid. Teams form and disband, size moves up and down, and one person may sit on several at once.

Two examples from the chapter show how ordinary this has become, and neither of them is a technology company doing something clever.

  • Software development routinely puts the programmers in one country, the project managers in another and the testers in a third, working on one release.
  • Health care assembles dieticians, physicians, surgeons, pharmacists and social workers from different cities to coordinate the care of a single patient.
  • What both share is that the specialist a problem needs is no longer limited to whoever happens to practise or program nearby.

The tools come in three families

The chapter groups collaboration tools into three categories, and the grouping is more useful than it looks, because each family answers a different question about a team.

  • Electronic communication tools carry verbal and written information and move files between people — instant messaging, email, voice mail, blogs, wikis and ordinary websites.
  • Electronic conferencing tools support richer interaction than a message can carry, such as screen sharing and videoconferencing, where the point is watching something together.
  • Collaboration management tools run the group itself rather than the conversation: electronic calendars, knowledge management systems, intranets and shared online document systems.

Electronic communication tools — Carry verbal and written information, and move files and content between people.

  • Email — The chapter's first example of a communication tool: it conveys written information and carries attachments, and it does nothing to manage the group sending them.
  • Voice mail — A recorded verbal message waiting for its recipient. It is the oldest asynchronous tool most workplaces still run, and it belongs with the other message carriers.
  • A public wiki page — The chapter lists wikis with blogs and static websites as communication tools, because their job is conveying content. That they are jointly written is a fact about how the content arrives, not about what the tool is for.

Electronic conferencing tools — Support information sharing and rich interaction, where the point is doing something together in the moment.

  • Screen sharing — Nothing is conveyed that could have been written down instead. The value is that two people are looking at the same thing at the same moment and can point at it.
  • Videoconferencing — The richest interaction in the chapter's list: tone, expression and the ability to interrupt, none of which survives a written message.
  • A live customer chat window — Instant messaging appears in both of the first two families, and a live chat with an agent sits on the conferencing side because it is a real-time exchange rather than a message left to be collected.

Collaboration management tools — Facilitate meetings and manage the group's activity rather than carrying the conversation itself.

  • A shared electronic calendar — It carries no content at all. Its whole function is arranging when the group can be in the same place, which is managing the collaboration rather than conducting it.
  • A knowledge management system — It organises what the organization already knows so somebody can find it later. That is group activity management, and the chapter files it here rather than with the message carriers.
  • A shared online document system — One authoritative copy that several people can work on is a way of managing an activity. The chapter groups these with intranets and calendars for exactly that reason.

Same time, or not; same place, or not

The distinction that actually decides which tool to reach for is not the family. It is time. Synchronous communication is coordinated in time — a call, a live chat, a videoconference — and asynchronous communication is not, which covers email, texting and a discussion board.

Meetings of virtual teams take the form of virtual meetings and can run either way. When time matters, because a deadline is close or a customer is angry, synchronous media are usually better: every hour of delay is process inefficiency or a dissatisfied customer, and both are expensive.

Cross time with place and you get the four modes collaboration software is built to serve. Step through them and read what each one costs and buys.

Same time, same place — Everyone is in one room, and the technology is in the room with them.

Everyone free at once (And able to reach one building) → Electronic meeting room (Shared screen, shared display) → The argument happens live (Objections raised and answered) → Decision recorded (Written down before anyone leaves)

  • The fastest way to close a genuine disagreement, because objections are answered in the moment rather than in a reply tomorrow.
  • The most expensive mode by a wide margin once travel is counted, and the cost falls on people rather than on equipment.
  • Its failure mode is the meeting that did not need to be a meeting: an hour of several salaries spent on something an email would have settled.

Same time, different places — A synchronous distributed meeting: the clock is shared, the building is not.

A decision is urgent (Waiting a day would cost something) → Videoconference or call (Everyone joins from where they are) → Live discussion (Tone and interruption survive) → Decision, then a written note (Because nobody shared the room)

  • This is the mode that replaced most business travel, and the one the chapter says a virtual team lives in when a deadline is close.
  • Time zones are the hidden cost. A meeting convenient in three places is unpleasant in the fourth, and that seat is where attendance quietly stops.
  • Its failure mode is a decision nobody wrote down, because the shared room that used to produce minutes was never there.

Different times, same place — An electronic meeting facility used as a home base, worked on in shifts.

Work continues in shifts (Not everyone is present at once) → A shared room or board (State is left where it stands) → Each shift picks it up (Reads what the last one left) → Progress without a meeting (Nobody waited for anybody)

  • The least discussed of the four and the most familiar in practice: a shift handover, a laboratory bench, a design board a team walks past.
  • It buys continuity without coordination, which is exactly what a firm wants when the work is continuous and the people are not.
  • Its failure mode is context that lived in somebody's head, so the next shift repeats the reasoning instead of the work.

Different times, different places — The virtual team in its purest form, and the mode most of this chapter is about.

Members across time zones (No shared hour worth using) → Discussion board, email, wiki (Everything asynchronous) → Each contributes when able (The record is the meeting) → Work moves around the clock (Handed on rather than paused)

  • The only arrangement that lets a firm use expertise wherever it happens to be, which is the reason virtual teams exist at all.
  • It is also the slowest to resolve a real disagreement, because every exchange costs a full turnaround rather than a sentence.
  • Its failure mode is a question posted on a Friday afternoon in one time zone and answered on a Tuesday in another, with the deadline in between.

Collaboration software grew out of groupware, the class of software that lets people work together more effectively, and the first mainstream product of that kind appeared in 1989.

What has changed since is the price rather than the idea. Most of these capabilities are now free to anyone with a connected device, so the interesting question moved from whether a firm can afford them to whether it can get anybody to use them.

The cost of getting the mode wrong

Choosing synchronous when asynchronous would do wastes people. Choosing asynchronous when a decision is urgent wastes days. The case below puts numbers on both, for a hypothetical firm.

1. The exhibit separates working time from waiting time. What does that separation tell the firm?

  • A. The engineers are slow, so the fix is to set a reply deadline on internal email — Three hours of work spread over nine days is not slowness. A reply deadline would compress the waiting a little and would not touch the thing producing it, which is that every exchange costs a full turnaround.
  • B. The delay is turnaround, not effort, so the fix is a mode change rather than more effort — correct. Three hours of actual work took nine days because the medium is asynchronous and the question needed several rounds. Anything that removes rounds, or removes the wait inside a round, attacks the real cause.
  • C. The cost analyst is the bottleneck, so that role should be duplicated in each country — The cost analyst waited longest, but pricing could not begin until the first two had agreed what was being priced. Duplicating that role removes the last wait and leaves the two before it untouched.
  • D. The question was too complex for email, so it should have gone to a formal task force — A task force is the slow traditional answer the chapter contrasts virtual teams against. Standing one up adds structure and logistics to a question that three people could settle in twenty minutes.

2. The engineering manager proposes one scheduled videoconference with all three specialists instead of the email round. What is the honest cost of that choice?

  • A. It removes the record, since a live discussion produces nothing anybody can look up later — A live meeting does lose the automatic written trail, and that is a real risk worth managing with a written summary. It is not a cost the firm pays in money or in goodwill, which is what the question is asking about.
  • B. It requires the dedicated room system the firm has not budgeted for this year — Desktop videoconferencing needs a webcam, a microphone, software and a connection, all of which a laptop already has. The expensive room systems the chapter prices are a separate category of purchase.
  • C. It moves the work to a synchronous tool, which the chapter treats as the weaker choice — The chapter treats synchronous and asynchronous as suited to different situations rather than ranking them. When a deadline is close, it says synchronous media are usually the better fit.
  • D. It needs an hour that works in three time zones, and someone pays for that in their evening — correct. A synchronous meeting across three time zones has no hour that is convenient everywhere, so somebody joins outside their working day. That cost is real, it is unevenly distributed, and it is why attendance quietly decays.

3. Suppose the change turns out to be routine and this same question arrives roughly twice a month. What does that change about the recommendation?

  • A. Nothing, because the mode that suits a question does not depend on how often it arrives — Frequency changes the arithmetic completely. A one-off question is worth interrupting people for; a question that recurs twice a month is worth engineering out of existence.
  • B. It argues for a recurring meeting, so the three specialists are already booked together — A standing meeting is a real improvement over waiting for email, and it still spends three salaries on every instance of a question that has already been answered before.
  • C. It argues for writing down the answer once, so most future questions skip the specialists — correct. A recurring question is a knowledge problem wearing a coordination problem's clothes. Recording the reasoning where the next person can find it is what the chapter's collaboration management tools are for, and it is the answer the next section develops.
  • D. It argues for hiring a fourth specialist who can answer all three parts alone — One person who covers materials, manufacturing and costing is not a realistic hire, and the whole reason this team is distributed is that each specialism sits where its specialists are.

What this case was really about. Three hours of work took nine days, and no individual was slow. The delay came from the mode: an asynchronous medium multiplied by several rounds of clarification. That is the chapter's point about time being the distinction that matters. Notice also where the case ended up. A synchronous meeting fixes one instance and costs somebody their evening; writing the answer down once fixes every future instance and costs nothing after the first. Coordination problems that recur are almost always knowledge problems, which is exactly why the next section is about intranets and search rather than about meetings.

Videoconferencing, from a theme park to a laptop

Video calling was demonstrated to theme-park audiences in the 1960s and then took about thirty years to matter. What changed was the shopping list.

  • Desktop videoconferencing needs four things: a webcam, a microphone and speakers, the software, and a connection fast enough to carry it.
  • Most laptops arrive with three of the four, and the software is generally free, so the marginal cost of one more meeting is close to nothing.
  • Dedicated room systems still exist, and the chapter prices high-end installations in the hundreds of thousands of dollars, which is a capital purchase rather than a download.

That gap is this chapter in one line. A capability that used to require a budget request now requires only a decision.

Collaboration became strategic when it stopped being expensive. Once anybody can assemble the right people from anywhere, the firms that still cannot are losing to the ones that can, and losing on speed rather than on cost.

Section 5–1b

Inside the firewall: intranets, portals, and search

The previous section ended on a coordination problem that turned out to be a knowledge problem. This section is about the machinery firms build for that: a private web, a search engine that can read it, and a portal that lets an employee serve themselves.

A private web, behind the firewall

An intranet is a private network built with web technologies, used to move proprietary information securely inside one organization. It looks and behaves like an ordinary website. The difference is that its pages sit behind the company firewall, so only authorised people reach them with a browser.

Modern intranets build each employee a page rather than serving everyone the same one, which turns one site into several thousand.

  • The look and the data are shared, so every page is drawn from the same underlying systems and nobody maintains a separate copy for each department.
  • What differs is entitlement, following the principles of least permission and least privilege: somebody in human resources sees payroll and hiring figures, and somebody in the workshop does not.
  • The filtering happens at assembly, so the restricted data is absent from the page rather than present and hidden, which is a meaningful difference when a laptop is lost.

That worked while everyone was in the building. Since they are not, most firms let employees reach the intranet through a virtual private network, which carries the connection across the public internet inside an encrypted tunnel and delivers it through the firewall. Once connected, a laptop in a hotel behaves as though it were plugged in at the office.

  3. The tunnelled traffic arrives at the corporate firewall — The firewall is the boundary. Traffic arriving through the tunnel is treated as internal; the same request arriving without one would be refused here.
  4. The request reaches the intranet server inside the corporate network — Only now is the employee, in effect, inside the building. The server sees a request from the internal network rather than from a hotel.
  5. The server identifies the employee and their job function — Modern intranets build a page per person rather than serving one page to everyone, so it has to know who is asking before it can decide what to assemble.
  6. A page is assembled containing only the data that person may see — Least permission and least privilege applied in practice: the underlying data is shared, and the payroll figures a human resources employee sees are simply absent from a warehouse supervisor's copy.

Three things a corporate intranet is actually for

The chapter names three benefits, and each one replaces a specific older practice rather than adding a new capability.

  • Real-time access to information replaces the paper document that had to be updated, reprinted and redistributed every time anything changed, which meant several versions were always in circulation at once.
  • Enterprise search replaces knowing who to ask, by retrieving content across internal documents, databases, applications and cloud storage that a public search engine cannot see at all.
  • Collaboration replaces sending work back and forth, by letting one person revise a shared drawing or document and put the updated version where the next person will look for it.

The chapter's example of the first is an aircraft manufacturer distributing company news as multimedia over its own intranet, so employees see releases as they happen instead of waiting for a printed bulletin. Its example of the third is the same firm, sharing three-dimensional models of aircraft components between engineers at different sites and cutting product development time as a result.

Why enterprise search is a different problem

Searching the public web and searching a company are not the same task, and the chapter is explicit about why. Internal content sits in different languages, on different servers, in databases and applications rather than on pages, and increasingly in cloud storage a firm does not own.

A public search engine also has millions of pages linking to each other, which is most of how it decides what is important. A company has no such signal. The result is that as an intranet grows, the information in it becomes harder to find rather than easier, which is precisely the point at which employees give up and ask a colleague instead.

The portal, and the cost of a paper form

Intranets also carry employee portals, which support employee self-service: administering benefits, managing a retirement plan, submitting the ordinary human resources paperwork of a working life.

The chapter's figures for one form are worth carrying around, because each channel costs roughly an order of magnitude less than the one before it.

  • On paper the chapter puts processing at roughly twenty to thirty dollars a form, most of it the time of somebody re-keying what an employee already wrote.
  • Through a telephone system that falls to a few dollars, which is a large saving and still involves a conversation somebody has to be paid to have.
  • Through intranet self-service it falls to a few cents, at which point an employee filing about fifteen transactions a year stops being an administrative cost at all.
  • Errors fall as well, because templates are managed centrally so nobody submits last year's version, and entries are checked as they are typed rather than after filing.

Section 5–2a

The web that answers back

Everything in the first half of this chapter is a firm talking to itself. This half is about what happened when the web stopped being a broadcast, and the people a company used to talk at started producing the content about it.

One change, and everything downstream of it

Until the early 2000s the web was treated as a one-way medium. Some organizations made content; everybody else consumed it. The chapter calls that arrangement Web 1.0, and it holds a strict line between the two roles.

Web 2.0 names the dynamic applications that broke the line. They let people collaborate and share online, which turns a user from a consumer of content into a creator of it. The chapter's own comparison is the sharpest one available: a television network's site carries what the network made, while a video-sharing site carries user-generated content and would be empty without it.

The same contrast holds for reference material, and businesses adopted the pattern almost immediately once they saw what it bought them.

  • Reference works changed hands: a traditional encyclopaedia commissions professionally researched articles, while an online one is written and edited by its own readers, and the chapter notes the older publisher stopped printing in 2012.
  • Retailers took the cheapest version of the deal, adding customer reviews to product pages and gaining a channel for customers, better decisions for the next shopper, and more traffic, without writing any of it.
  • Platform businesses took the whole premise, matching people who have something with people who want it, which leaves the platform owning no inventory and producing no content.

Social media is the part of Web 2.0 that matters here

Applications that embody the collaborative and sharing values of Web 2.0 are classed as social media, or social software: they let people communicate, interact and collaborate. That is the whole category, and it is broader than the handful of large sites people picture.

What follows from that is societal rather than technical, and the chapter names three shifts.

  • How people look for information changed: a network of acquaintances is now consulted where a reference work used to be, which is a change in whose judgement is trusted.
  • What people treat as private shifted markedly, and the chapter is unsentimental about the consequences of publishing without considering who eventually reads it.
  • Every conversation now generates data, which the platforms use and which the organizations operating on those platforms increasingly use as well.

The workplace inherited the expectations

The people who grew up on these tools now work. The chapter reports a set of changed expectations that has more to do with employment than with software.

  • A portfolio career instead of a single long job, which changes how long a firm has to capture what an employee knows before they take it somewhere else.
  • Loaning talent rather than being a resource, a shift in self-description that shows up in what people expect to be consulted about.
  • Working across the old boundaries, so that a firm collaborates with customers and partners with rivals rather than treating industry lines as walls.

The managerial consequence is concrete. A firm that blocks social tools at work will find that people route around the block or take a job somewhere that does not have one. Whether that is fair is a separate question from whether it is true.

What is still a forecast

Two ideas in this chapter have not arrived, and it is worth being precise about which parts have.

The semantic web is a set of design principles that would let computers index pages by meaning rather than by keywords, so a search for what eats penguins returns an answer rather than pages containing three words.

Search engines ask webmasters to add structured markup for exactly this reason, and one large engine's knowledge panels are a visible result. The full vision remains largely unrealized, and may stay that way on grounds of complexity, feasibility and privacy.

Web 3.0 is contested even as a term. Some frame it around mobility, some around context — content filtered by time, location and activity — and some around artificial intelligence extending what the web can do. Treat it as a set of competing forecasts rather than a description of anything.

The shift that matters is not technical. When the audience became the authors, a firm lost its monopoly on what is said about it, and gained a source of labour, content and judgement it does not employ. Every remaining section of this chapter is a consequence of that one trade.

Section 5–2b

Communicating and cooperating in public

The chapter sorts social media by the job it does: communicate, cooperate, collaborate, connect. This section takes the first two. Communication is a firm putting something out and hearing back. Cooperation is the arrangement where one person's contribution improves everybody else's outcome, including the contributor's.

Three ways a firm talks in public

Three tools carry almost all of it, and they differ in length, in audience and in how long the exchange stays open.

  • A blog is an online diary of dated entries, long enough to explain something, published on a site the author controls; firms use one because it reads as more approachable than a press release.
  • Microblogging broadcasts short status updates in near real time to anyone who chooses to follow, with topics marked by a hashtag and a topic tagged faster than others said to be trending.
  • Instant messaging is real-time written conversation, and it has absorbed internal team channels, customer-facing live chat, and automated assistants that gather details before passing somebody to a human.

The chapter takes one criticism of blogging seriously: publishing without editorial process brings news out fast and produces what one critic calls the amateurization of journalism, with unverified sources and visible bias. Answering customers on a microblog has the mirror-image cost, since the channel that signals attentiveness hands critics the same megaphone.

Blogs · Long-form, chronological, owned

  • What it does: Publishes dated entries at whatever length the subject needs, on a site the author controls, usually with comments or a linked discussion.
  • What a firm gets from it: A voice that reads as a person rather than as a communications department, and a place to explain a decision at length before anybody else frames it.
  • What it does not solve: It does not reach anybody by itself. A blog with no audience is a filing cabinet, and building the audience is a separate and much harder job.
  • Where it goes wrong: Speed at the expense of verification. The chapter's charge of amateurization is that corners get cut to publish first, and a company blog carries the firm's name when they are.

Microblogging · Short updates, public by default

  • What it does: Broadcasts brief status updates in near real time to anyone who has chosen to follow, with topics marked by hashtags and repostable by recipients.
  • What a firm gets from it: A public channel for answering customers where other customers can see it happening, which is a signal about the firm as much as an answer to one person.
  • What it does not solve: It cannot hold an explanation. Anything requiring context has to live somewhere else, with the post serving only as a pointer to it.
  • Where it goes wrong: The channel belongs to everybody. A hashtag a firm creates can be taken over by its critics within hours, and the chapter's example of exactly that involved a police department.

Instant messaging · Real-time written conversation

  • What it does: Carries synchronous written exchanges between two people or a group, now including team platforms organised into channels and customer-facing live chat.
  • What a firm gets from it: Immediate feedback without booking anybody's time, and a customer service channel that costs far less per conversation than a telephone line.
  • What it does not solve: It produces no durable record anybody will find later. Decisions made in a channel are lost to the next person unless somebody writes them somewhere else.
  • Where it goes wrong: Everything becomes urgent. A medium built for immediate answers tends to be used for questions that did not need one, which is the interruption cost distributed teams complain about.

Media sharing and webcasting · Host once, consumed anywhere

  • What it does: Publishes images, video, audio or presentations on a hosting service, where others can view them in place or embed them in their own pages.
  • What a firm gets from it: Reach the firm did not pay for. An embedded video improves somebody else's page and gains an audience in the same movement, which is cooperation working as advertised.
  • What it does not solve: It does not give the firm control of the surrounding context. Content is consumed next to whatever the embedding site put there, including a competitor.
  • Where it goes wrong: Live video in particular. Content appears faster than anyone can review it, and the chapter is clear that continuously monitoring a live broadcast is close to impossible.

Tagging and geotagging · Metadata added by hand and by machine

  • What it does: Attaches descriptive keywords, and sometimes coordinates, to content so it can be categorised, searched and placed on a map.
  • What a firm gets from it: Findability. Photographs, videos and documents are almost unsearchable without it, and a tag cloud shows the shape of a collection without anyone reading it.
  • What it does not solve: It does not make tags consistent. Ten people tag the same thing ten ways, which is why an untended tag set degrades rather than improves.
  • Where it goes wrong: Location metadata attached without anybody deciding to attach it. The chapter's case is a photograph that revealed where a man in hiding was, because nobody thought to strip it.

Social bookmarking and cataloging · Shared collections, shared categories

  • What it does: Lets people save and categorise links or items publicly, building shared categorisation systems that grow more useful as more people contribute.
  • What a firm gets from it: Inside a firm, a map of who knows what. The chapter's point is that shared bookmarks expose the islands of expertise an organization chart does not show.
  • What it does not solve: It does not evaluate anything. A heavily bookmarked page is popular rather than correct, and the two are easy to confuse when you are in a hurry.
  • Where it goes wrong: It depends entirely on participation. Below a certain number of contributors a shared collection is worse than one person's own bookmarks, and nobody returns to it.

Cooperation, and why it is not collaboration

Cooperation creates a win-win: one participant's success improves the chances of the others. Nobody has to work on the same thing at the same time, which is what distinguishes it from collaboration.

Media sharing is the clearest instance of it working.

  • An embedded video hosted on a sharing site makes somebody else's article better and brings the video an audience, and neither party had to negotiate anything at all.
  • Webcasts extend the same logic to on-demand audio and video, which organizations use for shareholder meetings, training, road shows and recorded lectures.
  • Subscription feeds, such as RSS, close the loop by notifying readers of new content, so nobody has to remember to check forty sources by hand.

Live video is the newest form and the hardest to govern. Broadcasting as it happens lets a firm answer questions in the moment, and it also removes every check between something occurring and an audience seeing it.

The chapter is blunt that continuously monitoring live broadcasts is close to impossible for the platforms themselves, and it cites a murder streamed live in 2016 to make the point. A firm going live is accepting the same asymmetry at a smaller scale.

Metadata is the quiet part

Metadata is data about data: who, where, when, why. Some is captured automatically — a document's author and save time, a photograph's date, shutter speed and coordinates — and some has to be added by a person.

The added kind is a tag, and tags are what make user-generated content findable at all, because photographs and videos categorise themselves no better than they title themselves. A tag cloud displays them with size standing in for frequency, so the shape of a collection is visible at a glance.

Geospatial metadata is called a geotag, and it is where this gets serious. Coordinates attached to a photograph put it on a map, which is how a mapping service can show pictures, reviews and landmarks it never produced.

It is also how a photograph of a man in hiding, posted by a journalist who forgot to strip the location, led police to the place it was taken.

Sharing what you found, and what you own

Two more cooperative forms round out the section, and both work on the same principle: individually useful, collectively far more useful.

  • Social bookmarking lets people share the pages they found worth keeping, and the categories they build together are called a folksonomy, so each additional participant makes the shared set more complete for everybody.
  • Social cataloging does the same for collections rather than links — books, music, academic citations — which is why researchers use shared citation tools to assemble reference lists they would otherwise each build alone.

Both have a use inside a firm that the chapter is specific about. Bookmarking maps the islands of knowledge in an organization, which makes it possible to find the person who already knows something. Cataloging structures the volume of information a firm holds about suppliers, complaints and products so it can be reached later.

Section 5–2c

Collaborating at scale

The last two jobs on the chapter's list are the ones with the largest strategic consequences. Collaboration is several people working the same problem at once. Connection is what happens when a firm can reach a crowd, and the crowd can reach back.

The cloud made collaboration ordinary

Sharing a document used to mean emailing it and hoping. Cloud-based collaboration tools removed the round trip: a file lives in one place, syncs to every device, and several people work the current version rather than their own copy. Cloud productivity suites go further and put the software itself in the browser.

That sounds like an unmixed good, and the chapter is careful to price both sides. The benefits and the risks fall in four places, and a firm rarely notices a risk until the day it arrives.

One of the four deserves saying plainly before you sort them. A cloud tool needs a live connection, so the work stops when the connection does, and the firm has traded a problem it could fix for one it can only wait out.

Work done by people a firm does not employ

Collective intelligence is the idea that a distributed group holding divergent information and expertise can outperform individual experts. The chapter takes it from a book on the wisdom of crowds, and the word doing the work in that definition is divergent: a group that all knows the same thing is just one expert repeated.

Peer production is what collective intelligence looks like when it makes something: goods or services created by self-organizing communities, where the outcome depends on incremental contributions and anyone can improve it.

Firms reach for this deliberately as well. Open innovation is the practice of pulling external stakeholders into a firm's own research and development, forming ad hoc networks that reach past the people on its payroll.

Three arrangements in the chapter run on that principle, and they are not interchangeable.

  • Open source software is built and maintained by volunteers worldwide, and firms participate to influence its direction, to audit code they depend on, and to build goodwill in a community they draw from.
  • Wikis let anyone create, edit or delete content, with every prior version retained so vandalism can be reverted and disputes can be argued in the open rather than in private.
  • Crowdsourcing distributes small, self-contained pieces of work called human intelligence tasks — tagging an image, fixing a title, transcribing audio — to whoever will take them, usually for a few cents each.

Compare all three side by side, because the differences decide which one a firm can actually use for a given problem.

Section 5–2d

Connecting at scale

The last of the chapter's four jobs is connection, and it is the one with the largest strategic consequences. Collaboration changes what a firm's own people can do together. Connection changes who counts as the firm's people at all, and it introduces the single mechanism that decides who wins these markets.

Networks, professional and internal

Social networking builds social online communities where people with a broad and diverse set of interests meet, communicate and collaborate. Professional networks do the same for careers, letting people post a profile, ask for expert advice and be approached about work.

Enterprise social networks bring the format inside a company, and the chapter names why they look so familiar. Consumerization of information technology is the pattern where a product designed for the consumer market arrives at work, because that is what people already know how to use.

The three kinds are worth keeping apart, because a firm's reason for being on each one differs.

  • A general-interest network is where a firm meets its customers and the wider public, gets feedback on what it sells, and maintains a brand image it does not fully control.
  • A professional network is a labour market: profiles, expert advice and approaches about work, which makes it a recruiting channel before it is anything else.
  • An enterprise network is private to one organization, and it exists to connect employees to each other and to what colleagues already know.

The effect that decides everything

What makes these markets behave strangely is the network effect. Each additional member makes the service more valuable to every existing member, so growth feeds itself and a leader becomes very hard to dislodge.

The chapter's counter-example matters just as much. The network that was largest before the current one declined until it barely ranked, which is the same effect running in reverse: value falls with every departure, and the falling value causes the next one.

  • A firm's product is cheaper to make this year because component prices fell — A cost movement in a supply market. It changes the firm's economics and has nothing to do with how many people use anything.

Making something spread on purpose

Viral marketing uses the same mechanism to spread brand awareness, by making content people choose to pass on. The term was coined to describe marketing driven by word of mouth, in the way a real virus moves through the contacts somebody already has.

The chapter's principles for it are unglamorous, and each one has a matching way of being broken.

  • Do something unexpected, because a viewer who can predict the rest of the piece from its first seconds has no reason to show it to anybody.
  • Make people feel something, since a purely informative piece is passed on by nobody, however accurate and however well made it is.
  • Make sequels, because an audience assembled once and never given anything else is an audience the firm has spent and thrown away.
  • Allow sharing and easy distribution, and never restrict access, because a registration wall stops the mechanism at the first hand-off and leaves nothing to spread.

The chapter's own founding example is an email service that added a single line advertising itself to the bottom of every message its users sent, and bought twelve million subscribers for a fraction of what advertising would have cost.

What connection costs

Two consequences are worth naming before the next section, because both land on people rather than on firms.

  • Feeds are assembled by algorithms predicting what will hold attention, which quietly filters out material conflicting with a user's existing views and produces what critics call a filter bubble.

Section 5–3a

Making it work inside a company

Every tool in the last three sections is available, cheap and proven somewhere. Most internal deployments of them fail anyway. This section is about why, and it is the part of the chapter that separates people who can name a technology from people who can introduce one.

The tool is necessary and nowhere near sufficient

The chapter's position is blunt: technology is a critical success factor, and it is not the only component. Change management decides the outcome. Providing the tools and hoping employees use them for the intended purpose is not a plan, and management has to make sure people know the tools exist, what they are for, and what the rules around them are.

Because these applications run on social interaction, information sharing and network effects, corporate culture matters more here than for almost any other class of system. A culture of knowledge sharing, trust and honest feedback makes them work. A culture without those three does not fail to adopt the tool; it adopts the tool and produces nothing.

What the web proves, and what it does not

The first trap the chapter names has a name of its own: the enterprise is not the web. Applications that succeeded publicly did so over many years and under conditions a firm does not have.

Two of those conditions are worth spelling out, because both are invisible from outside.

  • Public participation is voluntary, and people join because they want to, whereas nobody inside an organization can be made to contribute anything worth reading.
  • Seamless collaboration usually is not: good encyclopaedia articles rest on long arguments behind the scenes, and many open source projects let anyone suggest a change while letting only a few apply one.

Four factors that decide the outcome

The chapter lists a set of caveats to weigh before deploying. Four of them account for most real failures, and each one shows up as a recognisable complaint rather than as an announcement.

  • Culture is the first, because open communication does not sit comfortably inside rigid hierarchies built on control, and self-expression cannot be mandated into existence.
  • Critical mass is the second, because these tools depend on network effects and a small user base never reaches the point where participating repays the effort of participating.
  • Technological inertia is the third, because people do not switch tools until they see a real, tangible benefit, and familiarity is itself a benefit they already have.
  • Security is the fourth, because any application that increases sharing increases exposure, and a firm has to weigh collaboration against protecting intellectual property and meeting its reporting obligations.

The other four, which arrive later

The remaining factors tend to appear after a pilot has gone well, which is exactly what makes them dangerous.

  • Organizational context means the work should choose the tool: ask what objective you are trying to meet before deciding what to install, rather than setting up a wiki and hoping.
  • Organizational hierarchies matter because a pilot runs inside one department, and an organization-wide deployment needs changes to culture, process and often the hierarchy itself.
  • The generation gap is a tendency rather than a rule: a workforce accustomed to interactive online environments raises the odds of adoption, and senior members may not see the point at all.
  • Technological integration decides whether the tool joins the firm's existing systems or simply becomes one more place somebody has to remember to look.

Scale changes which of these bites hardest. A small firm can carry a weak culture on personal relationships and will struggle to reach critical mass at all, because there are not enough people for participation to repay itself. A large firm has the numbers and has the hierarchy, the inertia and the compliance obligations that come with them.

Section 5–3b

When it goes wrong, and how fast

Every capability in this chapter has an inverse. The reach that carries a campaign carries a complaint at the same speed, the reviews that help buyers choose can be manufactured, and the anonymity that protects a whistleblower protects somebody else entirely. This section is the inverse, and the response playbook that follows from it.

Two rules the chapter states outright

The first is that the internet never forgets: material that embarrassed a firm stays retrievable years later, and deleting the original rarely removes the copies. The second is that a firm has to watch continuously and react fast, because the alternative is finding out from a journalist.

Those two rules generate everything else in this section, including the parts that look like separate problems.

Reviews, posts, pages, and video

The chapter works through four failure points, and each one fails differently.

  • Reviews are consulted before buying and are not always what they seem: firms hire people to post favourable reviews or unfavourable ones about competitors, and analysis tools have found products where the large majority appear fabricated.
  • Microblogging fails in public and at speed. A police department invited photographs with its own hashtag and received pictures of alleged brutality instead.
  • Company pages are free to host and not free to run: somebody has to watch what appears, and decide where the line sits between content the firm dislikes and content it removes.
  • Video is where it becomes uncontrollable, because a complaint that is filmed rather than written travels to an audience no press office was built to answer.

Two of the chapter's examples set the scale. A tenant with a couple of dozen followers was sued over a post about mould, and the coverage of the lawsuit reached a national audience the post never would have.

Two employees posted a prank video from a restaurant kitchen that reached over a million people within days. The company decided not to respond, changed course after two days, opened a public channel and put its president on video, and a later survey found the response had largely restored trust.

The same visibility runs the other way through employment: employers check these networks before hiring, and people have been dismissed over posts they assumed a manager could not see.

1. The chief executive wants to say the firm did nothing wrong, since it never commissioned a review. Why is that a poor response?

  • C. It will not be believed, so a firmer denial would serve the firm better — Escalating a denial makes matters worse, and the exhibit is a public artefact that any journalist can reproduce. Firmness is not the variable in play.

  • B. Say nothing until the review analysis has been independently verified — Verification is prudent and takes longer than the story does. The chapter's case of a firm that waited two days before responding is the cautionary version of exactly this instinct.
  • C. Ask the marketplace to remove the flagged reviews before responding publicly — Removing the evidence before commenting reads as concealment whatever the intent, and the flagged reviews are already documented in a report the journalist has.

Two more inversions, further from the marketing department

The chapter carries two cases that are not about a brand's reputation at all, and both run on capabilities earlier sections presented as benefits.

  • Cheap production and free distribution serve any message. The chapter's account of extremist propaganda turns on that and nothing else: portable cameras and simple editing software make high-quality video cheap, and social platforms distribute it globally at no cost, including to recruit teenagers.
  • A contribution buys a promise, not a product. Crowdfunding raises small amounts from many people for projects conventional finance would refuse, and the chapter's examples include campaigns that raised millions and shipped to a fraction of their backers, one that never produced a prototype, and one under investigation.

Neither is a failure of the technology. Both are the reach and the low cost this chapter has been recommending, pointed somewhere nobody intended.

The playbook

The chapter gives four preparations, and their value is that all of them are done before anything happens.

  • Assemble a crisis team in advance, drawn from inside the firm and outside it, so that nobody is deciding who has authority to speak while the clock is running.
  • Name your worst social media nightmare, and work out what its early signals look like, including the search terms an opponent would use.
  • Monitor the environment continuously and stay connected and responsive, because a complaint you have not seen is one you are not answering.
  • Act fast, because the first day decides the shape of it, and a response arriving after the story has settled is answering a version of events somebody else wrote.

  1. Assemble a crisis team, from inside the firm and outside it — First, and on an ordinary day. Deciding who has authority to speak, and which lawyer is on the call, is not something to work out while a story is spreading.
  2. Name the worst thing that could plausibly be said about you — Second, because you cannot watch for signals until you know what you are watching for. The chapter asks firms to identify their own nightmare explicitly rather than generally.
  3. Work out the search terms an opponent would use, and watch them — Third, and it follows directly from the previous step. Your own brand name is the term you would think of; the useful ones are the terms somebody attacking you would choose.
  4. Monitor continuously, and stay connected and responsive — Fourth, and it is a standing activity rather than an event. A complaint nobody has seen is a complaint nobody is answering, and monitoring doubles as ordinary business intelligence.
  5. When something emerges, act inside the first day — Fifth. The chapter is specific that the first twenty-four hours count, because a story left unanswered settles into the version somebody else wrote.
  6. Answer on the channel where it happened, not only on your own — Sixth. A statement on a corporate site reaches people already looking for the firm; the audience that matters is the one on the platform the material spread across.
  7. Fix the underlying cause, and say specifically what you fixed — Last, and it is what the chapter's most successful example actually did: found those responsible, closed and cleaned the site, and described the change rather than promising to do better.

Firms do this in practice through social media monitoring. The chapter's example is a manufacturer that opened a listening centre tracking mentions of itself in about a dozen languages, which lets it answer a customer before a problem spreads and doubles as business intelligence.

A customer's video about your product is spreading on a video platform. It is accurate. Four decisions across the first day, at a hypothetical firm.

Decision 1. The video has around forty thousand views and is climbing. Your monitoring caught it about an hour ago. What is the first move?

  • Post a holding statement immediately, since the first day counts — Speed matters and a statement that says nothing spends the firm's one chance at a first impression. The chapter asks for a fast and appropriate response, and appropriate requires knowing what happened.
  • Convene the crisis team and establish what actually happened — the stronger call. Right. The team exists so this takes minutes rather than a morning, and a response issued before anybody has established the facts is the one that has to be retracted, which starts a second story.
  • Contact the platform and request the video be taken down — A takedown of accurate criticism is itself the story, and copies exist within hours. This is the move that converts a complaint about a product into a complaint about the company.
  • Wait for the view count to plateau before deciding anything — This is the decision the chapter's restaurant example actually made, and it cost two days. By the time the count plateaus the account has been written by other people.

Decision 2. The facts hold up: the customer is right, and the fault is yours. Where do you respond?

  • In a press release, which is the firm's formal channel for statements — A press release reaches journalists and reaches almost nobody watching the video. It also reads as a firm addressing a different audience about them rather than to them.
  • On the firm's own site, so the wording stays under the firm's control — Control of wording is real and control of audience is not. People who saw the video have no reason to visit your site, so the response is published where the problem is not.
  • Privately to the customer, offering to resolve it if the video comes down — A resolution conditional on silence is exactly the arrangement that becomes the next video. The private fix is worth doing, and attaching a condition to it is not.
  • On the platform the video is on, and to the customer directly — the stronger call. Right. Answering where the audience already is, and reaching the person themselves, is the pattern the chapter's successful example follows. Both parts matter, and neither substitutes for the other.

Decision 3. A colleague notices the customer has a small following, and suggests the firm can wait it out.

  • Point out that the audience is whoever shares it, not who follows them — the stronger call. Right, and the chapter has the case for it: a tenant with a couple of dozen followers reached a national audience once the dispute itself became the story. Follower counts predict very little about reach.
  • Agree, and reduce the response to a single reply on the video — A minimal response is defensible on its merits and not for this reason. Sizing the reply to the poster's following is the reasoning error, whatever reply you end up with.
  • Agree, but ask monitoring to re-check the numbers every hour — Watching more closely while doing nothing is still doing nothing, and it hands the initiative to whoever picks the story up next.
  • Disagree, and escalate the response because the firm cannot risk it — Over-responding has its own costs: it draws attention the story had not yet earned, and it signals that pressure works, which shapes what arrives next.

Decision 4. The immediate response has gone out and the coverage is easing. What closes this properly?

  • Publish an apology from the chief executive and consider the matter closed — An apology without a described change invites the same complaint again, and the second occurrence is materially worse because the record of the first is permanent.
  • Increase advertising for a period to move attention onto other messages — Buying attention against an unresolved complaint is expensive and reads as exactly what it is. The chapter's rule that the internet never forgets means the material stays retrievable regardless.
  • Fix the cause, then say specifically what changed and how it was checked — the stronger call. Right. The chapter's example named what had been found, what was closed and cleaned, and what would change in hiring. Specificity is what a survey later found had restored trust, not the apology.
  • Add a review process so no employee can post about the firm publicly — This answers a problem the firm did not have, since the video came from a customer. It also imposes a control that people route around, which the chapter warns about directly.

What anonymity does, and what algorithms do

Anonymity online has legitimate uses and a well-documented cost. People who cannot be identified are more likely to enter deindividuation, a loss of self-awareness associated with antisocial behaviour.

An internet troll posts inflammatory content deliberately to provoke, and much of that behaviour is cyberharassment: false accusation, defamation, threats, identity theft and doxing, which is publishing somebody's private details. The chapter's account of one coordinated campaign against several women in the video game industry established a template that is still followed.

The other societal shift is quieter. Editorial judgement about what a public sees has largely been replaced by algorithms predicting what will hold attention, which filters out material that conflicts with a user's existing views. The chapter's balance is worth keeping: the same democratisation lowered the cost of being heard for candidates and causes that could not previously afford it.

A firm cannot prevent any of this. It can decide in advance who speaks, what it is watching for, and how fast it will answer — and those three decisions, made on an ordinary day, are the whole of what separates a bad week from a lasting one.

You now know what every tool in this chapter does. That is worth almost nothing on its own. A manager who can list collaboration technologies and a manager who can say which competitive pressure each one relieves are doing different jobs, and only the second one is doing analysis.

What this section is. It is an application supplement rather than a textbook objective. It teaches no new framework and assumes you have already met the five competitive forces and the value chain in the strategy chapter. Every firm in it is hypothetical, and it solves no graded case for you.

The two frameworks, in one list each

The five forces describe the pressure on a firm's profitability from outside it. A force is not good or bad; it is high or low, and the analyst's job is to say which and on what evidence.

  • Rivalry among existing competitors is pressure from the firms already selling what you sell, into the market you are already in.
  • Threat of new entrants is how easily somebody who is not yet competing could start, which is a question about barriers rather than about anybody in particular.
  • Threat of substitutes is a customer meeting the same need another way entirely, without buying from anybody in your industry.
  • Bargaining power of buyers is your customers' leverage over your price and your terms, including how cheaply they can leave.
  • Bargaining power of suppliers is the leverage held by whoever supplies your inputs, and access to customers is an input like any other.

The value chain answers the other half of the question: not what is pressing on the firm, but where inside it value is actually created. Five primary activities carry the product, and four support activities carry the firm.

  • Inbound logistics, operations and outbound logistics are the physical spine: getting inputs in, turning them into something, and getting the result to the customer.
  • Marketing and sales, and service are the customer-facing pair, covering everything that persuades somebody to buy and everything that happens once they have.
  • Firm infrastructure is the shared capability every other activity draws on, including planning, finance, legal and the systems that hold the firm together.
  • Human resource management, technology development and procurement are the three remaining supports: finding and keeping people, improving how the firm makes things, and buying what it needs.

A recommendation that does not land in one of those nine is a recommendation nobody can act on.

Both are worth practising outside this module too. A companion page, Five Forces and the Value Chain — a working review, turns them into a rater, an evidence sort, an investment simulator and three case studies.

Bargaining power of suppliers — How much leverage do the people you depend on have over you?

What it is (Leverage held by your inputs) → What moves it here (Platforms, and the labour market) → Evidence to look for (Share of business through one channel) → What a firm can do (Own a relationship you can keep)

  • The platform that carries your customers is a supplier, and an unusually powerful one. It lends you its audience, keeps the relationship, sets the commission, and can change either without negotiating.
  • Crowdsourcing and open source cut the other way by reducing what a specialist supplier can charge. A labelling agency, a stock photography library or a proprietary software vendor each faces an alternative that did not exist.
  • The evidence is the share of revenue arriving through any single channel, what that channel keeps, whether the firm holds the customer's contact details, and what the switching cost would be if terms changed tomorrow.

Threat of substitutes — What else could your customer do instead of buying from anybody in your industry?

What it is (A different way to meet the need) → What moves it here (Peer production and free tools) → Evidence to look for (Customers meeting the need elsewhere) → What a firm can do (Sell what the substitute cannot)

  • This is the force people most often misidentify. A cheaper rival is rivalry; a customer meeting the same need by another route entirely is a substitute, and peer production is very good at creating those.
  • A community that answers each other's questions substitutes for paid support. A free encyclopaedia substituted for a purchased one. A crowd that labels images substitutes for a service firm that employed people to do it.
  • The evidence is where the need is being met now: forum threads answering what your support desk used to, or a free tool covering the work a purchase used to cover, counted rather than asserted.

Rivalry among existing competitors — How hard are the firms already in your industry competing, and on what?

What it is (Pressure from firms like yours) → What moves it here (Speed, visibility, and response) → Evidence to look for (How fast rivals answer, and where) → What a firm can do (Change the basis, not just the pace)

  • Collaboration tools raise rivalry by compressing cycles. When a competitor can convene the right people in an hour and ship a change in a week, matching them is now part of competing rather than an ambition.
  • Visibility is the other half. Every rival's response time, tone and failures are public, so customers compare service on evidence they can read rather than on reputation.
  • The evidence is a comparison anybody can gather in an afternoon: how long each competitor takes to answer a public complaint, on which channels, and what a customer sees when they look for each firm by name.

The move that matters is the last one in each chain, and it is where most analyses stop too early. Naming a force is a diagnosis. Naming the evidence is what makes the diagnosis contestable, which is the only reason anybody should believe it.

Where does the work actually land?

A force tells you what is pressing on the firm. It does not tell you where to do anything about it. That is the value chain's job, and this chapter's technologies land in a surprisingly narrow part of it.

  • Marketing and sales and service take most of the customer-facing tools, because reviews, public replies, viral campaigns and listening centres all act on somebody who is buying or has bought.
  • Technology development takes the internal knowledge tools, because a wiki, a shared design workspace and an engineering forum all change how fast the firm can produce the next thing.
  • Human resource management takes the portal, the self-service form and the professional network, because all three change what employing somebody costs and how the firm finds them.
  • Firm infrastructure takes the intranet, enterprise search and the retention rules, because those are the shared capabilities every other activity draws on rather than work anybody does.

  • B. Marketing and sales only, since it is what thirty-eight percent of buyers cite — The acquisition effect is real and it is the smaller half of what the exhibit shows. Eleven thousand answered questions is service work by any definition.
  • C. Service and marketing and sales, because it both resolves and persuades — correct. The exhibit deliberately holds two things: support work the firm did not pay for, and acquisition it did not pay for. An honest analysis names both, and the assessment asks for at least two areas for this reason.
  • D. Firm infrastructure, since it is a shared capability the whole firm draws on — Infrastructure is capability every activity draws on, such as the intranet or the governance rules. The forum does specific customer-facing work, so it belongs with the activities that do it.

3. The owners can fund one initiative. Which recommendation follows from the analysis rather than from the panic?

  • A. Cut the kit price to match the competitor, and absorb the margin loss — Price is the dimension the competitor chose because it wins there. Meeting it spends the firm's margin on a fight it is structurally placed to lose, and it does nothing to the asset the analysis identified.
  • B. Invest in the forum's participation, since seventeen percent carries everything — correct. Every number in the exhibit rests on the seventeen percent who post, which makes participation the single point of failure and the only input that compounds. It answers the force the analysis named rather than the competitor's move.
  • C. Add a support telephone line, so the firm matches the competitor's offer — A telephone line copies the competitor's weaker substitute for what the firm already has, adds fixed cost, and would reduce forum participation by removing the reason to post.
  • D. Advertise the community heavily, so more buyers hear about it before choosing — Promotion converts an existing asset into more sales and does nothing to strengthen it. It is also the recommendation most likely to be chosen because it feels active, which is why it is worth naming as declined.

What this case was really about. Three moves, in order, and none of them optional. The observation is a cheaper competitor. The force is not rivalry, which is where instinct goes, but the threat of new entrants, because what protects this firm is a network-effect asset a challenger cannot buy. The location is two value-chain activities rather than one, since the exhibit measures both support absorbed and buyers acquired. And the recommendation follows from the force: strengthen the thing that compounds, rather than matching a price on a dimension chosen by somebody with more scale. Notice that the analysis also names what it declined and why, which is an analytical move rather than a hedge.

One warning before the next section. Every claim in this supplement is an argument rather than a fact about the chapter.

That a wiki sits in technology development, or that a network effect raises an entry barrier, is a reading you are being taught to construct and should be prepared to defend. The evidence line in each chain exists because a reading is only as good as what supports it.

A technology is not a strategy, and neither is a framework. The analysis is the join: this observation, therefore this force, therefore this place in the value chain, therefore this one thing to do. Everything in this module has been building the middle two steps, because they are the ones people skip.

1. A firm writes: our customers are very active on social media, so buyer power is high. What is wrong with it?

Application supplement 5–5

Turning this chapter into the written analysis

An observation is not a diagnosis. A firm can count forum threads, reply times and marketplace commission and still hold nothing anybody can act on, because a figure never says which pressure produced it. This supplement practises the move from counted evidence to one recommendation, in the order a written analysis has to make it.

What this section is. It is an application supplement rather than a textbook objective. It teaches no new framework, and it assumes you have worked through the previous section. Everything here runs on a hypothetical firm, and it solves no graded case for you.

The shape of the deliverable

The written analysis this course asks for is fixed, and knowing its shape in advance is half the work. Four things have to be in it, and each one fails in a predictable way.

  1. Rate all five forces on the firm. All five, including the ones that turn out to be weak, and each with the evidence that made you rate it that way.
  2. Find at least two places in the value chain where technology could create an advantage. Two genuinely different activities, not one activity described twice.
  3. Recommend exactly one initiative. One. With what it changes, what it costs, and what you would measure afterwards to know whether it worked.
  4. Write a comparison of 150 to 200 words against a company you choose. Not a profile of that company: a lesson drawn back to the firm you are advising.

The firm below is hypothetical and every figure attached to it is invented. Take the case first; the explanation after it reads as the debrief of what you have just tried.

1. A national competitor opened in the region and clients now choose from a reviewed directory. Which is the more significant force change, and why?

  • A. The competitor, since a national practice can undercut a regional one on price — A larger competitor is real pressure and is the familiar kind. Price is also not established anywhere in the exhibit, so treating it as the mechanism is an assumption rather than a finding.
  • B. The directory, since it moved the selection decision to before any contact happens — correct. The directory raises buyer power by moving information to the client's side: forty-one percent of enquiries now arrive after a comparison the practice was not present for. That is a structural change, and the competitor is one more entry on a page the client already reads.
  • C. Neither; both are rivalry, and the practice should treat them as one problem — Collapsing the two loses the distinction that makes the analysis useful. One is a firm competing with you; the other changed how every firm gets chosen, including the new one.
  • D. The competitor, since rivalry tends to dominate the other forces in professional services — Nothing establishes a dominant force in advance, and asserting one is the move this module warns against. The rating has to come from the evidence in front of you.

2. The four-and-a-half day answer time and the sixty-two re-derived decisions. Where do those land, and are they one problem or two?

  • B. It is also a service fix that changes nothing about the two largest structural risks, and the brief asks for the one initiative rather than the easiest.
  • B. Capture detailing decisions as they are made, before nine seniors retire — correct. It answers the sixty-two re-derived decisions and the nine retirements at once, it is the only option addressing something the practice cannot recover once it is gone, and it lands in technology development where the firm's ability to produce the next project is decided.
  • C. Open a fifth office in the competitor's city, to defend the region directly — A fifth office is a large capital commitment answering rivalry with scale, which is the dimension a national practice is better placed to win. Nothing in the exhibit supports it.

A national competitor is visible and rivalrous; the directory quietly moved selection to before the practice is ever contacted, which is buyer power and the larger change. The two internal figures are deliberately separable, because the deliverable asks for at least two value-chain areas and they are genuinely different: reaching a person, and reaching a record. And the recommendation follows from what is irreversible. Review response time can be fixed next month and any time after that; nine retirements cannot be undone, which is what makes knowledge capture the one initiative this evidence supports.

What this chapter supplies to the frameworks

Each decision asked for the same two moves: name the pressure an observation belongs to, then say where in the firm a fix would land. A symptom is not a force. Five ideas from this chapter make that translation for you.

  • Network effects and accumulated participation raise a barrier to entry that a challenger cannot buy, which is why a community, a review corpus or a knowledge base is a strategic asset rather than a marketing channel.
  • A platform that carries your customers is a supplier whose power grows with every order it handles, because it lends you its audience and keeps the relationship attached to itself.
  • Reviews and public complaint move information to the buyer, so buyer power rises even when nothing about the product or the price has changed at all.
  • Internal knowledge tools sit in technology development, because what they change is how quickly and how well the firm can produce the next thing rather than how it sells the last one.
  • Coordination cost is a value-chain cost, so nine days of waiting for an answer is an operations and technology development problem with a number attached, not a complaint about meetings.

1. Column F: what share of each channel's enquiries were resolved by other customers rather than by staff? Give it as a percentage, rounded to one decimal place.

=ROUND(C2/B2*100,1)

One channel resolves most of its own volume and the other three resolve none. That single ratio is the whole argument for treating a community as an asset rather than as a channel, and it is the number a competitor without one cannot produce.

2. Column G: how many staff hours did each channel actually consume? Multiply the staff resolutions by the minutes each one took, and convert to hours.

=ROUND(D2*E2/60,1)

Now the two columns disagree, and the disagreement is the finding. The forum takes by far the most enquiries and consumes the fewest staff hours of any channel here, while the page the firm controls least consumes the most.

3. Column H: mark any channel that consumes more than eight hundred staff hours for review, and mark the rest as held. Column G holds your own answer rather than sheet data, so work the hours out again inside this formula.

=IF(D2*E2/60>800,"Review","Hold")

A flag is a decision rule written down, which is the honest way to use a threshold. Notice what it selects: three channels for review, and the one that carries the most enquiries is not among them, which is the opposite of what enquiry volume alone would have told you.

A resolution rate and a staff-hour total tell you where the effort is going. They do not tell you which channel makes customers wait, which leaves work unfinished, or which one the firm does not control. Those are questions for the records themselves.

1. List the topic and the hours to first reply for every post on the forum.

SELECT topic, hours_to_reply FROM posts WHERE channel = 'forum'

Choosing columns and choosing rows are separate decisions. Read the result and the pattern is already there: forum replies arrive in under two hours, and three of the four consumed no staff time at all.

2. How many staff minutes did each channel consume this week? Show the largest first.

SELECT channel, SUM(staff_minutes) AS minutes FROM posts GROUP BY channel ORDER BY minutes DESC

Grouping turns a list of posts into a description of the channels. The channel with the most posts is not the one consuming the most staff time, which is the first sign that post counts are a poor way to judge a channel.

3. Which channels left more than one post unresolved? Show the channel and how many.

SELECT channel, COUNT(*) AS unresolved FROM posts WHERE resolved = 'no' GROUP BY channel HAVING COUNT(*) > 1

WHERE filters rows and HAVING filters groups, because at the WHERE stage nothing has been counted yet. The answer also says where unfinished work accumulates, which is on a channel somebody else owns.

4. What did each channel cost the practice in staff time? Show the channel, the minutes, and the cost, most expensive first.

SELECT p.channel, SUM(p.staff_minutes) AS minutes, SUM(p.staff_minutes * c.staff_rate / 60) AS staff_cost FROM posts p JOIN channels c ON p.channel = c.channel GROUP BY p.channel ORDER BY staff_cost DESC

This answer exists in neither table. Minutes sit on one and the rate sits on the other, and only the join shows that the most expensive channel is also the slowest to reply and the one where the practice does not own the relationship. You have the practice's records and a week before the partners expect a document. Four decisions, in the order they arrive.

Decision 1. You have a week with the practice's records before you write anything. What do you come back with?

  • A written summary of what the four office heads believe is going wrong — Their judgement is worth having and will help you read the numbers later. On its own a list of beliefs cannot be checked, and it tends to describe the month rather than locate a pressure.
  • A review of the national competitor's website and brochures, noting every service the practice lacks — This produces a feature list, and a feature list quietly becomes the recommendation. You end up proposing work because a rival advertises it rather than because a force demands it.
  • Every project file exported, so nothing at all is left out of the analysis — Completeness is not evidence. Without a question in mind you cannot tell which figures matter, and the work stalls in the data rather than in the argument you were asked for.
  • Reply times by channel, re-derived decisions, directory share, and the retirement profile — the stronger call. Right. Each of these can be counted, each attaches to a force or to a value-chain activity, and each can be checked by somebody who doubts you. Evidence is what separates an analysis from an opinion.

Decision 2. Your first draft says: cross-office questions take four and a half days, and clients now choose from a directory. What is missing?

  • Nothing. It states two observed facts, and an analysis should stay close to its evidence — Staying close to the evidence is right, and an analysis is more than the evidence. A description that names no pressure gives the reader nowhere to go and no way to test whether you are correct.
  • It names no force. Say which pressure each observation is evidence of — the stronger call. Right. As drafted the sentence describes the year. Naming buyer power and placing the coordination cost inside the value chain is what lets a reader argue with you and what makes a recommendation follow from something.
  • A stronger opening claim, such as calling this an existential threat to the practice — Force of language is not force in the framework sense. Escalating the wording adds urgency without adding a diagnosis, and it makes the whole piece harder to trust.
  • More figures, so add the re-derived decisions and the retirement count to that sentence — More numbers in the same sentence still leave it a description. The figures belong in the evidence; the sentence exists to say which pressure they are evidence of.

Decision 3. You have three candidate initiatives you like, and the brief asks for one. What do you write?

  • All three, ranked in order, so the partners can pick whichever suits the budget — Ranking looks generous and quietly hands the decision back to the reader. It also hides which pressure you were answering, and no single option ever gets costed properly.
  • The most ambitious of the three, since a bolder call shows a firmer grasp — Ambition is not a criterion. The recommendation has to answer the pressure the evidence showed, and a bold initiative aimed at the wrong force is worse than a modest one aimed at the right one.
  • One initiative, with the force it answers, what it costs, and what you would watch — the stronger call. Right. A single recommendation can be costed, argued and later judged. The other two can be named in a sentence as deliberately not chosen, which is itself an analytical move rather than a hedge.
  • A new collaboration platform, because the national competitor is known to use one — This is technology with no force behind it. If you cannot say which pressure the platform relieves and where it sits in the value chain, the reader has no reason to accept it.

Decision 4. The last piece is a comparison of 150 to 200 words against a company you choose. Which draft does the work?

  • Name what that company does differently, then what the practice should do or refuse — the stronger call. Right. The comparison exists to produce a lesson for the firm you are advising. Naming the difference in the vocabulary of the frameworks is what makes it a lesson rather than a story about somebody else.
  • A short profile of the company, its history, its scale and its best-known work — This is the commonest failure of the whole piece. It reads well and says nothing about the practice, and by the end the reader knows more about a company nobody asked you to advise.
  • A list of every difference between the two firms, so nothing relevant is left out — Length is not coverage. Most differences do not bear on the pressure you diagnosed, and burying the one that matters among twenty that do not is how a reader misses your point.
  • An argument that the practice should copy the company as closely as its budget allows — Copying ignores what your own evidence said about this firm, which holds ground the comparison company may not hold at all. A lesson is not an instruction to imitate.

The comparison summary

The last piece is short and is where most of the marks are quietly lost. Three things make it work: pick a comparison with a real similarity or a real contrast, name what that company does differently in the vocabulary of the frameworks, and end on what your firm should therefore do or deliberately refuse to do.

A summary that spends its two hundred words admiring somebody else has answered a different question. Every sentence should be recoverable as advice to the firm in front of you.

Chapter vocabulary

Every term the chapter defines, written so it makes sense on its own, with an example beside it. Search by word, or filter by the objective the term belongs to. 5.1collaboration
Two or more people working together to achieve a common goal, inside an organization or across its boundaries.For example: Three engineers in different countries producing one specification between them, rather than three opinions about it.
5.1task force
A temporary work group with a finite task and a defined life cycle, formed to solve a problem that existing departments cannot.For example: A group pulled from four departments for six months to fix a recurring delivery failure, then disbanded.
5.1virtual team
A group whose members are drawn from different geographic areas and assembled as needed for a particular project, with membership that forms, grows, shrinks and disbands as the work requires.For example: Programmers in one country, project managers in a second and testers in a third, working as one team on one release.
5.1synchronous communication
Exchange that is coordinated in time, so the participants are present together and answers arrive immediately.For example: A telephone call, a live chat window, or a videoconference where somebody can interrupt to disagree.
5.1asynchronous communication
Exchange that is not coordinated in time, so a message waits until its recipient chooses to attend to it.For example: Email, a text message, or a discussion board thread that collects replies over three days.
5.1virtual meeting
A meeting held in an online environment rather than a room, which may run at the same time for everybody or spread across days.For example: A teleconference for the same-time version, and a moderated discussion board for the different-times version.
5.1collaboration software
Software that lets people work together, usually classified by whether it supports same-time or different-time work and whether the participants share a location.For example: A shared document editor, a videoconferencing tool, and a project board are three points in the same category.
5.1groupware
The older class of software from which collaboration tools evolved, built to let people work together more effectively, with the first mainstream product appearing in 1989.For example: A shared mail, calendar and discussion system installed on a company's own servers in the early 1990s.
5.1electronic communication tools
The family of tools that convey verbal and written information and move files, documents or other content between people.For example: Instant messaging, email, voice mail, blogs, wikis and ordinary websites all belong here.
5.1electronic conferencing tools
The family of tools that support information sharing and rich interaction, where the value comes from participants doing something together in the moment.For example: Screen sharing during a design review, or a videoconference where tone and interruption matter.
5.1collaboration management tools
The family of tools that facilitate meetings and manage a group's activity rather than carrying the conversation itself.For example: Shared calendars, knowledge management systems, intranets and online document systems.
5.1desktop videoconferencing
Video meetings run from an ordinary computer or mobile device, needing only a camera, a microphone, software and a fast connection.For example: Joining a client meeting from a laptop in a hotel room using software that costs nothing.
5.1webcam
A small video camera connected directly to a computer or built into a laptop screen, which is one of the four things desktop videoconferencing requires.For example: The camera above a laptop display that most people never think about until it is covered with tape.
5.1intranet
A private network using web technologies, used to move proprietary information securely inside one organization, with its pages behind the company firewall.For example: An internal site where a human resources employee sees payroll figures and a warehouse supervisor does not.
5.1virtual private network (VPN)
An encrypted connection carried across the public internet that lets a remote device use an organization's internal resources as though it were inside the building.For example: A salesperson opening the company benefits page from an airport, through a tunnel nothing in between can read.
5.1enterprise search
Search built to retrieve content from an organization's own documents, databases, applications and cloud storage, which is a different problem from searching linked public pages.For example: Finding the one specification a colleague wrote three years ago, in a language you do not read, on a server you did not know existed.
5.1social intranet
An internal site where every user can create and update content and connect to whoever wrote it, rather than only a small group holding editorial rights.For example: A workshop supervisor correcting a procedure page directly, instead of emailing the communications team about it.
5.1employee portal
An intranet-based entry point that gives each employee the tools and information their own job requires, including self-service for administrative tasks.For example: One page that shows a nurse their roster, their payslip and the forms they are entitled to file.
5.1employee self-service
The practice of letting employees file and manage their own administrative transactions rather than sending paper to a central team.For example: Changing a pension contribution through a form that checks the entry as it is typed, at a cost of a few cents rather than tens of dollars.
5.2Web 2.0
The dynamic web applications that let people collaborate and share content online, turning a user from a consumer of content into a creator of it.For example: A video site whose entire catalogue was uploaded by the people watching it.
5.2user-generated content
Material created and uploaded by a site's own visitors rather than by the organization running the site.For example: Product reviews, uploaded videos, and encyclopaedia articles written by the people who read them.
5.2social media
Applications embodying the collaborative and sharing values of the later web, which let people communicate, interact and collaborate; also called social software.For example: Blogs, wikis, media sharing, social networks and bookmarking services are all in the category.
5.2semantic web
A set of design principles intended to let computers index pages by meaning rather than by keywords, so a search returns an answer instead of pages containing the right words.For example: Marking up the parts of an address so a machine can tell a street from a postcode, rather than seeing one string of text.
5.2webmaster
The person responsible for creating and maintaining a site's pages, and the person search engines ask to add structured markup.For example: Whoever actually edits the page templates, which in a small firm is often somebody with another job title entirely.
5.2Web 3.0
The contested label for the next wave of internet technologies, framed variously around mobility, around context, or around artificial intelligence extending what the web can do.For example: Content filtered by where you are, what time it is and what you were doing an hour ago, rather than by what you typed.
5.2blog
An online diary made up of dated entries, published on a site the author controls, usually with comments or a linked discussion.For example: A company's official site where it explains a decision at length before anybody else frames it.
5.2blogging
The practice of keeping such a diary, which began as a way for people with no technical skill to publish and became a way for firms to sound like people.For example: A software company posting the reasoning behind a change, rather than a press release announcing it.
5.2amateurization of journalism
The criticism that publishing without editorial process trades accuracy for speed, producing unverified sources and visible bias alongside genuinely faster news.For example: A breaking story that reaches readers hours before a newsroom could confirm it, and is wrong in a way nobody catches for a week.
5.2microblogging
Broadcasting short status updates in near real time to anyone who has chosen to follow, in contrast with a social network where the author chooses the audience.For example: A company account answering a customer complaint in public, where every other customer can see the answer.
5.2hashtag
A word or phrase marked with a leading symbol to indicate a post's topic and relate it to other posts about the same thing.For example: A tag a firm creates for a campaign, which its critics can use just as easily as its customers.
5.2trending
The state of a word, phrase or topic being tagged at a greater rate than others, whether through a concerted effort or because something happened.For example: A topic climbing within minutes of a sporting result, then disappearing by the next morning.
5.2instant messaging
Real-time written conversation between two people or a group, with immediate feedback from the other participants; also called online chat.For example: A team platform organised into channels, or a live chat window on a shop's own website.
5.2cooperation
An arrangement creating a win-win, where one participant's success improves the chances of the others without anybody working on the same thing at the same time.For example: A video embedded in somebody else's article, which improves the article and finds the video an audience.
5.2webcast
Digital audio or video distributed for on-demand playback whenever the audience chooses; also called a podcast, despite having nothing to do with any particular device.For example: A recorded lecture a student plays on the way to work, or a shareholder meeting made available afterwards.
5.2RSS
A feed format publishers provide so that subscribers are notified of new content automatically, instead of checking several sources by hand.For example: A reader that collects new posts from forty sites and presents them in one list.
5.2metadata
Data about data, describing content in terms of who, where, when and why, some captured automatically and some added by a person.For example: A document's author and save time, or a photograph's date, shutter speed and coordinates.
5.2tag
A keyword added by a person to digital content so that it can be categorised and found later, as distinct from the metadata a device records by itself.For example: The names of the people in a photograph, which no camera can record and no search can find without.
5.2tag cloud
A display of tags in which each word's size represents its frequency or importance, so the shape of a collection is visible without reading it.For example: A view of a site's topics where the largest words show what it is mostly about.
5.2geotag
Geospatial metadata such as latitude, longitude or altitude attached to a photograph, video or post, allowing it to be placed on a map.For example: Coordinates a phone attaches to every picture, which is how a photograph revealed where a man in hiding was staying.
5.2social bookmarking
Sharing the pages people found worth keeping, and building shared categories for them, so the collection grows more useful as more people take part.For example: Inside a firm, a way of finding who already knows about a topic rather than which document mentions it.
5.2folksonomy
A categorisation system that emerges from the labels many users apply, rather than one imposed by a librarian or an administrator.For example: The set of topic labels a bookmarking community settles into over a few years, without anybody designing it.
5.2social cataloging
The building of shared catalogues of items rather than links, on specific topics such as books, music or academic citations.For example: A shared citation library researchers draw on to assemble reference lists instead of each building one alone.
5.2cloud-based collaboration tools
Services that store shared files and often the productivity software itself remotely, so several people work the current version from any device with a browser.For example: A document three people edit at once, which none of them has ever emailed to the others.
5.2content management system (CMS)
Software that lets people publish, edit, version and retrieve digital content without going through a technical department, with roles controlling who may do what.For example: A product manager correcting a description on the company website, without raising a request with anybody.
5.2learning management system (LMS)
Software that carries teaching material, administers assessment, and handles self-service tasks such as enrolment and viewing grades.For example: The system a course runs on, increasingly carrying discussion and group work alongside the content.
5.2collective intelligence
The idea that a distributed group holding divergent information and expertise can outperform the capabilities of individual experts.For example: An encyclopaedia article whose quality rests on hundreds of small corrections rather than on one commissioned author.
5.2peer production
The creation of goods or services by self-organizing communities, where the outcome depends on incremental contributions and anyone can improve it.For example: An open encyclopaedia and an open source project are the chapter's two examples of the same mechanism.
5.2open source software
Software created, maintained and updated by volunteers worldwide, whose source code anyone may read, use and propose changes to.For example: A firm contributing to a project it depends on, to influence its direction and to audit code it is trusting.
5.2wiki
A site allowing people to create, edit or delete content and to discuss changes, backed by a database that keeps every prior version so anything can be reverted.For example: An internal knowledge repository written by the engineers who know things rather than by whoever was assigned to document them.
5.2crowdsourcing
Distributing small, self-contained tasks to whoever will take them, usually through a marketplace and usually for a few cents each.For example: Tagging images, fixing product titles, or transcribing short audio clips, posted by a firm and completed by strangers.
5.2human intelligence task (HIT)
A small, self-contained piece of work that a person can do easily and a computer cannot, which is the unit crowdsourcing marketplaces trade in.For example: Deciding whether a photograph contains a receipt, which takes a person two seconds and a program a great deal of effort.
5.2open innovation
The practice of bringing external stakeholders into a firm's innovation process, forming research and development networks beyond its own staff.For example: A manufacturer running a public challenge for a materials problem its own engineers have not solved.
5.2social networking
Building online communities where individuals with diverse interests meet, communicate and collaborate, whether generally or around a profession.For example: A professional network where people post career profiles and are contacted about opportunities.
5.2social online community
The group that forms on such a site, held together by shared interest rather than by employment or geography.For example: A network built around checking in at places, or one built around a single profession.
5.2consumerization of information technology
The pattern where technologies designed first for consumers make their way into organizations, because that is what people already know how to use.For example: An internal social network deliberately built to resemble the consumer apps its users are accustomed to.
5.2network effect
The property whereby each additional participant makes a service more valuable to every existing participant, so growth reinforces itself.For example: A social network that became more attractive to join precisely because so many people had already joined.
5.2clickbait
An incomplete or tantalising headline written to be clicked rather than to describe accurately, because the revenue comes from the visit rather than the reading.For example: A headline promising a revelation that the article then never quite delivers.
5.2viral marketing
Using the network effect to increase brand awareness, by making content that entices viewers to pass it to other people.For example: An email service adding a short line about itself to every message its users sent, which brought in millions of subscribers.
5.2social-local-mobile
The intersection of social recommendation, location awareness and mobile devices, where a phone that knows where it is meets reviews written by other customers.For example: Opening an app to find a well-reviewed restaurant within walking distance, and being shown an advertisement from one of them.
5.3crowdfunding
Funding a project or venture by raising small amounts from a large number of people, usually online, for projects traditional finance would not support.For example: A product that raised millions from tens of thousands of backers and shipped to a fraction of them years later.
5.3critical mass
The level of participation a social application has to reach before taking part repays the effort of taking part.For example: A staff directory with four completed profiles, which nobody consults, so nobody completes a fifth.
5.3technological inertia
The reluctance to move to a new application until a real and tangible benefit is visible, with familiarity counting as a benefit already held.For example: A team that keeps using email for something the new platform does better, because email is already understood.
5.3deindividuation
A loss of self-awareness that people are more likely to enter when they cannot be identified, and which is associated with antisocial behaviour.For example: Behaviour in an anonymous forum that the same person would not sign their own name to.
5.3internet troll
Somebody who creates discord online by starting arguments or posting inflammatory content with the deliberate intent of provoking an emotional response.For example: An anonymous account that appears in a discussion only to make it unpleasant, and then moves on.
5.3cyberharassment
Use of the internet to stalk or harass a person, group or organization, covering false accusation, defamation, threats, vandalism and identity theft.For example: A coordinated campaign against several women in one industry, which established a template still followed.
5.3doxing
Researching and publishing somebody's personally identifiable information, which converts online hostility into a physical safety problem.For example: Posting a target's home address and workplace so that others can act on it.
5.3filter bubble
The result of feeds assembled by algorithms that predict what will hold attention, which filters out material conflicting with a user's existing views.For example: Two people searching the same thing on the same day and being shown two different pictures of what is happening.

1. 5.1 At a hypothetical instrument maker, Larchfield Controls, a calibration question needs a metallurgist in one country, a production lead in a second and a compliance officer in a third. Handled by email it takes eight days and about two hours of actual work. The operations director proposes a rule that internal email must be answered within four hours. What is wrong with that proposal?

  • A. It treats a turnaround problem as an effort problem rather than removing the delay — correct. Two hours of work spread across eight days is a mode problem: each round of clarification costs a full turnaround. A faster reply shortens each wait and leaves the number of rounds untouched.
  • B. It applies to all email rather than only to the messages that belong to a project — Scoping the rule more narrowly would make it more palatable and would not change what is producing the delay, which is the number of exchanges rather than which messages they are.
  • C. It cannot be enforced, because nobody can be required to read email at a particular time — Enforceability is a fair practical objection and a secondary one. Even a perfectly obeyed rule would leave a question needing four exchanges taking most of a day.
  • D. It will slow other work, because staff will interrupt what they are doing to answer messages — Interruption cost is real and is an argument about the price of the rule rather than about whether it addresses the cause of the delay.

2. 5.1 A hypothetical logistics firm, Pellworth Freight, buys videoconferencing systems for all five of its depots and reports a year later that collaboration has not improved. Most of the work its planners do involves handing over partly finished schedules between shifts and time zones. What best explains the disappointment?

  • A. The equipment was too expensive to be used casually, so staff booked it only for formal meetings — Formality does discourage casual use, and that would still leave the systems well matched to the meetings that were held. It does not explain why the work itself did not improve.
  • B. Five depots are too few for a network effect, so the systems went largely unused — Network effects govern tools whose value rises with participation. A meeting between two depots works perfectly well with two depots taking part.
  • C. The purchase serves scheduled same-time meetings, and most of that work is different-time work — correct. Handing partly finished work between shifts is the different-times mode. Equipment built for a same-time meeting between fixed locations does not touch it, however good the equipment is.
  • D. Videoconferencing is a conferencing tool, and planning needs collaboration management tools — The three families are not ranked, and a planning function draws on all of them. Naming the family is closer to the problem than the others and still misses the time dimension that explains it.

3. 5.1 A hypothetical hospital group is told by its ward managers that clinical staff will not use the proposed employee portal, because there is no machine available to them mid-shift. The project manager records this as resistance to change and proceeds. What has been misread?

  • A. The objection is about access rather than attitude, and it is the best information available — correct. The managers are reporting a physical condition of the work. Filing that under resistance discards a solvable design constraint and converts a fixable problem into a grievance later.
  • B. The objection is about training, which the project should budget for before proceeding — Training answers a capability gap. Nobody here has said staff cannot use a browser; they have said there is nothing to use one on.
  • C. The objection is about seniority, since ward managers speak for a group they do not represent — Whether managers speak for their staff is worth checking and does not change the content of what they said, which is a claim about equipment rather than about feelings.
  • D. The objection is about timing, and the portal should launch after the next roster change — A launch date does not create a machine on a ward. Delay postpones the same failure to a later month.

4. 5.1 A hypothetical manufacturer's intranet has grown to about ninety thousand documents. Employees increasingly stop searching it and ask a colleague instead. What does the chapter identify as the underlying difficulty?

  • A. Employees prefer human answers, so any search tool will be underused whatever its quality — There is a genuine preference for asking somebody, and the chapter treats the search failure as the cause rather than as an unavoidable human trait.
  • B. Internal content sits in systems with no linking signal for a search engine to rank by — correct. The chapter contrasts enterprise search with web search precisely here: internal content lives in documents, databases and applications rather than in pages that link to one another, so the signal a web engine ranks by does not exist.
  • C. Ninety thousand documents is beyond what an internal search index can practically hold — Index size is not a limit at this scale. Public engines index vastly more, and the difficulty the chapter names is about ranking rather than capacity.
  • D. The documents were never tagged, so a search engine has nothing to match a query against — Full-text search matches untagged documents perfectly well. Tags help, and their absence is not what makes the enterprise problem different in kind.

5. 5.1 A hypothetical charity wants its four regional offices to stop keeping separate copies of its safeguarding procedure, which currently exists in three versions. Which capability addresses the actual cause?

  • A. Enterprise search, so staff can find whichever version is nearest to hand — Finding a version faster is not the same as there being one version. Search over three copies returns three copies, which is the problem restated.
  • B. A virtual private network, so every office reaches the same server — A private network governs who can reach the network and from where. Four offices already connected can still keep four separate documents.
  • C. Real-time access to one authoritative document held in a single place — correct. The chapter's first intranet benefit exists for this: a paper or filed document has to be updated, reprinted and redistributed, so older copies keep circulating, and a single updated page has no older copies.
  • D. A social intranet, so any member of staff can correct the procedure directly — Open editing raises how much knowledge reaches the intranet and would let four people edit four copies just as easily. It does not by itself establish a single source.

6. 5.1 A hypothetical engineering consultancy assembles a project team from three offices. The project manager schedules a daily meeting at an hour that suits two of the three. Six weeks in, attendance from the third office has quietly stopped. What does this illustrate?

  • A. Technological inertia, since the third office prefers the tools it was already using — Inertia describes refusing to move to a new tool. Nobody here has refused anything; they attended for six weeks and then stopped.
  • B. A generation gap, since the third office has a longer-serving workforce — Length of service might correlate with anything and nothing here measures it. Attributing the drop-off to age is an assumption rather than a reading of the situation.
  • C. A critical mass problem, since three offices are too few to sustain a daily meeting — Critical mass governs tools whose value depends on how many people take part. A scheduled meeting between three offices does not need a threshold to be useful.
  • D. The uneven cost of synchronous work across time zones — correct. A same-time meeting across time zones has no hour convenient everywhere, so the cost falls on one group. Attendance decaying rather than being refused is exactly how that cost shows up.

7. 5.2 A hypothetical retailer, Ashmere Home Supply, adds customer reviews to its product pages and its returns fall by a fifth. Its chief executive describes this as a technology improvement. What is a more accurate description?

  • A. A shift in who creates the content, which supplied information the firm could not write — correct. This is the Web 2.0 shift stated exactly: the audience became the authors, and owners know things about fit, bulk and suitability that no staff writer working from supplier data can supply.
  • B. An improvement to product descriptions, achieved at lower cost than hiring more writers — The cost comparison is real and describes the saving rather than the mechanism. Ten more writers with the same source material would not have produced the same information.
  • C. A marketing improvement, since reviews increase the persuasiveness of a product page — Persuasion is one effect, and a returns fall is evidence that buyers chose more accurately rather than more enthusiastically, which is a different thing.
  • D. A service improvement, since reviews answer questions that would have reached support — Deflecting support contacts is a genuine secondary benefit. The returns figure measures decisions made before purchase, not questions answered after it.

8. 5.2 A hypothetical trade publisher argues that it adopted Web 2.0 when it launched a daily news feed written by its own editors. What is wrong with the claim?

  • A. A feed is a communication tool, and the later web is defined by conferencing tools — The three families of collaboration tools classify tools, not eras, and a conferencing tool used one-way would be just as one-way.
  • B. The strict line between content creator and content consumer is exactly where it was — correct. The change the chapter describes is in who creates content. A feed written by editors and read by everybody else preserves precisely the arrangement the later web dissolved.
  • C. Daily publication is not frequent enough to count as a dynamic web application — Frequency was never the distinction. A daily bulletin and a monthly one are both the organization talking while the audience listens.
  • D. The publisher would need a social network presence before it could make the claim — Presence on a particular platform is not the test. A publisher could run reviews, a forum and a wiki entirely on its own site and have changed the relationship completely.

9. 5.2 A hypothetical outdoor brand posts progress photographs from a client site. Nobody types a location, yet a reader identifies the site within hours. Which distinction explains this?

  • A. Between public and private posting, since the account's privacy settings were never adjusted — Privacy settings govern who can see a post. Everyone who could see this one could also read what was inside the files, whatever the setting.
  • B. Between a tag and a hashtag, since hashtags make content findable to people outside the audience — Hashtags do widen reach and they are typed deliberately, so they are visible to anybody reviewing the post before it goes out.
  • C. Between metadata a person adds and metadata a device attaches without being asked — correct. Coordinates, timestamps and device identifiers are attached automatically, which is why reviewing what somebody wrote catches nothing. The chapter's own example is a photograph that revealed where a man in hiding was.
  • D. Between synchronous and asynchronous publishing, since immediate posting leaves no time to review — Timing affects how much review is possible and changes nothing about what is inside the file, which would be there a week later too.

10. 5.2 A hypothetical software firm embeds a video it hosted on a sharing service into a partner's blog post. Both parties benefit and neither negotiated anything. What is this an instance of?

  • A. Collaboration, since two organizations produced a single piece of content together — Collaboration requires working the same problem together. Here two parties each did their own thing and both gained, which the chapter treats as a separate category.
  • B. Cooperation, where one participant's success improves the chances of the other — correct. The chapter's definition is exactly this: a win-win where one participant's success improves the chances of the other, and the embedded video is its own example of it.
  • C. Viral marketing, since the arrangement spreads the content through a network — Viral marketing depends on people choosing to pass content on. One deliberate embed between two known parties is not that mechanism.
  • D. Open innovation, since an external party contributed to the firm's own output — Open innovation brings outside parties into a firm's research and development. Nothing was invented here; existing content was placed somewhere useful.

11. 5.2 A hypothetical news site's editor argues that because a complainant has only twenty-eight followers, a hostile post about the site can be ignored. What does the chapter's evidence suggest?

  • A. Follower counts predict reach poorly, and the response to a post often outgrows the post — correct. The chapter's case is a tenant with a couple of dozen followers whose landlord sued her, at which point the dispute itself reached a national audience the original post never would have.
  • B. Ignoring it is correct, since responding would draw attention the post had not earned — Proportionate response is a real consideration and it has to be decided on the merits rather than on the poster's following, which is the reasoning error the chapter illustrates.
  • C. The post should be reported to the platform, since a small account is easier to have removed — Attempting removal is what converted the chapter's example from a small complaint into national coverage, so this is closer to the cause than to the cure.
  • D. Follower counts matter, but only on microblogging services rather than on other platforms — Nothing in the chapter limits the effect to one kind of platform. The mechanism is that other people amplify, and that works everywhere.

12. 5.2 A hypothetical museum wants tags added to forty thousand digitised photographs so they can be found. Its curator proposes a crowdsourcing marketplace. Under what condition is that a good fit?

  • A. When the budget is limited, since marketplace tasks cost a fraction of staff time — Cost is why people reach for it and not what makes it work. A cheap answer that is wrong is not cheaper than a correct one.
  • B. When speed matters, since a marketplace completes in weeks what staff take months over — Speed is a genuine advantage of the arrangement, and it is available regardless of whether the task is one a stranger can actually do.
  • C. When the tags describe what is visible rather than what a local would know — correct. The chapter's rule is that these tasks must be easy for any person and hard for a computer. Naming a street from 1974 is easy only for somebody who was there, which no fee can supply.
  • D. When accuracy is not critical, since a marketplace trades quality for cost and speed — Accepting poor quality is not a condition for using crowdsourcing; it is what happens when the task was a bad fit. Well-matched tasks return accurate work.

13. 5.2 A hypothetical utility adopts a cloud collaboration suite. Eight months later a feature its field teams depended on is changed by the provider without notice, and the workflow around it breaks. Which of the chapter's four domains does this belong to?

  • A. Information technology, since control of the tooling has moved to the provider — Loss of control over data and service quality is the chapter's technology risk, and it is about where things live rather than about when they change.
  • B. Organization, since staff had no documentation or support when the change landed — Thin documentation and support is the organizational risk and it is a consequence here rather than the cause. The change happened first.
  • C. Competition, since the disruption slowed the utility relative to its rivals — The competitive risk in the chapter's table concerns security and compliance enforcement. Being slowed down is an effect rather than the category.
  • D. Upgrade cycles, since the provider now decides when and how the software changes — correct. The chapter's upgrade-cycle risk is stated in these terms: tools and features can change without notice, causing problems for users and for the firm's technology strategy.

14. 5.2 A hypothetical trade association runs a members' wiki. In a year it accumulates four hundred pages, of which nineteen have ever been edited by anybody other than their original author. What does that figure indicate?

  • A. The pages are accurate, since incorrect content attracts correction quickly — Nothing establishes accuracy. Unedited could mean correct, unread by anybody who knew better, or not worth the trouble, and the figure cannot distinguish them.
  • B. Members are reading rather than writing, which is normal and needs no response — The reading-to-writing ratio is indeed skewed everywhere, and this figure measures something narrower: whether the people who do write ever build on each other.
  • C. Peer production is not occurring, so the wiki is functioning as a document store — correct. What makes a wiki more than a shared folder is that contributions improve on one another. Pages nobody touches after publication are documents in a different container.
  • D. The version history is not being used, which suggests the wiki is misconfigured — Retained history makes editing safe rather than making it happen. The mechanism is available and the behaviour is not occurring.

15. 5.2 A hypothetical bank's internal social network is described by staff as clumsy compared with the apps they use outside work. What does the chapter call this pressure?

  • A. Consumerization, where consumer products set the usability benchmark for internal tools — correct. The chapter names this directly: applications designed for the consumer market make their way into organizations, and users judge an internal tool against what they already use.
  • B. Technological inertia, since people will not adopt a tool without a tangible benefit — Inertia is refusing to move at all. These staff have moved and are reporting that the destination compares badly, which is a different complaint.
  • C. A generation gap, since younger staff hold higher expectations than older colleagues — A gap describes differing comfort across a workforce. Here the staff agree with each other, which is the opposite of a gap.
  • D. Technological integration, since the tool has not been joined to existing systems — Integration governs whether a tool connects to other systems. A perfectly integrated tool can still be unpleasant to use.

16. 5.2 A hypothetical food producer runs a campaign whose video can only be watched after registering an email address. Sharing is minimal. Which of the chapter's viral marketing principles was broken?

  • A. Do something unexpected, since a registration form is a familiar and tiresome device — Familiarity is a weakness of the campaign and not the mechanism that stopped it. An unexpected video behind a form would also fail to spread.
  • B. Make sequels, since a single video gives an audience nothing to come back for — Sequels sustain a campaign over time. This campaign failed at the first hand-off rather than at the second piece of content.
  • C. Make people feel something, since a form interrupts whatever the video established — Emotional content travels further and the form sits before the video rather than inside it, so it prevents the feeling rather than diluting it.
  • D. Never restrict access, since a gate stops the sharing the whole mechanism depends on — correct. The mechanism is that each viewer passes the content to the next one. A gate breaks that at the first hand-off, whatever the content is like.

17. 5.2 A hypothetical firm asks why a review platform it does not own can influence which restaurants near it succeed, given that the platform writes no reviews and takes no photographs. What is the answer?

  • A. It holds the reviews, and each new participant makes the collection more useful to everyone — correct. This is the network effect doing the work. The platform's asset is participation it did not create, and each contribution makes the whole more valuable to every user, which is what a competitor cannot buy.
  • B. It advertises heavily, which is why consumers consult it rather than a competitor — Advertising follows the position rather than producing it, and a well-advertised platform with no reviews would have nothing to consult.
  • C. It is preinstalled on most devices, so consumers reach it before any alternative — Integration with device software is real and downstream. It happened because the data was already worth reaching, not the other way round.
  • D. It is more accurate than the alternatives, having invested heavily in verifying that its reviews come from real customers — Nothing establishes superior accuracy, and the chapter records criticism about how reviews are handled, so this claim is unsupported.

18. 5.3 A hypothetical charity introduces an internal discussion platform. Every post is approved by a manager before it appears, and after four months there is almost no discussion. Which factor is this?

  • A. Critical mass, since too few people joined for the platform to feel worth using — Thin participation is the symptom being observed. The question is what produced it, and an approval queue is a cause rather than a count.
  • B. Technological integration, since the approval step was not built into the workflow — The approval step is working as designed. Building it more smoothly into the workflow would make a discouraging arrangement more efficient.
  • C. Culture, since open communication collides with a structure built on control — correct. The chapter says social applications do not do well within traditional top-down structures based on rigid hierarchies and control, and pre-approval is that structure applied to conversation.
  • D. Technological inertia, since staff continue to use the tools they already know — Inertia would show as people staying with email. Here they came to the platform and found their posts held, which is a different failure.

19. 5.3 A hypothetical insurer's pilot of an enterprise social network in one department is judged a success. The organization-wide rollout stalls at low participation. What did the pilot fail to test?

  • A. The software's ability to handle the whole organization's load rather than one department's — Capacity is testable and rarely the issue for this class of tool. Nothing described points at performance.
  • B. Whether the wider organization has the culture and processes a department volunteered — correct. The chapter warns that department-led pilots often work while organization-wide implementation needs changes in culture and processes and often the flattening of hierarchies. The pilot measured a self-selected department.
  • C. Whether the tool integrates with the systems used outside the pilot department — Integration gaps are a real risk and would show as friction rather than as absence. People would complain about the tool rather than not appearing.
  • D. Whether the training approach would scale to every location the insurer operates in — Training scale matters when capability is the constraint, and the pilot showed that people are capable. What is missing is a reason to participate.

20. 5.3 A hypothetical distributor discovers that most useful discussion has moved to a messaging tool one team installed itself, connected to nothing. What is the strongest response?

  • A. Block it, since an unapproved tool creates exposure the firm has not assessed — The exposure is genuine, and blocking moves the conversation somewhere the firm cannot see rather than back onto the official platform.
  • B. Ignore it, since the official platform is the one the firm is measuring — Ignoring it makes the firm's own measurement misleading and leaves two disconnected records of how decisions were made.
  • C. Migrate the team onto the official platform and close the unapproved tool — Moving a working conversation onto a platform that has already failed to attract one changes the location and nothing about why.
  • D. Treat it as evidence of what the work needs, and integrate rather than prohibit — correct. It is the clearest signal available about what people actually need, and the chapter's integration point applies: a tool joined to nothing becomes another place to look, which is what the official platform has become.

21. 5.3 A hypothetical brand discovers its agency bought favourable reviews for its product. A journalist has asked for comment by tomorrow. Which response best matches the chapter's playbook?

  • A. State that the firm commissioned no reviews, since that is the accurate position — The blame point is arguable and answers a question the public is not asking, which is whether the ratings mean anything now.
  • B. Respond publicly today, say what was found and what is being changed — correct. The chapter's playbook says the first day counts and asks for a response that is fast and appropriate, and its most successful example named what was found and what was being changed.
  • C. Wait until an independent analysis has verified the review flagging — Verification is prudent and slower than the story. The chapter's cautionary case is a company that waited two days and had to recover from its own silence.
  • D. Ask the marketplace to remove the flagged reviews before commenting — Removing the evidence before commenting reads as concealment whatever the intent, and the flagged reviews already appear in a report the journalist holds.

22. 5.3 A hypothetical airline monitors social media for mentions of its own brand name and nothing else. What does the chapter say it will miss?

  • A. Complaints where the brand name is misspelled or abbreviated by the poster — Misspellings are a genuine gap and a technical one that fuzzy matching handles routinely. It is not the gap the chapter identifies.
  • B. Coverage in traditional media, which social monitoring tools do not index — Traditional coverage is generally indexed and is downstream in any case. By the time a story is in the press the early signal was already missed.
  • C. Discussion using the terms an opponent would choose rather than the airline's own — correct. The chapter asks firms to identify their worst nightmare and know the signs to look for, including the search terms their opposition could use, which are rarely the firm's own vocabulary.
  • D. Anything posted on platforms where the airline does not itself hold an account or a published page — Monitoring tools read public content regardless of whether the firm has an account. Absence limits where a firm can reply rather than what it can see.

23. 5.3 A hypothetical restaurant group's board asks why it should assemble a crisis team now, when nothing has happened. What is the strongest answer?

  • A. Because deciding who may speak, and with which adviser, cannot be done while a story is running — correct. The chapter puts the crisis team first among its preparations precisely because the first day decides the shape of a story, and authority questions settled in advance take minutes rather than a morning.
  • B. Because the team will identify risks in the business that nobody has yet noticed — Risk identification is the second preparation, naming your worst nightmare, and it is a different activity from establishing who speaks.
  • C. Because insurers and regulators increasingly expect a documented crisis process to be in place before an incident — Insurance and governance expectations may well exist and are external reasons rather than the operational one the chapter gives.
  • D. Because the team can monitor social media more cheaply than an external service — Monitoring is the third preparation and is usually a tool or a service rather than something a crisis team does by hand.

24. 5.3 A hypothetical council debates whether anonymous accounts should be permitted on its public consultation forum. Which framing matches the chapter's treatment?

  • A. Anonymity should be removed, since it is the main cause of abusive behaviour online — The chapter documents deindividuation and the harassment that follows it and opens by naming legitimate needs for anonymity, so a one-sided removal misstates its position.
  • B. Anonymity should be preserved, since the harms are outweighed by convenience for users — Convenience is not the value the chapter identifies. It names privacy protection and freedom from oppression, which is a much stronger claim than convenience.
  • C. It is a dilemma: anonymity protects privacy and dissent while enabling harassment — correct. The chapter presents both sides: legitimate need for privacy and freedom from oppression on one, and deindividuation, trolling and cyberharassment on the other, which is what makes it a dilemma rather than a decision.
  • D. It is a technical question, since anonymity cannot be removed on this network anyway — The chapter does note that the design makes anonymity hard to remove, and difficulty is a practical constraint rather than a reason the question is finely balanced.

25. 5.3 A hypothetical software vendor is deciding what to say after resolving a widely shared complaint. Which closing action does the chapter's most successful example support?

  • A. Publish an apology from the chief executive and treat the matter as closed — An apology without a described change invites the same complaint again, and a second occurrence is worse because the record of the first is permanent.
  • B. Increase advertising for a period so that attention moves to other messages — Buying attention against an unresolved complaint is expensive and reads as exactly what it is, and the material stays retrievable regardless.
  • C. Introduce a review process so no employee can post about the firm publicly — That answers a problem the firm did not have when the complaint came from outside, and it imposes a control people route around.
  • D. Fix the cause and describe specifically what changed and how it was verified — correct. The chapter's example named what had been found, said the site had been closed and cleaned, and described what would change, and a survey afterwards found the response had largely restored trust.

26. 5.4 A hypothetical consultancy writes: our clients are very active on review sites, therefore buyer power is high. What does the claim lack?

  • A. Evidence that clients can act on the firm, such as switching, price pressure, or changed behaviour — correct. Buyer power is leverage. Activity is not leverage, and the sentence would be equally true of clients who cannot switch, which is where the reasoning fails.
  • B. A comparison against the industry average level of buyer power in professional services — Benchmarking adds useful context and does not repair an unsupported claim. A claim with no evidence is unsupported at any benchmark.
  • C. An acknowledgement that review activity is a substitutes question rather than a buyer-power one — The observation concerns the firm's own clients, so filing it under substitutes would replace one unsupported claim with a misclassified one.
  • D. A distinction between clients who post reviews and clients who only read them — That distinction would sharpen the evidence and is a refinement of a claim that currently has none rather than the missing element itself.

27. 5.4 A hypothetical kit maker's nine-year user forum absorbs most of its support questions and is cited by four in ten new buyers. A larger competitor launches a similar product with no community. Which force does the forum principally act on?

  • A. Rivalry, since it is how the firm differentiates against this particular competitor — Differentiation is what the forum does today and misses why it works. A rival can copy a feature and cannot copy nine years of accumulated answers.
  • B. The threat of new entrants, since it is a barrier money cannot buy — correct. A network-effect asset that grows with participation and cannot be purchased quickly is a barrier to entry, which is what explains a larger, cheaper competitor not automatically winning.
  • C. Bargaining power of buyers, since forum members are less sensitive to price — Nothing establishes a relationship between posting and price sensitivity, and inventing one to reach a force is the unsupported move the supplement warns against.
  • D. The threat of substitutes, since the forum substitutes for the firm's paid support — The forum does absorb support work, and a substitute is something a customer uses instead of buying from the industry at all rather than a part of what they bought.

28. 5.4 A hypothetical wholesaler wants to place an internal engineering wiki in the value chain. Where does it belong, and why?

  • A. Firm infrastructure, since it is a shared capability every part of the business draws on — Infrastructure covers capabilities like the intranet, governance and finance. A wiki holding engineering decisions serves one function rather than all of them.
  • B. Operations, since it makes the production of each order more efficient — Operations transforms inputs into outputs. A wiki changes the design that operations then executes, which is a step earlier.
  • C. Human resource management, since it captures what employees know before they leave — Retention of knowledge sounds like a people question and is about employment terms and staffing rather than about how the firm designs its products.
  • D. Technology development, since it changes how fast and how well the next thing is produced — correct. What an engineering wiki changes is the firm's ability to produce the next design well and quickly, which is what the technology development activity covers.

29. 5.4 A hypothetical publisher takes forty percent of its orders through one marketplace, which has raised its commission twice. Which force is this, and what follows?

  • A. Supplier power: the channel is an input, and the answer is to build a relationship it does not intermediate — correct. The platform supplies access to customers, which makes it an input, and its power grows with the share of revenue it carries. The response is to own a customer relationship the platform does not sit inside.
  • B. Rivalry: other publishers on the same marketplace are competing the margin away — Rivalry on the platform may well be intense and would show as price pressure rather than as the platform itself taking more.
  • C. Buyer power: the marketplace's customers are extracting better terms from the publisher — The marketplace's shoppers are not negotiating anything. It is the intermediary rather than the buyer that changed its terms.
  • D. New entrants: the marketplace has lowered the cost of entry for competing publishers that hold no distribution of their own — The marketplace does lower entry costs for competitors, which is a separate and real force. It does not explain a commission increase on this publisher's own orders.

30. 5.5 A hypothetical analyst is asked why a recommendation must name a specific value-chain activity rather than saying the firm needs a better digital strategy. What is the substantive reason?

  • A. The framework requires every recommendation to reference one of its nine activities — Satisfying a framework is ceremony. If naming the activity added nothing, the requirement would be worth dropping.
  • B. It turns a diagnosis into work that can be assigned, costed and measured afterwards — correct. A force says what is pressing on the firm; an activity says who can be given the work, what it will cost and what to watch afterwards, without which nothing can be acted on or judged.
  • C. Support activities are cheaper to change than primary activities, so the choice matters — Cost varies enormously within both categories, and this chapter contains cheap primary-activity changes and expensive infrastructure ones.
  • D. Forces describe the industry while activities describe the individual firm being advised — The distinction between industry-level and firm-level analysis is accurate as a description of the two frameworks and is not why a recommendation needs a location.

31. 5.5 A hypothetical practice has four candidate initiatives and is asked for one. Its analyst submits all four, ranked, so the partners can choose by budget. What is the analytical objection?

  • A. Four is too many to cost properly in the time available for the analysis — Time is a practical constraint and would be solved by working longer. The objection stands even with unlimited time.
  • B. Ranking implies a scoring method the analyst has not made explicit — An unexplained ranking is a fair criticism and a fixable one. Making the method explicit would not repair the deeper problem.
  • C. It hands the decision back and hides which pressure was being answered — correct. A single recommendation can be costed, argued and later judged. A ranked list looks generous, hands the decision back, and leaves no option properly costed or tied to a diagnosed force.
  • D. The partners will choose the cheapest, which is rarely the strongest option — Budget-driven selection is a plausible outcome and describes what the partners might do rather than what is wrong with the submission.

32. 5.5 A hypothetical analyst's comparison summary spends most of its two hundred words describing the history and scale of the company chosen for comparison. What has gone wrong?

  • A. The comparison company is too large, so the parallel with the advised firm is weak — Size mismatch can weaken a comparison and a well-drawn lesson can survive it. A small firm can learn something specific from a very large one.
  • B. The summary is descriptive where it should end in advice to the firm being advised — correct. The comparison exists to produce a lesson for the firm in front of you, and every sentence should be recoverable as advice to it. A profile answers a question nobody asked.
  • C. Two hundred words is insufficient to profile a company and draw a lesson from it — The length is a constraint that forces the choice rather than causing the error, and a summary of this length is ample for a lesson if the profile is left out.
  • D. The company should have been a direct competitor rather than one from another sector — A contrast from another sector is often the more useful comparison. What matters is the lesson, not the sector.

33. 5.5 A hypothetical practice measures a median of four and a half days to answer a cross-office technical question and sixty-two design decisions re-derived in a year because nobody found the earlier one. How should the analysis treat these two figures?

  • A. As one operations problem, since both describe project delivery running slower than it should — Calling both operations captures the delay and loses the second figure. Re-deriving a decision is not slow delivery; it is work being done that had already been done once.
  • B. As one technology development problem measured at two different scales — Both touch how well the firm produces the next thing, and merging them hides that they need different remedies: reaching a person, and reaching a record.
  • C. As two distinct problems: a coordination cost in delivery, and knowledge the firm cannot reach — correct. They are genuinely different activities with different fixes, and the written analysis asks for at least two value-creating areas, which these two supply honestly rather than by describing one twice.
  • D. As one human resource problem, since both describe how staff work with one another — Human resource management covers recruiting, developing and administering people. Neither figure concerns employment terms, and filing them here puts the recommendation where nobody could act on it.

34. 5.4 A hypothetical manufacturer, Denmoor Castings, tells its board that its internal engineering wiki gives it competitive advantage because none of its rivals has one. What does that claim still need?

  • A. A count of how many rivals have something comparable, gathered from their published material — Counting rivals establishes that the firm has something unusual. Unusual and advantageous are different claims, and plenty of things no competitor has are worth nothing.
  • B. A statement of which pressure the wiki relieves, and the evidence that it does — correct. An asset becomes an advantage only when it answers a pressure. Naming the force, and the evidence that the wiki moves it, is what turns a possession into an argument somebody can test.
  • C. A costing of the wiki, so the advantage can be set against what it was built for — A cost belongs in the recommendation and says nothing about whether an advantage exists. A cheap thing that relieves no pressure is still worth nothing.
  • D. A comparison of the wiki against the commercial products the firm could have bought — Product comparisons decide what to build. They are a procurement question, and they arrive after somebody has established that the capability is worth having.

35. 5.4 A hypothetical recruiter-heavy consultancy runs a professional-network campaign that halves its cost of hiring an experienced engineer. Where does that land in the value chain, and does it matter which activity you name?

  • A. Marketing and sales, and naming the activity matters mainly for presentation — Marketing and sales acts on somebody deciding whether to buy. A candidate is not buying anything, and treating the naming as cosmetic gives up the reason for doing it.
  • B. Operations, because a shorter hiring cycle means projects start sooner — Projects do start sooner, which is a downstream effect. Operations is where inputs are turned into outputs, and hiring sits before that rather than inside it.
  • C. Human resource management, and naming it decides who owns the work and what is measured — correct. Recruiting is human resource management, and naming it puts the work with the people who do it and fixes what gets measured, which is what makes a recommendation actionable rather than decorative.
  • D. Firm infrastructure, because recruiting is a shared capability the whole firm draws on — Infrastructure covers planning, finance, legal and the systems everything draws on. Recruiting is a distinct support activity with its own name in the framework.

36. 5.5 A hypothetical analyst rates four of the five forces on a firm and skips supplier power, on the grounds that a professional services firm has no real suppliers. What is wrong with that?

  • A. Nothing, provided the omission is stated; a force that does not apply need not be rated — Declaring an omission is better than hiding one and still leaves the analysis incomplete. A force rated low with evidence is a finding; a force skipped is a gap.
  • B. The firm does have suppliers, since it buys software, premises and professional insurance — Those purchases are genuine supplier relationships and they are the least interesting ones, because none of them has leverage over the firm worth writing about.
  • C. All five are asked for, and the platforms and labour markets it depends on are suppliers — correct. The deliverable asks for all five, and in a services firm the powerful inputs are the directory or platform that brings enquiries and the labour market for scarce specialists, both of which can dictate terms.
  • D. Supplier power should be inferred from buyer power, which the analyst has already rated — The two forces are independent. Strong buyers tell you nothing about whether your inputs are concentrated, and inferring one from the other invents a finding.

37. 5.5 A hypothetical analyst offers marketing and sales as the two value-chain areas where technology could create an advantage, describing an advertising change for one and a lead-handling change for the other. What is the objection?

  • A. The two changes are too similar in cost, so the comparison between them is uninformative — Similar costs would make the choice between them harder and would not affect whether two distinct areas had been found, which is the requirement in question.
  • B. Marketing and sales is a single activity, so only one area has actually been identified — correct. Marketing and sales is one primary activity in the framework, so describing two changes inside it identifies one area twice. The deliverable asks for at least two genuinely different activities.
  • C. Neither change is technological, so the areas do not answer what was asked — Both changes could be delivered by technology, and whether they are is a separate question from whether they sit in two different places in the value chain.
  • D. Advertising belongs to marketing and lead handling belongs to service, so both are misfiled — Lead handling belongs with marketing and sales rather than with service, which acts on somebody who has already bought. Misfiling is not the problem here.

What to do next. Spend a week watching one organization you already belong to — an employer, a team, a club, this course — and write down where its coordination actually breaks. Not the tool it uses; the moment somebody could not find what somebody else already knew, or waited a day for an answer that took ten seconds to give. Then name the force that moment is evidence of, put it somewhere in the value chain, and say what one initiative would change and how you would tell whether it worked. That is the whole method, and it is far easier to practise on an organization you can actually see.
