A decision is a choice made before you know how it turns out. This module is about narrowing that gap: what evidence a decision needs, which decisions can be answered by a procedure and which cannot, what business intelligence and machine learning each actually produce, what a generative model is good for and what it will confidently invent, and where knowledge and location fit. It ends by connecting the choice to Porter’s five forces and the value chain.

Learn by making the decisions

Each section teaches an idea and then asks you to use it. Nothing is graded and nothing is sent anywhere; every activity explains itself the moment you answer, including the options you did not choose. Work in this order:

  1. Start with the decision, not the tool. Work out what would have to be true for a choice to be obvious, and what evidence would settle it.
  2. Place the decision on the continuum. Structured, semi-structured or unstructured decides what kind of help is even possible.
  3. Read what the system actually reports. Slice a cube, read a dashboard, and find the chart that is telling the truth and misleading you at the same time.
  4. Follow a model through its workflow. Training data, fitting, evaluation on cases it has not seen, and the error rate nobody quotes.
  5. Use a generative model on purpose. Sort the jobs it is suited to from the jobs where a confident invention costs somebody real money, then govern the difference.
  6. Make the strategy connection. Read the same evidence through the five forces and the value chain, then recommend one initiative and defend it.
  7. Test your understanding. The final challenge reports by objective so you know which section to reread.

Every organization, person and figure used for practice is invented. No vendor, product, model name or version appears anywhere in this module: capability is taught by use case, because any product comparison is out of date before a term ends.

Objective 7.1 · Part 1

Four kinds of analytics, and which can act on its own

Eight requests land on your desk in your first week at Fairmount Provisions, an invented regional distributor of food and household goods running forty depots and eleven hundred staff. Not one of them says what kind of analysis it wants. Each describes a problem and stops. Before you can build anything you have to know which of four questions a request is actually asking, because the four need different tools, different budgets and different amounts of human judgement afterwards.

Four questions, in the order a decision maker asks them

Business analytics answers four questions about the same business, and the chapter arranges them as a continuum. Each one takes you a step further from the record and a step closer to the action.

  • Descriptive analytics summarizes and describes past data to establish what happened, and it is the foundation the other three are built on.
  • Diagnostic analytics looks for the correlations or causal relationships inside past data, so that a mistake is not repeated or a success is.
  • Predictive analytics forecasts a likely future outcome, such as the sales of one product in one region over the next quarter.
  • Prescriptive analytics analyzes the possible scenarios and recommends the best course of action, and at its far end takes that action itself.

The chapter's own examples are deliberately ordinary. A sales manager wants the sales of one product in one period by one salesperson, which is descriptive. Understanding the seasonality of demand, or the demographics of the buyers, explains why a launch succeeded or failed, which is diagnostic.

Sorting an inbox is not a vocabulary exercise, because the department that wrote the request almost never uses any of these four words. Ask instead where the answer lives: in a month that has finished, in a relationship inside that month, in a month that has not happened yet, or in an action somebody has to take tonight.

Getting it wrong is expensive in an unglamorous way. A request filed under the wrong kind is built with the wrong tool and then built again, so the firm pays for a build nobody uses. The four builds costed later in this section average $64,500, and finance prices a misfile across all eight requests at about $60,000.

Descriptive — What happened. Summarizing and describing data about a period that has closed.

  • Break Depot 12's 9,400 kilos of chilled write-offs out by product line and by weekday — It asks for last month's figures arranged in a more useful shape, which is a summary and nothing more.

Diagnostic — Why it happened. Correlations or causal relationships inside data about the past.

  • Account for three depots' Tuesday delivery windows all slipping in the same March week — Account for it is a request for the relationship behind an event that has already happened.
  • Account for fuel cost per drop rising 14% in the South while it fell 4% in the North — Both numbers are already known and quoted in the request; what is wanted is the reason for the gap between them.

Predictive — What will happen. A forecast of a likely future outcome, for a person to act on.

  • Tell supply planning the pallet count the East depots will need in week 31 — A quantity for a week that has not happened yet is a forecast, and a planner still decides what to order against it.
  • Name the restaurant accounts most likely to stop ordering from us next quarter — Most likely and next quarter put this in the future; deciding what to offer those accounts is the human's part.

Prescriptive — What to do. A recommended course of action, and sometimes the action itself.

  • Set tonight's loading plan for all 40 depots each morning, with no planner touching it — It asks for the course of action itself, taken automatically, which is decision automation rather than decision support.
  • Quote each account a courier rate from the competitor's price and our van costs, automatically — A recommended action generated and then executed with nobody in between is prescriptive analytics at its automated end; a recommendation a manager signs off first is prescriptive too, and stops at decision support.

Where the human stands, and where one does not

Descriptive and diagnostic analytics both look backwards. They give a detailed picture of the current state of the business, and the chapter is blunt that this provides only limited input into a decision. You know what happened and why, and you still have to decide.

Predictive analytics provides more input by naming what is likely. Even then, human input remains essential for determining the best course of action. A demand forecast does not say how much marketing budget to allocate against it, how many pallets to order, or which customer to call first.

Prescriptive analytics is the only one of the four that reaches the action, and it does so in two quite different ways. Each of the two carries a failure worth naming beside it.

  • Decision support hands a manager a recommended course of action, which that manager can accept, amend or refuse.
  • Decision automation takes the human out of the loop and lets the system act on its own recommendation.
  • A prediction nobody acts on changes nothing, which is why a forecast is usually paired with a decision about what to do with it.
  • An automated decision with a weak model behind it repeats that model's mistakes at scale, politely, and under your company's name.

The chapter's example of the automated end is a ride-hailing service that combines predictive and prescriptive analytics to set its own fares. The price rises on its own when demand is predicted to increase, after a concert or during bad weather, which in theory pulls more drivers to where that demand is.

Notice what the automation did not remove. Somebody chose that fares should follow predicted demand, chose how far they may move, and answers for it when the rule embarrasses the company. Automating a decision moves the human earlier in the process rather than deleting them.

What a data-driven organization actually is

Organizations that make decisions which can be backed up with verifiable data are called data-driven organizations. The research the chapter cites finds them measurably more productive and profitable, better able to respond to ongoing threats and opportunities, and better able to plan for the future.

There is a second effect that will reach you sooner than the profit figure. When data backs a decision, the decision can be pushed lower into the organization. A depot manager holding a reliable forecast does not have to escalate, which frees senior management time for the decisions only that level can make.

Having the right tools is not sufficient. Three things have to be true besides the software, and a company missing any of them owns an expensive system and little else.

  • Integrated information has to exist, because the missing figure is usually sitting already in disconnected spreadsheets, reports and databases.
  • Data literacy, the ability to organize, analyze and communicate with data, is a skill every business user needs rather than the analysts' private specialism.
  • Judgement and creativity are what translate an insight into an action, since no analysis on its own decides what the company should do next.

The weather these decisions get made in

External factors create opportunities and threats in the same movement, and the chapter insists on that pairing because managers tend to file each factor under one heading or the other.

  • Globalization opens new markets to compete in while exposing the firm to new competitors and to geopolitical turmoil.
  • Consumer access to information through social media and mobile devices makes switching to a competitor easier than it has ever been.
  • Regulation, such as the Sarbanes-Oxley Act that followed large corporate and banking failures, adds reporting requirements a firm must satisfy.
  • Shorter product life cycles force companies to develop new products faster and to answer changing conditions quickly.

With pressure to reduce costs, money goes to the systems that pay back the most, and pay it back soonest. These tools return quickly for three plain reasons: they put the right information in front of the right person in time to react, they leverage the enterprise systems already paid for, and they help retain the most profitable customers.

What the systems themselves cost to run

There is one more cost, and the chapter files it as a sustainability question rather than a line on an invoice. AI is genuinely useful for sustainability, an approach sometimes called Green-by-AI: predicting energy demand, tuning building lighting and heating, optimizing traffic flows and transit routes, and cutting waste in agriculture through more precise use of seed, fertilizer and water.

The same systems carry a footprint of their own, and it is not a small one.

  • Energy is consumed in tremendous amounts by training and running these models, and by generative models most of all.
  • Carbon depends on what powers the data center, so the same model is not equally clean in every location.
  • Water and waste heat matter because data centers need large volumes of water for cooling and give off heat.
  • Raw materials and e-waste arrive at both ends, in producing the processors and again in disposing of them.

The answer the chapter describes, Green-in-AI, tries to shrink that footprint without losing the performance that makes the green applications work. Smaller language models trained on smaller, more specific data sets need fewer resources and may do better on a narrow task. Statistical techniques can cut model size or make training more efficient. Renewable power and more efficient processors relieve some of what is left.

Bring that back to your inbox. The four kinds do not cost the same to run, and here the expensive ones are the ones that run without you. A monthly summary reads records you already keep. A model that issues a loading plan for forty depots retrains and runs every night, and its bill arrives every night too.

Objective 7.1 · Part 2

The decision-making continuum

Fairmount Provisions is a hypothetical regional distributor of food and household goods: 40 depots, 1,100 staff, selling to independent grocers, school kitchens and restaurants. In week 31 its East depots ran short of chilled stock, and three school-kitchen contracts went elsewhere. Nobody was surprised by the shortage. It happens every year. What failed was a staging figure set in January and never looked at again.

Planning stopped being an annual event

Organizations used to lack the data and the tools to plan continuously. A strategic plan was agreed for a cycle, say a year. Managers then built budgets for their part of it and spent the year executing them.

Those budgets were backward looking. They rested on historical data rather than on a clear understanding of current conditions and forecasts of future trends. While the pace of change stayed slow, that was adequate.

It is not adequate now. In a continuous planning process, an organization keeps monitoring and analyzing its data and its business processes. Those results lead to adjustments in how the organization is managed, and the same results are reflected in updates to its plans.

The chapter draws that as a circle of four stages: manage leads to plan, plan leads to monitor, monitor leads to analyze, and analyze leads back to manage. Each stage has an ordinary job at a distributor.

  • Manage is real-time management: tonight's trucks get loaded and this week's calls get made while the week is still running.
  • Plan updates and adjusts the plans themselves, which is where the week-31 staging figure should have been revised in June rather than defended in August.
  • Monitor tracks the business processes, which here means pallets shipped, pallets short, and orders a depot could not fill.
  • Analyze compares actual performance against the goal, turning the distance between what was staged and what was needed into a number.

The loop runs on timely and accurate insight drawn from relevant data. Fairmount had the data and worked the gap out every year. What never happened is the second half of that sentence: the result was never reflected in an update to the plan, and three contracts is what the missing step cost.

A second question, about the decision itself

The continuum in the chapter's own heading runs from what happened, to why, to what will happen, to what should be done, and Part 1 of this objective worked through it. A second question is worth asking before any of those tools gets scoped, and it is about the decision rather than the analysis.

Ask how much of the procedure can be written down in advance, before the situation arrives. Answers fall into three shapes.

  • Structured decisions have procedures that can be specified ahead of time, so they can be programmed into operational systems and made with little human involvement.
  • Semistructured decisions have part of their procedure specified in advance, but not far enough to yield one specific recommendation, so judgment and expertise finish the job.
  • Unstructured decisions have few or no procedures that can be specified in advance, because the problem is complex and nonroutine and its consequences reach the whole organization.

At Fairmount all three sit on one Tuesday. A depot reorders chilled milk when the case count falls below its reorder point. A planner decides how many extra pallets to stage in the East for week 31. The board decides whether to serve school kitchens at all.

Structured — The procedure can be written down before the situation arrives, and the answer can be checked afterwards.

  • Reordering chilled milk at Depot 12 when the case count falls below its reorder point — The rule exists before the situation does, so the system can raise the order and a supervisor only confirms it.
  • Holding an order that would push an account past its agreed credit limit — The limit and the balance are both already in the system, so the check is a written rule with a checkable result.
  • Applying the agreed volume discount once a grocer's order passes its contracted case count — Both the threshold and the discount were agreed in advance, so the system applies them and the invoice shows the result.

Semistructured — Part of the procedure is known, but not far enough to name one answer. Judgment finishes it.

  • Deciding how many extra chilled pallets to stage in the East for week 31 — A forecast narrows the range, but somebody still weighs spoilage against a stockout and picks the number.
  • Setting next quarter's promotional allowance for the restaurant accounts — Past response rates guide the figure, yet no procedure turns them into one defensible amount, so judgment closes it.
  • Choosing which depots get Saturday overtime when volume runs above plan — The volume figures are known and the rule is not, so cost, fatigue and customer promises get traded off by a manager.

Unstructured — Few or no procedures can be specified in advance. The problem is complex, nonroutine and far-reaching.

  • Deciding whether to keep serving school kitchens after losing three contracts — No procedure covers it, the answer reaches pricing, routing and staffing, and nobody can check it before the fact.
  • Deciding whether to lease seasonal cold storage or build a tenth East depot — The problem is nonroutine and the consequences run for years, so the figures inform the argument rather than settle it.
  • Deciding whether to merge the North and South regions under one manager — Few procedures can be specified ahead of a change this complex, and its effects reach across the whole organization.

The shape belongs to the decision, not the decider

Here is the mistake worth spending a paragraph on. The three shapes do line up loosely with organizational levels: structured decisions cluster at the operational level, semistructured ones at the managerial level, unstructured ones at the executive level. That is a tendency, not a definition.

The test is the decision. Ask whether the procedure for this situation can be written down before the situation arrives, and whether anyone can check the answer afterwards.

The same words change shape as they change hands. How much chilled stock do we need is structured for a depot supervisor ordering Tuesday's milk against a reorder point, with a shelf anyone can walk over and count. Asked of nine depots across a peak week seven weeks out, no reorder point covers it.

Credit behaves the same way. A clerk checking whether an order fits under an account's limit is applying a rule. A director deciding whether to raise that limit for a school district mid-contract is not.

Two practical consequences follow from where a decision sits.

  • Seniority follows the decision, because the less of a procedure can be written down, the higher in the organization the call travels.
  • The available help changes with it, since a system can decide only where the procedure is known and the outcome is checkable.

What kind of help is possible

Where a decision sits settles what a system can honestly offer it. The chapter's own figure makes the point from the other side: descriptive, diagnostic and predictive analytics all run through human input before a decision is made, and only prescriptive analytics splits into decision support and decision automation.

  • Automate it where the procedure is known and the result is checkable, which is how a reorder rule or an automatic price runs unattended.
  • Support it where part of the procedure is known, with a forecast, a what-if run or a ranked list that a person then acts on.
  • Inform it where no procedure exists, by assembling the evidence for an argument that somebody senior still has to have.

The chapter's example of an automated decision is a ride-hailing service whose fares rise on their own when demand is predicted to rise, with no person in the loop. Notice what makes that possible: the rule can be written down, and the outcome is measurable within the hour.

Support is the honest offer in the middle. Forecasting that the East region needs 1,240 pallets in week 31 does not order them, and a planner who reads that number may still stage fewer and answer for the difference.

At the unstructured end a system gathers evidence and stops. It can show what the school-kitchen contracts earned and how their margins moved. It cannot tell the board whether to keep them.

Classify the decision before you scope the system. A structured decision can be handed a rule, a semistructured one a forecast, and an unstructured one nothing better than good evidence.

Measures, dimensions, and the cube

The chief financial officer of Fairmount Provisions — an invented regional food and household goods distributor running forty depots — has four questions and a spreadsheet that answers none of them. Last year’s sales sit in a cube: four product lines, four regions, four quarters, and two measures, revenue and gross margin, both in thousands of dollars. Sixty-four numbers for each measure, which is a hundred and twenty-eight numbers more than anyone can hold in their head.

A decision support system is a model you can argue with

Start with the older tool, because the cube sits inside it. A decision support system (DSS) analyses structured data to support decision making, which is descriptive analytics. Modern ones reach a little way into predictive analytics as well.

A DSS is an interactive decision aid that uses models to manipulate data. A model here is a formula, not a machine learning model. Take an average of past sales and adjust it for seasonal change: that formula is the model. A more complicated one might use time-series analysis or linear regression.

Once a manager has a formula they can interrogate, there are four ways to interrogate it:

  • What-if analysis makes a hypothetical change to an input, such as a loan duration or an interest rate, and shows how the result moves.
  • Sensitivity analysis weighs each input by how likely it is to occur, so a one-in-four chance and a three-in-four chance do not count the same.
  • Goal seeking fixes the end state you want and works backwards to the input value that would produce it.
  • Optimisation finds the best balance between several parameters inside the constraints you are genuinely under.

Keep the limits in view. A decision support system handles relatively simple analyses of structured data, usually pulled from transaction processing systems, and what it produces is input to a decision. Human judgement still makes the final one.

Online analytical processing

Online analytical processing (OLAP) is the process of quickly running complex, multidimensional analyses of data held in a database that is optimised for retrieval. The questions are ad hoc, and they go past the summaries and aggregations an ordinary database query returns.

The shape to remember is the sort of question the chapter calls typical: what were the profits for each week last year, by sales region and customer type? That question carries one measure and three dimensions. A two-dimensional table cannot hold it, and a multidimensional query against a large transactional database used to take hours.

Three pieces of machinery bring the answer back while the meeting is still running:

  • The OLAP server knows how the data are organised in the database and carries special functions for analysing them.
  • Pre-aggregation works the totals out in advance, so a query extracts only the subset of data it actually needs.
  • In-memory computing holds the data in a computer’s main memory rather than on a comparatively slow hard drive.

Fairmount’s cube reads off a disk, and every query takes about 4.1 seconds. Moving it into memory costs $200,000 and brings that to 0.3 seconds. Nothing about the questions changes. What changes is that the third follow-up question in a meeting gets asked at all, and the third one is usually the one that matters.

Measures and dimensions

OLAP separates two things a flat table mixes together. Measures, sometimes called facts, are the values you want to analyse: sales revenue, the number of orders placed. Dimensions are the ways you summarise them: region, time, product line, distributor.

Said in one line: revenue is a measure, region is a dimension, and you add the first up by the second. Getting that backwards is how a cube ends up with a design nobody can query. Fairmount’s cube names its parts plainly:

  • Two measures are carried here, revenue and gross margin, both stated in thousands of dollars.
  • Three dimensions organise them: the product line, the region, and the quarter of the trading year.
  • Members are the individual values inside a dimension, so chilled, the West and Q3 are each one member.
  • Hierarchies run inside a dimension, which is what lets a user move between years, quarters, months and days.

The hierarchy is why a dimension is more than a list. Analysing sales by geography, you can drill down from state to county to city to the individual store, or roll up from state to sales region, to country, to continent. Time behaves the same way.

Reporting, dashboards, and asking what if

Three usage models on one screen

Visualization is the display of complex data relationships using a variety of graphical methods, so that a manager grasps the result of an analysis quickly. A digital dashboard is the everyday case: key performance indicators and other summary information, presented to the people who decide things.

To give a decision maker the greatest benefit, a dashboard supports three usage models at once. A screen carrying only the first of them is a report with rounded corners. Read them as three different jobs:

The design elements, and what they can hide

Dashboards use maps, charts, sparklines and graphics shaped like traffic lights, thermometers or speedometers, because a shape is read faster than a figure. Visual analytics pushes that further, combining analysis techniques with interactive visualizations so that a person's knack for making sense of noisy data and a machine's capacity to retrieve and compute are used together rather than separately.

Read faster also means wrong faster. Three chart habits mislead honest readers, and all three survive a review because every number underneath them is correct:

  • A truncated axis starts the scale at 92% rather than zero, so a one-point dip in on-time delivery is drawn as a collapse.
  • A share without its base reports that returns hit 40% of orders at a depot, omitting that the depot shipped five orders that day.
  • An average over a mix reports a fleet-wide 96% on-time rate while two depots sit at 78% and thirty-eight quietly cover for them.

The defence is not a better chart. It is asking, every time, where the axis starts, what the denominator is, and what the average is averaging over.

Objective 7.2 · Part 3

Patterns in data nobody labelled

A clustering run finished overnight on the Fairmount Provisions customer list and produced three groups. It labelled them 0, 1 and 2. That is the whole of what it has to say. Nobody told the algorithm what to look for, and there is nobody to ask what it found. The structure is the machine's work. The meaning, and the decision that follows from it, are yours.

Labelled data, and data with no answers attached

Machine learning lets computers learn from data without being explicitly programmed, and improve as they are exposed to more of it. It uses algorithms, which are step-by-step procedures or rules, to find patterns in large amounts of structured or unstructured data. The chapter splits it three ways, and the split turns on what you hand the algorithm to start with.

Supervised learning is trained on labelled examples, which the chapter calls the ground truth. Hand it deliveries already marked late or on time and it learns which attributes separate the two, then classifies a delivery it has never seen. Unsupervised learning is handed no labels at all. It is asked to find structure, and structure is all it gives back.

The two differ at every point of the job, not only at the input:

  • What goes in is a labelled data set for supervised learning, while unsupervised learning takes the rows exactly as they came off the system.
  • What it is for is classifying and predicting a new case, where unsupervised learning is there to help you understand the data you already hold.
  • What comes out is a prediction from supervised learning, and from unsupervised learning a set of patterns and relationships with no meaning attached.
  • What you do for supervised work is define and validate the training; for unsupervised work it is to validate and interpret the groups it produced.

A third type is worth naming once. Reinforcement learning learns through trial and error in a changing environment, chasing a strategy that maximises rewards over time. That is how a dynamic pricing scheme or a building's heating gets tuned, and it is not what this section is about.

Three jobs, and the techniques they need

Three things landed on your desk this week with no answer key attached: a question about which products move together, a fuel card file finance believes is fine, and that clustering run. Each one needs a different unsupervised technique, and the chapter names a fourth that is association discovery run over time.

  • Clustering groups records that have similar values for their attributes, which is how a customer list turns into segments.
  • Anomaly detection is a variant of clustering pointed the other way, hunting the outliers: the records that resemble nothing else in the set.
  • Association discovery finds items that turn up on the same transaction, which a grocer knows as market basket analysis.
  • Sequence discovery runs the same idea over time, answering what a customer buys next rather than what they buy alongside.

Each pays for itself somewhere different. A bank runs anomaly detection over its transactions to find unusual patterns that may be fraud. A turbine manufacturer runs it over vibration readings to replace a part before it fails. Neither one was told in advance what an unusual reading looks like.

Support and confidence, and why a rule needs both

An association rule carries two numbers. Support is how often the combination shows up across all the transactions analysed, and it tells you whether the rule is big enough to bother with. Confidence is the share of the transactions holding the first item that also hold the second, and it tells you how reliable the rule is.

The chapter's stock illustration is coffee and sugar: the pair occurs in 20% of transactions, and 80% of transactions containing coffee also contain sugar. Both figures are counted off one particular file, so the same pair of items reads differently on a different file.

Here is that arithmetic run on twenty orders from the Fairmount independent restaurant trade. Coffee is on ten of the twenty. Eight orders hold coffee and sugar together, so support is 40%. Eight of the ten coffee orders also hold sugar, so confidence is 80%. Merchandising will not move a depot pick-face for anything under 20% support and 75% confidence, because below that the disruption costs more than the rule is worth.

Rules fail that bar in two opposite ways, and a third thing changes when you read a rule backwards:

  • High confidence on tiny support is true and worthless. Saffron to cooking oil is 100% confident and stands on the one order in twenty holding saffron.
  • High support on low confidence is common and unreliable. Cooking oil to chicken turns up on 30% of the orders but holds only two times in three.
  • Direction moves one number and not the other. Chicken to cooking oil is the same 30% support, because support counts the orders holding both, yet its confidence is 100%.

The same limit runs through all three jobs, so hold it as one rule rather than three separate cautions:

Objective 7.2 · Part 4

The machine learning workflow, end to end

Fairmount Provisions has a data scientist for two quarters and a chief executive who wants something that knows a delivery is going to be late before it is. The parts that decide whether that works are not hers. What problem to point it at, what data to feed it, what the word late even means, and whether the result is good enough to put in front of a customer: those are yours, because they need the trade rather than the statistics.

A vision step, four stages, and arrows that run backwards

A machine learning project opens by defining the AI vision and then runs through four main stages, and going back a stage is an ordinary part of the process rather than a failure. The chapter draws two dashed arrows for exactly that reason: one from the model stage back to the data, and one from deployment back to the problem.

Here is the whole workflow, and what each stage is for:

  • Define the AI vision asks what the organization is trying to achieve with any of this, before a single use case has been named or costed.
  • Problem identifies candidate use cases, assesses each one for value and feasibility, and prioritises them. The accuracy bar is set here, in advance.
  • Data acquires the records, cleans and prepares them, labels them, then splits them into a set to train on and a set held back to test with.
  • Model selects an algorithm, trains it, validates it and tests it, which normally runs relatively quickly, and is also where you may learn the problem is not solvable this way at all.
  • Deployment puts the model into an app or a process, trains the people using it, then monitors, retrains and maintains it as conditions change.

The data scientist owns model selection and training. You own the problem, the data sources, the quality standard, the meaning of the label, the resources, and the decision at the end. Data scientists are hired for statistics; they are rarely hired for knowing your depots.

Garbage in, garbage out

Acquiring and cleaning the data is where the months go, and organizations are regularly surprised by how many. Fairmount's raw extract is wrong in six specific ways, and what survives the clean-up is the 2,400 labelled deliveries the model is built on. Every fix is a decision somebody has to defend later.

Four of them are worth learning as a set, because in each one the tempting answer is the wrong one:

  • 180 records with no arrival timestamp get dropped, because filling them with a depot average would invent the very fact the label is made of.
  • 412 exact duplicates, left by a January file imported twice, get removed, since duplicated rows add weight to one month rather than adding information.
  • 97 records showing minus four hours get dropped, because an impossible value is a broken record rather than an extreme one, almost certainly a clock or a time zone.
  • The September week a hurricane shut two depots stays in the file, flagged as a disruption, because deleting those deliveries teaches the model a world in which severe weather does not happen.

Then the data has to be labelled. For supervised learning the label is the ground truth, and everything the model learns, it learns from that definition. At Fairmount, late means the van arrived after the promised window closed, recorded by the same clock in every depot and dependent on nobody's opinion.

Two easier definitions were on the table and both are traps. Late as whatever the driver marked on the handheld teaches the model which drivers are strict. Late as whatever the customer complained about teaches it who complains, since most late deliveries generate no complaint at all.

The number you are allowed to quote

Some records train the model and the rest are held back, so the model can be tested on data it has never seen. Seventy to eighty percent for training is the usual balance. Fairmount trained on 1,800 records and held back 600.

Skip the split and something strange happens: the accuracy goes up. A model tested on the rows it memorised reports a wonderful number, and it is not lying. It is answering a different question. How well do you remember what I showed you is not the same question as how well will you do on Monday, and only the second one is a forecast.

A prediction, not a fact

The percentage at the bottom of a test is an average, and averaging is what hides the interesting part. Overall accuracy counts a missed late delivery and a false alarm as the same event. A missed late delivery is a customer nobody warned. A false alarm is a van re-sequenced for nothing. They do not cost the same.

So the setting that decides how readily the tool cries late is not a technical choice. Push it one way and you catch more genuine misses and waste more vans. Push it the other way and you waste fewer vans and warn fewer customers. Somebody has to price that trade, and it is not the data scientist.

Three habits follow from all of this, and they are the difference between a tool dispatch trusts and one they quietly stop opening:

  • Quote the held-back number to anyone making a decision on it, and say out loud how many unseen records that estimate is resting on.
  • Show the error table beside the percentage, because the two kinds of mistake land on different people and only the table tells you how many of each you get.
  • Set the bar before training, in writing, so the result is measured against a promise made in advance rather than against your relief at having a result.

What the data already believes

Bias in these systems is usually inherited rather than installed. A model learns the patterns in its training data, and any inequity in that data is reproduced in the output, sometimes amplified. Nobody has to write a discriminatory rule for a discriminatory result to arrive.

The chapter's case is a diagnostic assistant trained on more than 15,000 medical records. It tested well, then performed noticeably worse for rural, minority and lower-income patients, because the records came from large urban hospitals serving a narrow slice of the population. An audit found the gap. The file had held it all along.

The repair is not simple either. Gathering records from a wider range of clinics and regions would improve fairness, and would also collect sensitive data from people who may not know it is being used or may not have consented. Reducing bias and protecting privacy pull against each other, which is why training data is an ethical question and not only a technical one.

Fairmount met a smaller version of the same problem. Someone proposed predicting which depot managers deserve promotion, using twelve years of appraisal records. The data exists, which is the trap. The file records who was promoted before, not who deserved it, so the model would learn whatever those old decisions contained and hand it back with a percentage attached.

Two sentences carry this section. Accuracy measured on the data a model was fitted to is not evidence it works on new cases, and a model's output is a prediction with an error rate rather than a fact. The split, the held-back test, the error table and the bar set in advance all exist to keep those two sentences true.

Objective 7.3 · Part 1

How generative AI actually gets used

A depot supervisor at Fairmount Provisions has a customer on the phone about fourteen cases of chilled product that arrived warm. She types the question into a free chat tool and gets an answer in two seconds: returns accepted within 30 days, no documentation needed, credit to the original payment method. It is fluent, confident and entirely invented. The real policy is 72 hours with a photograph of the temperature logger. Nobody had told the tool the policy, so it wrote the one that sounded most likely.

What the machine is actually doing

The models in the previous objective were tied to their data. An algorithm that predicts which passengers will miss a flight will not work for a different airline, let alone for an operator of high-speed trains, because the source systems and the data are different. That is why those algorithms are called narrow AI.

Generative AI is a type of artificial intelligence that creates new content — text, images, audio — based on the data it was trained on. A large language model is the text version of that idea. It is fed tremendous amounts of writing and learns the semantic and syntactic relationships between words, structures and themes.

Asked a question, it analyses your prompt, works out the likelihood of the words that could come next, and selects one according to those probabilities. Then it does the same thing again on the text it has just written. It is autocomplete on an extraordinary scale.

Four consequences follow from that description, and each of them matters more than the machinery does:

  • It does not understand the text, but works from statistical patterns learned in training rather than from any notion of what is true.
  • The sampling is random, which is what makes the output feel human rather than mechanical, and also makes it less predictable run to run.
  • The output is novel and unstructured — a paragraph, an image, a summary — where narrow AI returned a classification or a predicted number.
  • One model does many jobs across domains it was never built for, adapting to new tasks with limited additional input and no retraining.

What companies actually do with it

The use cases in the chapter are ordinary long before they are exotic. Most of the value sits in work somebody is already doing by hand, at their desk, this afternoon.

  • Routine tasks cover writing emails, drafting meeting notes, filling in forms, and translating between languages.
  • Summarising documents condenses reports and presentations, and gives a first read of legal documents or financial statements.
  • Content creation produces drafts, curates external content, standardises job descriptions, and analyses requests for proposal to prepare bids.
  • Code generation completes, analyses and debugs code, which quietly shortens the work of building everything else on this list.
  • Customer interaction support drafts responses and personal follow-ups, and runs the chatbots a customer meets before any person.
  • Strategic planning produces project plans, competitive analyses and idea generation, where the point is a starting draft rather than a verdict.

Four benefits sit underneath all of that: productivity and efficiency gains, innovation and ideation support, personalization at scale, and cost savings through automation. The first two are the obvious ones. Summarising a customer's file, or documenting a call automatically, gives back the time a person was spending on it.

Personalization at scale is the one worth pausing on. It means individual advice, recommendations or replies for every customer without massively increasing customer-facing staff. A garden retailer answering planting questions and a distributor answering substitution questions are the same idea, which is why the next decision is not academic.

The deployment ladder is a menu, not a ranking

Deciding a job is worth doing is the easy half. The second half is how the tool reaches your company, and that is a question of cost and control rather than a technical one. The chapter gives five deployment models. Cheapest first:

  • A public tool, as it comes is free or near it, opened in a browser, and whatever an employee types into it may become training data.
  • The same model inside your own application, reached through the provider's interface, puts the capability in your software but leaves the answers limited to the training data.
  • Retrieval over your own systems augments the prompt with relevant records fetched from your knowledge base, so the answer carries your facts and your live figures.
  • A model fine-tuned on your examples is customised with prompt-and-answer pairs for a domain or a house voice, and is expensive enough to redo only periodically.
  • A model of your own, from scratch gives maximum privacy and control, and is out of reach for all but the largest organizations under strict regulation.

The costs are real, and the subscription line is only the visible part of them. An enterprise subscription runs in the region of $25 per user per month, and usage through a programming interface is billed by the volume of text going in and coming out. Fine-tuning, or building a model internally, runs between $5 million and $20 million where the aim is to transform a business model.

Three further costs get left off the business case:

  • Time matters because a model with reasoning capabilities can take long enough to answer that it stops being usable in a live customer conversation.
  • Errors cost what it takes to detect them, what it takes to fix them, and again for the ones nobody detects at all.
  • The environment absorbs the training and running of these models as demand for energy and raw materials, and eventually as electronic waste.

Those costs are why one industry forecast, made in 2024, expected 30 percent of generative AI projects to be abandoned by the end of 2025 — largely because companies could not translate the use into a financial benefit they could point at.

Plausible is not accurate

Now back to the supervisor and the warm chilled product. The tool did not malfunction. It was asked what a returns policy says, it had never been shown Fairmount's returns policy, and it produced what a returns policy usually looks like. That is the system working as described.

A hallucination is an inaccurate or nonsensical response produced that way. It arrives in the same register as everything else — no hedge, no tell, no change of tone — which is exactly what makes it dangerous. People believe output that is coherent and plausible, and this output is reliably coherent and plausible.

Fabrications have shapes worth learning to recognise. In one page of company copy that nobody had checked, three sentences were invented:

  • A flattering statistic claimed an on-time delivery rate of 99.4% every quarter since 2019, precise enough that the decimal point did the persuading.
  • A regulation claimed a temperature-controlled logistics licence under a federal rule number that does not exist, renewed annually.
  • An award claimed distributor of the year from a trade body that gave no such prize, the sort of line a customer repeats in their own marketing.

The test is not whether a sentence sounds right. It is whether you can check it in thirty seconds against your own records. "We operate 40 depots" survives that test. The other three do not — and the company owns every sentence its tools send out under its name.

A generative model produces plausible text, and plausibility is not accuracy: a confident answer can be wholly invented, which is how these systems work rather than a defect that one product has fixed. Pick the rung the job needs — placement, facts, voice or privacy — because each one buys a different thing at a very different price.

Objective 7.3 · Part 2

Agents, oversight, and governing the use of it

Fairmount Provisions, the invented food and household goods distributor running through this module, logs 300 order exceptions a quarter: short deliveries, damaged cases, wrong substitutions. Two people read each one, look up the account, choose a remedy and write to the customer. It takes them most of a week. Software can now run that whole sequence unattended. The interesting question is not whether it can. It is where a person stands in it, and who answers when it goes wrong.

From a prompt to a goal

Most of the tools in the previous part return a one-shot response. You write a prompt, you get one answer, and then you do the next thing yourself. A generative AI agent is a system that autonomously completes a multi-step task from a goal. You describe the outcome you want. It works out the steps.

The chapter names the pieces that make that possible. Read them against the exception queue above and each one has an obvious job to do.

  • Memory holds what happened in past interactions, such as how three similar damaged-case claims were settled last month.
  • Planning and reasoning breaks the goal into steps and sequences them: read, look up, decide, draft, send.
  • Tools are the databases, the business software and the other models it reaches through their application programming interfaces, or APIs.
  • Execution is the part that does it, writing the email, sending it, and logging it against the account.

Four features separate an agent from a basic tool. The first two rows change what you hand over and what comes back. The last two change what your job is.

FeatureBasic toolAgent
InputA promptA goal or objective
OutputOne response, then it stopsA multi-step plan, and the actions
AutonomyNoneMedium to high
IntegrationRareCommon: APIs, tools, the web

Integration is what turns a wrong sentence into a wrong action. A tool that drafts a bad email costs you the two seconds it takes to delete it. An agent that sends one has already spent your money, under your letterhead, politely, in writing.

So designing an agent is less about writing prompts and more about mapping the process you already run, planning the integrations, testing and overseeing the result, and designing how a person interacts with it. The role shifts from prompt engineer to agent designer.

What the chapter says can go wrong

Oversight earns its money because of a specific list of failures rather than a general unease. The chapter names six of them, and because they fail in different ways they need different controls.

  • Inaccuracy and hallucination means output that is wrong or nonsensical, delivered in the same confident register as everything else.
  • Lack of transparency means the system is a black box, so nobody can say how or why a response was produced.
  • Bias and fairness follows from training data that leans heavily towards one part of the world and one language.
  • Confidentiality and data leakage happens when the prompts and responses of users are reused to improve the model itself.
  • Overreliance produces complacency and deskilling, because people stop checking a tool that is usually right.
  • Legal and ethical concerns cover copyright, plagiarism out of the training data, and disinformation spread by malicious actors.

Deployment, cost and governance

The deployment model you choose sets the ceiling on some of those risks. A public tool may feed your input back into training. A tool embedded through an API inside your own application stays limited to what it was trained on. Retrieval adds your own records to the prompt. Fine-tuning buys a voice and a domain, periodically, at real expense.

The chapter is blunt that none of this is free, and it counts four kinds of cost. Read them before approving anything, because the third one is what this whole section is about.

  • Time goes into training, prompting and waiting for output, which rules these tools out of some time-critical work.
  • Money covers subscriptions and metered API calls, and fine-tuning or an internal model runs into millions of dollars.
  • Errors cost you twice over: what it takes to find and fix them, and what it costs when nobody finds them.
  • Environment is paid in energy, raw materials and electronic waste as the computing demand behind these systems keeps climbing.

Governance is how an organization holds all of that to its own values and its own regulators. The chapter names four mechanisms, and each one answers a different risk from the list above.

  • Prompt engineering guidelines train staff, supply standard templates, and forbid confidential data in prompts to public tools.
  • Access controls limit which roles may use the tool, and audit usage for unusual patterns or policy breaches.
  • Human-in-the-loop review requires a person to approve output before it is used, above all in high-stakes situations.
  • Data and model governance tracks what data is used for what, and classifies it by whether it may be entered at all.

Access control has a known failure mode worth naming. Staff determined to use these tools will find a workaround, and the workaround is usually a free public tool, which raises the risk instead of lowering it.

A voice that is not who it claims to be

The same technology that drafts your emails also fabricates media. A deepfake is deep learning plus fake: software maps and learns facial features, swaps faces and audio, and turns real footage into something else entirely. Voices can be cloned and synthesised into saying whatever the faker wants said.

This is not theoretical. Attackers cloned the voice of a chief executive of a company in the United Kingdom and used it in a wire transfer scam that took about US$220,000.

Early fakes were easy to spot by their glitches. Modern ones set a generator against a discriminator until the discriminator can no longer tell, which means “it sounded real” has stopped being evidence of anything. So stop listening to the voice and read the shape of the request instead.

  • Urgency compresses the window until there is no time left to check anything, which is the entire point of it.
  • An unusual channel moves a payment decision onto a phone call when it normally lives in a written approval process.
  • An unverifiable reason explains away the step you would otherwise take, such as a missing laptop at an airport.
  • A request for secrecy keeps the one colleague who would recognise the scam out of the conversation entirely.

The defence costs nothing. Hang up, and ring back on a number you looked up yourself. The attacker controls the call they placed, not the call you place.

Who answers for it

One principle survives every deployment model in this chapter. A business answers for what its own systems tell its customers. A tribunal has already rejected the argument that a chatbot is a separate legal entity, and made the company honour a policy the chatbot had invented.

Read that alongside the reviewer table. Automation moved the work. It did not move the accountability, and it multiplied the rate at which one error can be repeated under your name.

Objective 7.4 · Part 1

Knowledge management: what leaves when people do

Fairmount Provisions, the hypothetical distributor these practice cases use, runs forty depots and employs 1,100 staff. Your longest-serving depot manager retires in five months. He carries thirty-one years of knowing which chef will accept a substitution, which one will cancel the standing order, and which one will tell every other kitchen in the district. None of that is in a database. None of it can be forecast by a model. This section is about what an organization can actually do before he walks out.

Data, information, and the assets in between

Recall the ladder from the first chapter. Data are raw, unformatted symbols such as characters and numbers. Information is data that has been formatted, organized or processed so that it is useful to people. Knowledge is what you need in order to understand the relationships between separate pieces of information.

Knowledge management is the set of processes an organization uses to gain the greatest value from its knowledge assets. There is no universal agreement on the term, and that is not your problem to fix. What matters is knowing what counts as an asset.

The chapter counts two different sorts of thing as an asset, the second broader than most students expect, and gives two reasons to care:

  • What people carry covers skills, routines, practices, principles, formulas, methods, heuristics and the intuitions built by doing a job for years.
  • The artifacts that record it covers databases, manuals, reference works, textbooks, diagrams, displays, computer files, proposals and plans.
  • Why a company bothers is that knowledge assets, properly used, improve its efficiency, its effectiveness and eventually its profitability.
  • Why the subject is urgent now is retirement: as a large generation of staff leaves, firms are using these systems to capture what goes with them.

The line between explicit and tacit

Explicit knowledge assets reflect knowledge that can be documented, archived and codified, often with the help of information systems. Much of what sits in a database management system is explicit.

Tacit knowledge assets are the processes and procedures located in a person's mind for performing a particular task well. They are often the organization's real best practices, meaning the procedures widely accepted as the most effective or most efficient way to do something. Identifying them and keeping them accurate and available to everyone remains a significant challenge.

Carry one sentence out of this section. A knowledge management system holds an explicit asset as a record, and handles a tacit one mostly by connecting people. It can hold a torque setting of 42 newton metres outright. For the chef, what it holds is a recording, a mentoring pairing, and a note of whose afternoon to interrupt.

The system is a collection of tools, not a product

A knowledge management system is typically not a single technology. It is a collection of technology-based tools assembled so that tacit and explicit assets can be generated, stored, shared and managed.

Two families of tool do that work, and a system missing either one fails in a way you can predict from here:

  • Communication technologies such as email, corporate intranets, collaboration software and instant messaging are how knowledge moves between people.
  • Storage and retrieval systems such as wikis and database management systems are where knowledge waits while nobody is asking for it.

The benefits the chapter claims are ordinary business ones: enhanced innovation and creativity, improved customer service, shorter product development, streamlined operations, enhanced employee retention and improved organizational performance. Retention belongs on that list for a reason. People leave jobs where the same problem has to be solved from scratch every time.

The four challenges will be more useful to you than the benefits, because you meet them roughly in this order:

  • Getting employee buy-in comes first, because sharing costs the contributor an afternoon and pays somebody else, so participation has to be valued and rewarded.
  • Focusing too much on technology means choosing a tool before deciding what knowledge is needed, why it is needed and who is likely to have it.
  • Forgetting the goal means a deployment tied to no specific business objective, which drifts into a filing exercise nobody will fund twice.
  • Overload and obsolescence is the late one: knowledge erodes like any other asset, so updating, amending and removing have to be continuous.

One requirement runs underneath all four. The system has to be easy to use for retrieving knowledge, not only for entering it. A base that is pleasant to write into and painful to search gets written into once, and then falls into disarray.

Who actually holds what

The people using the system sit in different departments, do different jobs, and are spread around a building, a city or the world. The chapter calls each person or group an island, set apart by things like geography, job focus, expertise and age. Someone on one island is usually working on a problem someone on another island already solved. Finding that person is the hard part.

Social network analysis maps people's contacts to discover connections, and to expose the missing links sometimes called structural holes. It finds the groups who work together, the people who do not collaborate but should, and the experts on a subject. An organization chart shows none of this, because it records who reports to whom rather than who talks to whom.

Two different people fall out of that map, and telling them apart is the whole exercise:

  • The connector holds the most working relationships, which makes that person the fastest route into a group and a single point of failure inside it.
  • The bridge may hold fewer relationships but is the only one with ties on both sides of a structural hole, so the hole opens when that person leaves.

Lose the connector and a group slows down while it re-routes. Lose the bridge and two halves of a company that were already solving the same problem twice stop hearing about each other at all. The two need not be the same person, which is why this gets measured rather than guessed.

Collecting knowledge into a repository is only half the job; it then has to reach people. Knowledge portals are customized web front ends onto that repository, and the audience decides the plumbing: an intranet for employees, an extranet for suppliers, the open internet for the public. A national food and drug regulator publishing recall notices and drug trial status runs exactly that kind of portal.

Geographic information systems

Fairmount Provisions, the invented distributor you have been running through this module, has approved a forty-first depot and narrowed the choice to three sites. The property team sent the district map with every layer switched off. That is the honest way to send one, and it is also the dangerous way: a site looks excellent until the layer that disqualifies it is turned on, and nobody turns on a layer they have not thought of.

Location as a dimension of analysis

A geographic information system (GIS) is a system for creating, storing, analyzing, and managing geographically referenced data. It does not replace the reporting and the models from earlier in this chapter. It adds a spatial dimension to them, so that a characteristic of a place can be coupled with your own records and queried alongside them. The chapter also calls this location analytics, or location intelligence.

The coupling is the whole idea. A GIS can link the square footage of a commercial property to its exact latitude and longitude, then pair that with population density, average incomes, travel accessibility such as highways and major thoroughfares, or proximity to services such as fire, police, and public transportation stops.

You already use one. When you search for a restaurant near you, the map or satellite image is geographic data, and the name, address, opening hours, and reviews are attribute data about that same point.

Most of the questions a distributor asks have a spatial dimension hiding inside them:

  • Where the customers are is answered by geocoding survey answers or captured postcodes into coordinates and adding them to the map as a layer.
  • Where the next depot goes is a siting question, and a GIS used to model one is working as a spatial decision support system.
  • Which areas go in the next mailing turns on which districts are underserved, not on how many names sit on the list.
  • How far a buyer will travel is a question about drive time rather than distance, and the two disagree badly wherever a river does.

Layers, and the one nobody switched on

A GIS hands you a blank map of an area. You then add data stored in layers, each one resembling a transparency holding a single kind of information about the same ground. Adding and removing layers is how a question with a spatial dimension actually gets answered.

The five layers that came with the depot map are ordinary ones, and each can decide the case on its own:

  • Population density shades each district by how many people live on each square kilometer of it.
  • Arterial road access draws the main roads, because a depot a loaded truck cannot leave at six in the morning is not a depot.
  • Twenty-minute drive time rings each candidate with the ground a truck can actually reach inside twenty minutes.
  • Competitor depots marks the rivals already serving that ground, which is how you learn that a promising ring is somebody else's.
  • Floodplain outlines the ground that goes under water, and in this case it is the layer that quietly decides everything.

Those rings have a name. Trade area analysis combines location information with drive time to work out where customers can come from and in how long, whether an area is underserved, and whether two of your own sites overlap so far that one is eating the other.

Quantities against densities

Another way to show geospatial data is a thematic map, which color-codes data aggregated for specific geographic regions: median household income by block, average household size, or the blocks where car theft happens most often.

This is also where a thematic map will mislead you. There is a difference between a quantity and a density. A quantity is how many orders a district placed. A density is how many orders it placed per square kilometer of itself.

Last year's Fairmount order counts make the gap concrete. Postcode 47 placed 2,980 orders, more than any other district on the map. Postcode 52 placed 860, the fewest of the four. Now divide by the ground each one covers: 47 sprawls over 149 square kilometers and works out at 20.0 orders per square kilometer, the thinnest of the four; 52 is packed into 8 and works out at 107.5.

A count mapped raw mostly shows you where the people are. The largest district wins on quantity and can still be the emptiest ground on the map, which is how a delivery round ends up covering the most kilometers to reach the fewest customers. A rate — per square kilometer, or per head — is usually the honest layer.

What the map will not tell you

A map makes a pattern visible. It does not explain it. A cluster of cancelled orders on the east side is a fact about where the cancellations are. The reason could be a competitor's new price list, a closed bridge, or one driver who keeps arriving after the kitchen has started service. The map narrows the question to a few streets. Somebody still has to go and answer it.

Three further uses are worth knowing, because all three are already in the field:

  • Location data from phones and sensors supports analysis that was not possible before, such as estimating the traffic on a road from how the devices moving along it behave.
  • Map-based communication points the same output outward, in a store locator or a map of where an expansion is going, for people who will never open the analytics tool.

  • Independent grocers per square kilometer inside each depot's territory — Per square kilometer is the tell: the count has already been divided by the ground it covers.
  • Chilled cases sold per square kilometer across the northern districts — Sales divided by area is a density, and it will point at different districts than the raw sales figure does.

Change — The same measure on the same ground, before and after something happened.

  • Deliveries into the north side before and after depot 41 opened — The same measure over the same ground at two moments is a change reading, not a single snapshot.
  • How far the twenty-minute drive-time ring shrank after the bridge closed — Comparing the trade area before the closure with the one after it is a before-and-after reading of the map.

Switch on every layer that could disqualify a site before you choose one, and divide before you color a map. A layer you never turned on cannot rule anything out, and a raw count spread across unequal ground will point you at the largest district rather than the busiest one.

Decision systems through the five forces and the value chain

You can now say what descriptive, diagnostic, predictive and prescriptive analytics do, and what a model, an agent, a knowledge system and a map of layers are for. That is a description. A manager is paid for the step after it: naming the pressure the firm is actually under, saying which decision system relieves it, and admitting what no system can do.

Application supplement. This section applies two frameworks you met earlier in the course to this chapter’s material. It is not one of the chapter’s own objectives and it introduces no new framework. Fairmount Provisions — a regional food and household goods distributor with 40 depots and 1,100 staff, selling to independent grocers, school kitchens and restaurants — is hypothetical, and every figure used for practice here is invented.

The two frameworks, in the course’s words

The five forces describe the pressure on a firm’s profits from outside the firm. A force is not good or bad. It is strong or weak, and your job is to say which, and on what evidence.

  • Rivalry among existing competitors is the contest among sellers already inside your industry, showing up in price, distribution and service.
  • Threat of new entrants is the chance that firms not competing with you today will start tomorrow, bringing added capacity and lower prices.
  • Threat of substitutes is a different kind of product or service meeting the same underlying need, usually from an industry you do not monitor.
  • Bargaining power of buyers is your customers’ ability to push back on price, quality and terms, through concentration or the ease of leaving.
  • Bargaining power of suppliers is the ability of whoever supplies an input to set the terms, and it reaches you as cost rather than as lost sales.

The value chain asks a different question. Not what presses on the firm, but where inside it the work would land. Five primary activities carry the product, over four support bands that carry the firm.

  • Inbound logistics, operations and outbound logistics are the spine: receiving inputs, turning them into the product, and delivering the result.
  • Sales and marketing, and service are the customer-facing pair, covering the reason to buy and everything that happens after the money moves.
  • Procurement and technology development are two of the four supports: the buying decision itself, and building the systems the rest of the chain runs on.
  • Human resources, and administration and firm infrastructure are the other two: who is hired, trained and rostered, and the planning, finance and general management that belong to no single product.

Nine activities, then, and a recommendation that does not land on one of them is a recommendation nobody can act on.

Two confusions worth settling now

The first is substitutes against new entrants. Look at what the customer ends up with. If they bought the same thing from somebody new, that is entry. If they stopped buying it at all and met the need another way, that is a substitute.

Whether the seller owns a warehouse or a fleet is not the test. A wholesaler renting racking and third-party couriers by the month is still an entrant, because a grocer ends up with the same case packs. A caterer plating meals for a school district is a substitute, because that district now orders no ingredients from anybody.

The second confusion is about suppliers. A supplier is not only a firm that sends invoices. Anything the work depends on is an input, and that includes people.

  • Skilled people leaving is supplier power when the rate for a replacement rises, because labor is an input the firm has to buy.
  • A sole approved manufacturer holds the same kind of power when it raises its minimum drop size and moves you from weekly delivery to every second week.
  • Buying nobody can see whole hands power away by default, because a firm that cannot state what 40 depots purchase in total never uses the leverage it already has.

Calling the first of those a human-resources problem is the common mistake. Human resources is the support band where you do something about it, through pay, rostering and mentoring. Supplier power is what it is: an input whose price is set by somebody else.

The chapter’s own worry sits underneath. When the depot manager with 31 years of it retires, the tacit knowledge leaves along with the labor, and the chapter is blunt that recognizing, storing and sharing exactly that tacit part is a knowledge management system’s primary objective, and a standing challenge.

What a decision system changes, and what it does not

Each family of system in this chapter lands on particular activities, and reaches a force through them.

  • Business intelligence and dashboards land on administration and firm infrastructure, because what they change is how fast ordinary decisions get made.
  • Forecasting and prescriptive models land on the spine, where chilled stock is held, vans are loaded and a week-31 shortage either happens or does not.
  • Generative tools and agents land mostly on sales and marketing and on service, since drafting and answering customers is where the work is.
  • Knowledge management lands on human resources and technology development, because best practice has to be captured, kept current and put where the next person will find it.
  • A geographic information system adds location as a dimension, so it lands where distance costs money: outbound logistics, and the planning that chooses a depot site.

Now the limit, and it is the part a board mishears. A decision system that works improves this firm’s decisions inside the industry it is already in. It does not by itself change that industry’s structure. Ship the week-31 forecast and the region still holds the same distributors, entry is as cheap as it was, and the caterer is doing whatever it was doing before.

The software, meanwhile, is for sale to everybody. The course’s test of rivalry asks whether anything in your offer is hard for a rival to imitate, and a package on general sale is the easiest thing of all to copy: a rival buys the same forecasting package next quarter and stands where you stand.

The chapter says as much in its own words — having the right tools is not sufficient, because it takes data literacy and human judgment to turn an insight into a decision. So test a claimed advantage by asking what a rival would actually need to reproduce it.

  • The data comes first: 2,400 deliveries somebody labeled against an agreed meaning of “late”, which is slower work than making the deliveries.
  • The process comes second, because a forecast nobody loads the vans from is a number on a screen rather than a decision.
  • The people come third: dispatchers who know which chef will accept a substitution hold the tacit part a rival cannot license.

An advantage that survives all three questions is worth promising to a board. Anything that does not is a purchase, and should be argued as one.

Objective 7.6 · Application 7.6

Recommend one analytics initiative

Nobody approves analytics. A manager approves a change to a decision that is currently going wrong, at a price that recurs every year, with a date on which somebody says whether it worked. This closing application turns the whole chapter into one recommendation you could defend in a meeting.

Application supplement. Fairmount Provisions and every figure below are hypothetical and invented for practice. The section applies Chapter 7's vocabulary and ordinary cost reasoning to a decision a manager really faces; it is not one of the chapter's own objectives.

Name the decision, not the software

"We need a forecasting platform" is a purchase, not a problem. A problem statement names the decision that goes wrong, who makes it, how often, and what one failure costs. Written that way, the same situation reads: the weekly chilled replenishment order for the twelve East depots ran short 96 times in 624 orders last year, at about $1,150 each in emergency transfers and credits.

That sentence does real work. It names a decision that repeats, which is the chapter's own test for a machine learning candidate: repetitive, and settled by data rather than judgement. It can be checked, because anyone may go and count the 96. And it prices the problem at roughly $110,400 a year, which is the yearly loss every cost in your proposal gets weighed against.

Say which of the four questions you are asking

Next, say what the system would actually have to produce, and put that on the decision-making continuum. The four kinds are not four brands of one thing. Three end at a person who still has to decide; only the fourth reaches the action, and only sometimes.

  • Descriptive summarises what already happened, and it is where every other kind of analysis has to start.
  • Diagnostic looks for correlations or causes in past data, so a mistake is not repeated or a success is.
  • Predictive forecasts a likely future outcome, and a person still decides what to do about the forecast.
  • Prescriptive recommends the best course of action, and at its far end takes the human out of the loop.

Then write your own sentence out in full: predict the pallets of chilled stock each East depot will sell next week, so the buyer's Thursday order is placed against a forecast rather than last week's figure. That is predictive analytics feeding a human decision. Say so plainly. A board that believes it approved automation and received a forecast will feel misled by month three.

The trap: accurate and useless

Here is the failure that survives a workflow followed stage by stage to the letter. A model can be measured honestly, on records it has never seen, report a fine accuracy, and still be worth nothing, because nobody does anything differently when it is right.

So ask the question out loud. Who reads this number, on what day, and what do they do differently because of it? If sales already telephones every account on the churn list, the score changes no action at all, and the running cost has bought a tile on a dashboard. A prediction with no decision attached is a hobby.

Price what recurs, not what you sign

The quoted licence is the smallest number in the file and the only one anybody remembers afterwards. A model is not bought once. It is kept, fed and repaired.

A three-year figure a finance committee can trust adds up at least these lines:

  • Data you do not already hold is paid for in every year it is used, and an hourly regional weather feed is bought again in each of them.
  • Acquiring and cleaning often takes months, and organizations are regularly surprised by how much of the data turns out to be unusable.
  • Labelling pays for the ground truth a supervised model learns from, and for checking that those labels are actually right.
  • Retraining and monitoring pays for the years after launch, when performance is re-evaluated and the model is refitted as conditions change.
  • Changing the work pays to alter the process, retrain the buyers, and put the forecast where the order is actually placed.

That last line is where analytics projects die quietly. A forecast emailed at six in the evening to a buyer who places the order at two in the afternoon is a correct answer delivered too late to be used.

Say what would make it fail

Write the risks into the paper before somebody else writes them down for you. The list is short and unglamorous, and every item on it is cheaper to prevent than to explain:

  • A threshold set afterwards lets the bar slide down to whatever the model happened to score, so the test proves nothing.
  • Accuracy measured on training rows reports a wonderful number that says nothing about the weeks the model has never seen.
  • Inherited bias means the model learns what past decisions contained rather than what was right, and then repeats it at scale.
  • A prediction read as a fact hides the error rate, and every error left in it reaches somebody under your company's name.
  • Oversight left unpriced hides the cheapest question in the proposal, which is what a reviewer costs against the errors that reviewer catches.

Price that last one in the same paragraph. Where an agent picks the customer remedy, a reviewer standing at that step costs about $18,000 a year and catches nine wrong remedies in ten, against errors that cost $2,400 apiece. Written that way, the argument is arithmetic rather than temperament.

  • The model's accuracy on the held-back weeks, reported to the board each month. — Accuracy describes the model, not the shortfalls. A forecast can be right every week while the order is still placed the old way.
  • The East buyers will tell us in the spring whether the forecast has helped them. — Impressions run high after training, and they cannot separate a real gain from a mild summer and a quiet quarter.
  • Shortfalls per 100 weekly chilled orders, from 15.4 to under 6 by week 26. — the stronger call. Baseline, denominator, target and date, all fixed before the build, so the review can honestly go either way.

Fix the evidence before the work starts

Pick the measure now, while you can still be wrong in public. Fix the baseline: 15.4 shortfalls per 100 weekly chilled orders. Fix the denominator, so a mild summer is not mistaken for improvement. Fix the target and the date: under 6 per 100, reviewed twenty-six weeks after the forecast reaches the buyers.

Set the performance bar in the same breath, and set it before you have seen a result. A dispatch team that refuses a lateness tool below 85% accuracy has done this correctly. Name your buyers' bar while the number is still hypothetical, then name the guardrails, because a shortfall figure that falls while chilled write-offs double has only moved the loss.

Last, write the decision rule. If the number has not moved by week twenty-six, what happens: retrain, revert to the old order sheet, or stop? A project with no stopping condition does not end. It only gets quieter.

Lesson and answer summary — the interactive version of this activity loads when scripting is available.

  • I name a decision that is going wrong, and how often it goes wrong. Reread your first sentence. If it names a tool before it names a repeated decision, write it again with a count.
  • I say which of the four kinds of analytics this initiative is. Descriptive says what happened, diagnostic why, predictive what will happen, prescriptive what to do about it.
  • I name the person who acts differently, on what day, because of the output. A model nobody acts on is worth nothing however accurate it is. Name the buyer, the shift and the order it changes.
  • I give a build cost and the running cost that follows it every year. Add the data you buy each year, the cleaning and labelling, the retraining and monitoring, and the change to the work.
  • I set the performance threshold before anyone has seen a result. Write down the accuracy below which your users will keep doing it by eye, while that number is still hypothetical.
  • I say how the model was tested, and on which records. Accuracy measured on the rows the model learned from is not evidence. Quote the score on data it has never seen.

7.1data-driven organization

Looking inside past data for the correlations or causes that explain why something happened.For example: An analyst finds that Fairmount's week-31 shortage tracks school terms restarting rather than a price change.

Forecasting a likely future outcome from patterns in past data.For example: A model estimates how many pallets of chilled stock each Fairmount east depot will sell next week.

Weighing the possible scenarios and recommending the best course of action, and at its far end taking that action itself.For example: A system tells the Fairmount night shift which depot to move forty pallets to, and why.

Handing a person a recommended course of action that they can accept, amend or refuse.For example: A ranked list of at-risk orders reaches a Fairmount planner, who decides which ones to chase.
7.1decision automation

Keeping data and business processes under constant review so that the plan itself is revised during the year rather than only at the start of it.For example: Fairmount's week-31 staging figure would have been corrected in June instead of defended in August.

The range running from decisions whose procedure can be written down in advance to decisions that have no procedure at all.For example: Ordering Tuesday's milk, staging a peak week, and deciding whether to serve school kitchens sit at three different points on it.
7.1structured decision
A decision whose procedure can be specified before the situation arrives, so it can be programmed and made with little human involvement.For example: A Fairmount depot reorders chilled milk when the case count falls below its reorder point.
7.1semistructured decision
A decision whose procedure is partly known, but not far enough to yield a specific recommendation, so judgement and expertise finish the job.For example: A Fairmount planner decides how many extra pallets to stage in the east for a peak week.
7.1unstructured decision
A decision with few or no procedures that can be written down in advance, because the problem is complex, nonroutine and reaches the whole organization.For example: The Fairmount board decides whether to keep serving school kitchens at all.
7.1organizational levels
The operational, managerial and executive tiers a decision can sit at, which loosely track how much of its procedure can be written down.For example: A reorder rule stays with a Fairmount depot supervisor, a peak-week staging call with a planner, and leaving a market with the board.
7.1data literacy
The ability to organize, analyze and communicate with data, which every business user needs rather than only the analysts.For example: A Fairmount depot manager reads a forecast, understands what its error rate means, and loads vans accordingly.
7.2business intelligence
Tools and techniques for analyzing and visualizing past data so that managers can see what has already happened.For example: Fairmount's monthly revenue and margin reports by region are the plain case of it.
7.2decision support system
An interactive aid that uses models to manipulate structured data so a manager can explore a decision before making it.For example: A Fairmount screen recalculates courier margin as the manager changes the number of drops.
7.2what-if analysis
Changing one input to a model and watching how the result moves while everything else holds still.For example: Raising the cost of making one drop shows Fairmount what happens to the courier contract's margin.
7.2sensitivity analysis
Weighing each possible input value by how likely it is to occur, so that probability becomes part of the answer.For example: A one-in-four chance of a fuel rise counts for less in the Fairmount courier model than a three-in-four chance.
7.2goal seeking
Fixing the end result you want and working backwards to the input value that would produce it.For example: Fairmount asks how many drops the contract needs in order to reach the margin the board demanded.
7.2optimisation
Finding the best balance between several parameters inside the constraints the business is genuinely operating under.For example: A run picks the drop volume and price that earn Fairmount the most without exceeding its fleet hours.
7.2online analytical processing
Quickly running complex analyses across several dimensions of data at once, against a database built for retrieval rather than for recording transactions.For example: A Fairmount manager asks for profit by week, by region and by customer type, and gets it while the meeting is still running.
7.2measure
The value being analysed, sometimes called a fact.For example: Revenue and gross margin are the two Fairmount carries in its sales cube.
7.2dimension
A way of summarising a measure, such as by place, by time or by product.For example: Fairmount organises its sales by product line, by region and by quarter.
7.2cube
Data arranged so that measures can be read across several dimensions at once, instead of being flattened into a two-way table.For example: Fairmount's holds four product lines, four regions and four quarters.
7.2roll up
Including every member of a dimension so that the dimension disappears into a single total.For example: Four quarters of Fairmount chilled sales become one full-year figure.
7.2drill down
Putting a dimension back on an axis so that its members show separately.For example: A Fairmount region's chilled total opens up into the individual depots inside it.
7.2slice
Fixing one dimension to a single member, so that everything on the screen belongs to that member.For example: Every figure shown is chilled goods, with Fairmount's other product lines set aside.
7.2dice
Narrowing several dimensions at once to pull a smaller block out of the middle of a cube.For example: Two Fairmount product lines, two regions and two quarters leave eight cells to read.
7.2visualization
Showing complex data relationships graphically so that a person grasps the result of an analysis quickly.For example: A shaded map of Fairmount's late deliveries reads faster than the same figures in a table.
7.2digital dashboard
A screen of key performance indicators and summary information, built for the one person who has to decide something.For example: Fairmount's operations executive reads six tiles in the thirty seconds before a call.
7.2push reporting
Sending a summary to a decision maker whether or not they asked for it.For example: Fairmount's on-time delivery summary lands in the executive's inbox every Monday.
7.2exception reporting
Flagging only what needs attention right now, usually by colouring the item that has gone wrong.For example: A Fairmount depot turns red on the screen when its short-order count passes the limit.
7.2pull reporting
Letting a decision maker drill into an aggregate or run their own query without filing a request with an analyst.For example: The executive opens Fairmount's fleet on-time rate and finds the two depots dragging it down.
7.2machine learning
Letting computers learn patterns from data instead of being explicitly programmed, and improve as they are exposed to more of it.For example: A Fairmount model learns which deliveries arrive late from thousands of past ones.
7.2supervised learning
Training on examples that already carry the right answer, so the system can classify or predict a case it has never seen.For example: Fairmount deliveries already marked late or on time teach a model to flag the next one.
7.2unsupervised learning
Handing an algorithm data with no answers attached and asking it to find structure in it.For example: A run on Fairmount's customer list returns three groups and calls them 0, 1 and 2.
7.2clustering
Grouping records that have similar values for their attributes.For example: Fairmount's customers fall into small kitchens topping up almost daily, distant bulk buyers, and the twice-weekly core trade.
7.2anomaly detection
Hunting for the records that resemble nothing else in the set.For example: A Fairmount fuel card file is searched for the few transactions that fit no normal pattern.
7.2association rule
A statement that two items turn up on the same transaction, carried by two numbers rather than one.For example: Coffee and sugar appear together often enough on Fairmount restaurant orders to be worth a shelf change.
7.2support (of an association rule)
How often the combination shows up across all the transactions analysed, which tells you whether the rule is big enough to bother with.For example: Eight of twenty Fairmount restaurant orders hold both coffee and sugar, so it is forty percent.
7.2confidence (of an association rule)
The share of the transactions holding the first item that also hold the second, which tells you how reliable the rule is.For example: Eight of the ten Fairmount orders containing coffee also contain sugar, so it is eighty percent.
7.2ground truth
The agreed right answer attached to each training example, from which everything the model learns is learned.For example: At Fairmount, late means the van arrived after the promised window closed, by the same clock in every depot.
7.2training data
The records a model is fitted to, whose patterns and whose inequities it will reproduce.For example: Twelve years of Fairmount promotion records would teach a model who was promoted before, not who deserved it.
7.2held-back test
Measuring a model on records kept out of training, so that the score estimates how it will do on cases it has never seen.For example: Fairmount trained on 1,800 deliveries and kept 600 back to check the result.
7.3generative AI
Artificial intelligence that creates new content such as text, images or audio, based on the data it was trained on.For example: A draft reply to a Fairmount customer's complaint appears in two seconds.
7.3narrow AI
A model tied to one task and one set of source data, which will not transfer to a different business.For example: A model that predicts late deliveries for Fairmount's vans is useless to a passenger railway.
7.3large language model
The text version of generative AI: fed enormous amounts of writing, it works out which words are likely to come next and then does the same thing again.For example: Asked for a returns policy it has never been shown, it produces the words a returns policy usually contains.
7.3prompt
The question or instruction a person types, which the system analyses before producing its answer.For example: A Fairmount supervisor types the customer's complaint and asks for a reply.
7.3hallucination
An inaccurate or nonsensical answer delivered in exactly the same confident register as a correct one.For example: A tool invents a thirty-day Fairmount returns window when the real policy is seventy-two hours with a photograph.
7.3deployment model
How a generative tool actually reaches a company, from a free public site up to a model built in-house, with each rung buying something different.For example: Fairmount picks one rung for drafting friendly emails and a different one for quoting its own returns policy.
7.3retrieval over your own systems
Adding relevant records from the company's own knowledge base to the prompt, so the answer carries its facts instead of generic ones.For example: The returns policy Fairmount revised this morning is fetched and put in front of the model.
7.3fine-tuning
Customising a model with example prompts and the answers wanted, to hold a domain or a house voice steady across many items.For example: Four thousand Fairmount product descriptions come back in one consistent voice.
7.3generative AI agent
A system that takes a goal rather than a single question, works out the steps itself, and then carries them out.For example: Told to settle a Fairmount order exception, it reads the case, looks up the account, picks a remedy and sends the email.
7.3autonomy
How far a system acts without a person, which is what turns a wrong sentence into a wrong action.For example: A drafted Fairmount credit note costs nothing to delete; a sent one has already left the company.
7.3human-in-the-loop review
Requiring a person to approve output before it is used, above all where the stakes are high.For example: A Fairmount reviewer checks the remedy the system chose before any credit reaches a customer.
7.3governance
The rules, controls and records by which an organization holds its use of these tools to its own values and its regulators' requirements.For example: Fairmount writes down which data may be typed into which tool, and which roles may use one at all.
7.3deepfake
Synthetic video or audio built by mapping and swapping a real person's face or voice onto something they never did or said.For example: A cloned voice on the phone tells a Fairmount clerk to wire a payment urgently and tell nobody.
7.4knowledge management
The set of processes an organization uses to get the greatest value out of what its people and its records know.For example: Fairmount records how its retiring depot manager handles substitutions before he walks out.
7.4knowledge management system
Not a single product but a collection of tools assembled so that knowledge can be generated, stored, shared and kept current.For example: Fairmount runs an intranet, a wiki and a mentoring roster together as one arrangement.
7.4explicit knowledge assets
What can be documented, archived and codified, and therefore stored in a system.For example: The torque setting for a Fairmount dock lift is forty-two newton metres, and that is the whole of it.
7.4tacit knowledge assets
What sits in a person's mind about performing a task well, which a system can point at but never hold.For example: Knowing which chef will accept a substitution and which one will cancel the standing order.
7.4best practices
The procedures widely accepted as the most effective or most efficient way to do something, often held tacitly by whoever is best at them.For example: Fairmount's longest-serving manager always phones a kitchen ahead of a short delivery, and nobody wrote that down.
7.4social network analysis
Mapping who actually works with whom, rather than who reports to whom, to find the experts and the gaps.For example: Two Fairmount depots turn out to be solving the same routing problem without ever speaking.
7.4structural holes
The missing links between groups that should be connected and are not.For example: Fairmount's buying team and its night dispatchers share no working relationship at all.
7.4knowledge portals
Customised web front ends onto a knowledge repository, where the audience decides whether it sits on an intranet, an extranet or the open internet.For example: Fairmount suppliers reach labelling rules through one door and staff reach rosters through another.
7.4geographic information system
A system for creating, storing, analyzing and managing data tied to places, which adds location as a dimension of any analysis.For example: Fairmount compares three candidate depot sites on the same district map.
7.4layer
One transparency over a map, holding a single kind of information about the same piece of ground.For example: Floodplain, arterial roads and competitor depots each sit on their own, and one of them disqualifies a Fairmount site.
7.4geocoding
Turning addresses or postcodes into coordinates so that they can be placed on a map.For example: Fairmount's customer list becomes a scatter of points across the district.
7.4thematic map
A map that colour-codes a value aggregated for each geographic region.For example: Fairmount shades each postcode by how many orders it placed last year.
7.4quantity and density
A raw count of something, against that same count divided by the ground it is spread over.For example: The Fairmount postcode with the most orders covers so much ground that it is also the emptiest to drive.

1. 7.5 A grocery chain stops buying delivered stock and opens its own regional depot, chilling and distributing for itself. Which force is that, and why?

  • A. Supplier power, because the chain now competes for the same warehouse staff. — Hiring pressure is a real consequence, but the question asks what the chain has done to your market rather than to your payroll.
  • B. Rivalry, because another depot is now operating in the same region as yours. — Rivalry counts firms selling distribution to customers; this chain serves only itself and sells the service to nobody.
  • C. New entrants, because a firm that was not distributing before is doing it now. — A new entrant would sell distribution to other buyers as you do, competing for the same customers you quote for.
  • D. Substitutes, because the customer has stopped buying distribution altogether. — correct. Look at what the customer ends up with: not your service bought elsewhere, but the need met another way entirely.

2. 7.5 Two of Fairmount's route planners leave for a rival and the rate for replacements rises sharply. Where does that belong, and what does it cost the firm?

  • A. Supplier power, because the skills a firm buys are an input like any other. — correct. Whoever supplies an input decides how that relationship runs, and skilled people are an input the firm has to keep buying.
  • B. A human resources problem, which sits outside the five forces altogether. — Calling it a staffing matter files it where nobody analyses it; the frameworks treat labour as a supply question.
  • C. Rivalry, because the rival that hired them is competing on the same routes. — The rival hiring them may well be a rival, but the pressure described here is the rising price of an input.
  • D. New entrants, because the planners could set up a planning service themselves. — Nothing in the situation says the planners intend to sell planning to anybody; they took jobs at an existing firm.

3. 7.6 A proposal quotes the yearly software fee for a demand forecasting tool and nothing else. What has been left out of the figure?

  • A. The savings, which cannot be estimated before the tool has run for a year. — A proposal that estimates no benefit at all cannot be judged, but the question asks what is missing from the cost.
  • B. The discount, which a vendor will usually offer once a competitor is named. — Negotiating the price down changes one line rather than revealing the lines the paper never listed.
  • C. The hardware, which is the largest item in any analytics project's budget. — Naming hardware as always the largest item asserts a ranking this situation gives no figures to support.
  • D. The labelling, the cleaning, and the people who keep the data fit to use. — correct. A forecast runs on data somebody prepares and keeps current, and that recurring work is the cost nobody quotes.

4. 7.6 A recommendation promises that a new model will “improve stock availability”. What turns that into something checkable a year later?

  • A. A measurement taken now, a target figure, and the date it is judged. — correct. Without a figure recorded before the work starts there is nothing the later measurement can be compared against.
  • B. A clause obliging the supplier to confirm the improvement in writing. — A supplier confirming its own work is not independent evidence about what changed in the depots.
  • C. A monthly report circulated to everyone who touches the ordering process. — Circulating a report says who reads it, not what counts as the improvement having happened.
  • D. A survey of depot managers asking whether availability now feels better. — Asking whether something feels better records an impression that no one can check against the earlier position.

5. 7.1 A Fairmount Provisions depot wants a system that places the packaging-film reorder itself each night, with no buyer seeing it first. Which kind of analytics is that?

  • A. Prescriptive, because the system takes the course of action itself. — correct. Prescriptive analytics reaches the action, and here it takes that action with nobody in the loop.
  • B. Predictive, because the reorder rests on a forecast of next week's use. — A forecast of next week's use feeds this, but what was asked for is the order rather than the number.
  • C. Descriptive, because the nightly figure summarises what the depot used. — Summarising past use is an input to the reorder and not the nightly order the depot asked for.
  • D. Diagnostic, because the reorder corrects a shortage that already happened. — Explaining a past shortage is diagnostic work, and this system is being asked to buy film tonight.

6. 7.1 A bakery asks why its two busiest outlets both lost morning trade during the same fortnight. Which kind of analysis is that?

  • A. Descriptive, because the fortnight has closed and the totals are already known. — The totals are known and are quoted in the request; what is wanted is the reason behind them.
  • B. Predictive, because morning trade next month is what the owners really care about. — Next month is not what was asked about, and no forecast is needed to account for a fortnight that closed.
  • C. Prescriptive, because the answer will be used to change the opening hours. — A change of hours may follow later, but this request stops at explaining what already happened.
  • D. Diagnostic, because the question is about a relationship inside past data. — correct. Accounting for an event that has closed means looking for the correlation or cause inside past data.

7. 7.1 A manager claims that buying predictive analytics will take the planner's judgement out of the stock decision. What is wrong with the claim?

  • A. Predictive work describes a period that has closed, so it says nothing about a future order. — Predictive analytics forecasts a future outcome, so this sentence describes descriptive work instead.
  • B. Removing the judgement is the aim, though a second model is needed to finish the job. — Reaching the action takes prescriptive work, and the manager has not asked for that here.
  • C. A forecast states a likely outcome, and a person still decides what to do with it. — correct. Human input remains essential for determining the best course of action, whatever the forecast says.
  • D. The judgement moves to the depot supervisor, who would place the order instead. — Moving the decision to another person does not remove judgement from the process at all.

8. 7.1 An executive orders printer paper by applying the same reorder threshold the stores clerk uses. Where does that decision sit on the continuum?

  • A. Structured, because the procedure exists beforehand and the answer can be checked. — correct. The reorder threshold is written down before the situation arrives and the shelf checks it afterwards.
  • B. Unstructured, because a decision that reaches an executive is by definition unstructured. — The continuum measures the decision rather than the rank of whoever happens to make it.
  • C. Semistructured, because an executive weighs more factors than a stores clerk would. — Applying the same threshold is the same procedure, whatever else the executive knows.
  • D. It cannot be classified, because two levels of the firm make the same call. — One decision can be made at two levels, and that does not stop it having a shape.

9. 7.1 A family haulier must decide whether to start working a neighbouring county at all. What can an analytics team honestly offer that decision?

  • A. A forecast of the loads available there, which would settle whether to enter. — A forecast sizes the opportunity, and it does not decide whether the firm should be there.
  • B. A rule that books the first loads automatically once the county is entered. — Automation suits a decision whose procedure is known, and no procedure covers entering a market.
  • C. A ranked list of the hauliers there, which decides it on market position. — A ranking informs the argument, yet nothing converts that ranking into the decision itself.
  • D. Assembled evidence on which the owners still have to argue the decision out. — correct. Few or no procedures can be specified in advance, so a system informs the argument and no more.

10. 7.1 A water utility counts overdue repairs weekly and works out how far they run behind target, then files the figure. The annual repair plan is never revised. Which stage of continuous planning has failed?

  • A. Monitor, because nobody is tracking the repairs as the weeks go by. — The overdue repairs are counted every single week, so the monitoring stage is running fine.
  • B. Plan, because the evidence arrives and the plan it should change stands. — correct. Evidence reaching the analysts and stopping there is the backward-looking budget the loop replaces.
  • C. Analyze, because the distance from the target is not being worked out. — The distance behind target is worked out weekly, which is exactly what this stage does.
  • D. Manage, because the crews are not being dispatched while the week runs. — Crews are dispatched and the week is being run, so real-time management is happening already.

11. 7.2 A Fairmount cube is built with pallets shipped laid along an axis and the depot name stored inside each cell. Why can nobody query it?

  • A. The cube holds too few dimensions to answer a three-part question properly. — The number of dimensions is not the problem here; what each part of the cube holds is.
  • B. Each cell needs a hierarchy before any figure stored inside it can be read. — Hierarchies let a user move between levels, and they are not what makes a cell readable.
  • C. The two are swapped: the count is the measure and the depot is the dimension. — correct. A measure is the value you add up, and a dimension is the thing you add it up by.
  • D. Pallets shipped has to be pre-aggregated before it will fit onto an axis at all. — Pre-aggregation speeds a query up, and it does not decide what belongs on an axis.

12. 7.2 A report shows one profit figure for the whole year. The finance director wants to see whether a single quarter carried it. Which move does that?

  • A. Drill down, putting the time dimension back on an axis so its members show. — correct. Putting a dimension back on an axis shows the separate members a rolled-up total hid.
  • B. Roll up, so that the four quarters are gathered into a single yearly total. — The four quarters are already gathered into the yearly figure the director is looking at.
  • C. Dice the cube, narrowing several dimensions to a smaller block at once. — Dicing narrows several dimensions at once, which answers a far narrower question than this.
  • D. Slice to whichever quarter looks likeliest and read the figure off there. — Picking one quarter in advance assumes the answer the director is trying to find.

13. 7.2 A Fairmount merchandiser asks for two product lines, in three regions, over two quarters, pulled out of a much larger cube. Name the move.

  • A. Rolling up, because several members of each dimension are gathered into one figure. — Rolling up folds a dimension into a total, and this request keeps every named member separate.
  • B. Slicing, because the cube is being reduced to a smaller set of its own cells. — A slice fixes one dimension to a single member, and three are being narrowed here.
  • C. Drilling down, because the request names members rather than asking for totals. — Drilling down adds members back onto an axis, where this request takes members away.
  • D. Dicing, because more than one dimension is narrowed to a subset at once. — correct. Narrowing several dimensions at the same time lifts a block out of the middle of a cube.

14. 7.2 A Fairmount operations screen shows this week's totals and a set of red flags. A manager asks why one depot is red and has to email an analyst to find out. Which usage model is missing?

  • A. Exception reporting, since the red flags do not point at anything actionable. — The red flags are the exception reporting, and they are doing their job on this screen.
  • B. Pull reporting, since the manager cannot drill into the aggregate unaided. — correct. Drilling into an aggregate or running a self-service query without an analyst is pull reporting.
  • C. Push reporting, since the weekly totals arrive without anybody asking for them. — The weekly totals already arrive unrequested, so push reporting is present here too.
  • D. Visual analytics, since the screen carries no chart a reader could interrogate. — Interactive charts would help, but the missing capability is the manager's own query.

15. 7.2 A warehouse screen colours any lane whose picking accuracy falls below its agreed floor. Which usage model is that?

  • A. Exception reporting, because formatting points at what needs attention now. — correct. Conditional formatting doing the pointing is what the chapter means by an alert.
  • B. Pull reporting, because the reader chooses which lane and which period are shown. — Nothing here is chosen by the reader, since the colouring happens without any request.
  • C. Push reporting, because the figure arrives without the reader requesting it. — The screen does arrive unrequested, but push reporting is the summary rather than the flag.
  • D. Visualization, because a coloured lane is read faster than a printed figure. — Colour is the display technique, and a usage model describes what the tile is for.

16. 7.2 A haulage model reads: margin equals loads times the gap between rate and cost per load, less fixed costs. An analyst raises the cost per load and leaves every other input where it was. Which analysis is that?

  • A. Sensitivity analysis, because a cost that may or may not rise is being weighed. — Sensitivity analysis weights each possible value by its likelihood, and no probability is used here.
  • B. Optimisation, because the best balance among the parameters is being sought. — Optimisation searches for a balance inside constraints rather than moving a single figure.
  • C. Goal seeking, because the analyst is working backwards from a wanted result. — Goal seeking fixes the end state first, and this analyst has fixed no result at all.
  • D. What-if analysis, because one input is moved and the result is read off. — correct. Changing one input while everything else holds still is the definition of a what-if run.

17. 7.2 A board fixes the margin it wants and asks what rate per load would reach it, assuming the same number of loads as last year. Which analysis is that, and what is it holding still?

  • A. Sensitivity analysis, holding the rate still and weighting the cost by its likelihood. — No probabilities appear in the request, and sensitivity analysis is built out of them.
  • B. Optimisation, holding the constraints still and searching inside them for a balance. — Optimisation searches for a balance, where the board has already named the result it wants.
  • C. Goal seeking, holding the volume still, which is the assumption worth questioning. — correct. Fixing the end state and solving for an input is goal seeking, and a rate helps decide the volume.
  • D. What-if analysis, holding the wanted margin still and moving the cost per load. — A what-if run moves an input forward, where the wanted result is this request's starting point.

18. 7.2 A Fairmount rule says every order holding an imported spice also holds rice, and that pair appears on two of four hundred orders. What should the buyer do with it?

  • A. Leave it, because the rule rests on too few orders to be anything but coincidence. — correct. Support says whether a rule is big enough to bother with, and two orders is not enough.
  • B. Act on it, because a rule that holds every time is as reliable as a rule can be. — One out of one is arithmetic rather than a pattern, and it cannot carry a shelf move.
  • C. Act on it in the depots that stock the spice, where the rule is at its strongest. — Narrowing the rule to fewer depots adds no orders to it, so the support stays as thin.
  • D. Recount it, since a confidence figure that high points to an error in the counting. — Nothing is miscounted: a rule can be perfectly confident and rest on almost nothing.

19. 7.2 A rule reads: orders holding paper towels also hold hand soap, seventy per cent of the time. A manager writes that towels drive soap sales. What is wrong with the sentence?

  • A. The rule should be read from soap to towels, which reverses the claimed cause. — Reading the rule backwards changes the figure and leaves the causal claim just as unsupported.
  • B. Confidence counts how often the two travel together, not what moved either one. — correct. Association discovery finds correlations among items, and a correlation is a count rather than a mechanism.
  • C. Support has not been quoted here, so the seventy per cent cannot be checked at all. — Support would say whether the rule is worth acting on, which is a separate question from cause.
  • D. The confidence is under the threshold most merchandisers would set for a shelf move. — Whether a figure clears a threshold decides if a rule is worth using, not whether it is causal.

20. 7.2 An unsupervised run splits a charity's donors into four groups labelled nought to three. The fundraising lead asks which group is the loyal one. What is the honest answer?

  • A. The algorithm ranks the groups, so the first one it returns is the loyal one. — Unsupervised runs return groups without ranking them, and the numbering carries no meaning.
  • B. Loyalty is a label, so the run should have been supervised from the start. — Supervised work needs a labelled file, and nobody here has marked any donor as loyal.
  • C. The run names the groups once enough attributes have been added to it. — More attributes change how the groups are formed, and the output still arrives unnamed.
  • D. Nothing in the output says; a person reads the attributes and supplies that. — correct. Clustering returns structure with no meaning attached, and the reading comes from a person.

21. 7.2 A council holds nine hundred expense claims an auditor has already marked proper or improper, and wants a tool that judges the next claim. Which technique fits?

  • A. Supervised learning, because the answer is attached to every existing row. — correct. Marked outcomes are the ground truth, and a model learns the relationship from them.
  • B. Anomaly detection, because improper claims resemble nothing else on the file. — Anomaly detection is used where nobody has said in advance what a bad record looks like.
  • C. Clustering, because the claims will fall into groups the auditor can inspect. — Clustering groups records that resemble each other and attaches no judgement to them.
  • D. Association discovery, because claims are judged by what appears alongside. — Association discovery counts what appears on the same transaction rather than judging one.

22. 7.2 A team trains a model, sees the score, and only then agrees with its users what accuracy would be acceptable. What has gone wrong in the workflow?

  • A. The deployment stage started early, before the users had been properly trained. — Nothing has been deployed here, and training the users comes after a result is accepted.
  • B. The data stage was skipped, so the records were never cleaned or labelled. — The model was trained, so the data stage plainly ran before anybody saw a score.
  • C. The bar belongs in the problem stage, before any result exists to judge. — correct. Setting the bar after the result lets it slide to whatever the model happened to reach.
  • D. The model stage ran twice, which is what the dashed arrow is drawn for. — Repeating a stage is normal practice and is not what went wrong in this sequence.

23. 7.2 A consultant reports a model is right ninety-six per cent of the time, measured on the records it was fitted to. What does that figure support?

  • A. It supports a decision to deploy, since the records came from this firm's own systems. — Where the records came from says nothing about whether the model has seen them before.
  • B. It supports the labelling, since a high score means the ground truth was consistent. — A model can repeat inconsistent labels perfectly well, so the score is no test of them.
  • C. It supports a lower bar, since performance on unseen records would come out higher. — Performance on unseen records is normally lower, which is why the split exists at all.
  • D. It supports nothing about new cases, since the model was tested on its own lessons. — correct. Repeating rows it was fitted to measures memory rather than performance next week.

24. 7.2 A leak-detection tool can be tuned to flag more pipes. Flagging more finds more real leaks and also sends more crews to sound healthy pipe. Who sets that tuning?

  • A. Nobody needs to, because the setting with the best overall accuracy is the one to use. — Overall accuracy averages the two kinds of mistake as though they were interchangeable.
  • B. The utility, because a missed leak and a wasted crew visit cost different amounts. — correct. Whoever carries the cost of each kind of error is the one who prices that trade-off.
  • C. The data scientist, because the threshold is a setting inside the model itself. — She can move the setting, and she cannot price what each mistake costs the business.
  • D. The crews, because they see which flags turned out to be worth attending to. — Crews see the outcome of individual flags without seeing what the missed leaks cost.

25. 7.3 A clerk asks a general text tool for the exact wording of an internal warranty clause and gets a fluent paragraph back. Why is the paragraph untrustworthy?

  • A. The tool predicts likely words, so it wrote what such a clause usually says. — correct. It works out which words are likely to follow, and a plausible clause is what fits the prompt.
  • B. The tool held an old copy of the clause and repeated it without any revision. — An internal clause was never in the training data, so there is no old copy to repeat.
  • C. The tool's guardrails removed the conditions that would have made it accurate. — Guardrails block categories of output and would not quietly rewrite a warranty condition.
  • D. The prompt was too short for the tool to locate the right internal document. — Length was not the issue, because the tool has no route to that document at any length.

26. 7.3 A drafted company page contains a flattering performance figure, a cited rule number, and a named industry award. Which test decides what may be published?

  • A. Whether a second tool produces the same three sentences from the prompt. — Two tools trained on similar text can agree on an invention, so agreement proves little.
  • B. Whether each sentence reads as something the firm would plausibly claim. — Plausibility is exactly what these systems produce, so it cannot separate true from invented.
  • C. Whether each sentence can be checked against the firm's own records. — correct. A sentence you can verify in seconds against your own records is one you can publish.
  • D. Whether the tool was asked to mark the sentences it was least sure about. — A confidence marking is produced the same way as the sentence, so it is no independent check.

27. 7.3 A firm wants a tool that answers questions about a fee schedule revised most months. Which deployment does the job?

  • A. Fine-tuning on the schedule, because the model then holds the fees it learned. — Tuning freezes what it learned on the day it ran, so the fees go stale between runs.
  • B. Retrieval over its own records, so the current schedule reaches the prompt. — correct. The model supplies the language while the firm's own systems supply the current figures.
  • C. A public tool, because a fee schedule is ordinary information rather than secret. — A public tool has no route to the schedule, so it writes the fees such a page usually shows.
  • D. Embedding the model in its own software, so the answers then use firm records. — Moving a model behind your own interface changes where it sits, not what it knows.

28. 7.3 A team argues that moving a general model inside the firm's own application will make its answers specific to the firm. What is wrong with that?

  • A. The application would have to be rebuilt before any answer could change. — A rebuilt application would still be asking the same model the same generic questions.
  • B. The provider's interface strips the firm's own records out of any prompt. — Prompts pass through intact, and the point is that nothing fetches the firm's records.
  • C. Answers become specific once the firm trains a model entirely of its own. — Retrieval makes answers specific at a small fraction of what training a model demands.
  • D. Placement changes where the capability sits, not what the model knows. — correct. Embedding buys placement, so the answers stay limited to what the training data held.

29. 7.3 A Fairmount manager wants software that reads each warranty claim, looks up the policy, picks a remedy, writes to the customer and sends it, from one instruction. What is being asked for?

  • A. An agent, which takes a goal and produces both a plan and the actions. — correct. A goal goes in, a multi-step plan and the actions come out, reached through integrations.
  • B. A prediction model that scores each claim for the claims team to work through. — A score is a number, and nobody's letter gets written or sent by a prediction model.
  • C. A prompt template long enough to hold the policy and the claim together. — A longer prompt is still one prompt, so autonomy stays at none and nothing gets sent.
  • D. A retrieval step that hands the policy to whoever is writing the reply. — Retrieval supplies a fact to a person, and the sequence of steps is still done by hand.

30. 7.3 An agent decides refunds unattended and one reviewer can be funded. Where does that reviewer generally belong?

  • A. At the very start, so that every claim is seen by a person before the agent begins. — Everything passes that gate and there is nothing to check yet, so it costs most and catches least.
  • B. At the end, reading each finished letter before it leaves the building. — A finished letter catches a little more and takes longer to read, for effort already spent.
  • C. At the point the remedy has been chosen, where an error first exists. — correct. This is the first moment the machine's judgement exists in a form a person can read quickly.
  • D. Nowhere, since a reviewer's cost exceeds what these errors themselves cost. — Unattended autonomy is not a saving; it is a liability that arrives politely under your name.

31. 7.3 A firm blocks outside text tools on its own machines. Six months later staff are using free tools on their personal phones. What does that illustrate?

  • A. Blocking works, and using a personal phone is a policy breach rather than a risk. — Calling it a breach does not stop company text reaching a tool nobody is watching.
  • B. The block should be widened to personal devices, which closes the route entirely. — A firm cannot police the phones in its staff's pockets, so the route stays open in practice.
  • C. Access control fails where staff have not been trained in writing good prompts. — Training helps with the quality of prompts, and it is not what drives people around a block.
  • D. Determined staff find a workaround, and the workaround usually raises the risk. — correct. The workaround is usually a free public tool, which raises the exposure a block was meant to lower.

32. 7.3 A recording circulates of a company director making a statement she denies making. What should govern how it is judged?

  • A. A convincing recording is strong evidence, because the glitches are hard to remove. — Early fakes had glitches, and the ones produced now are refined until those glitches are gone.
  • B. Modern fakes are trained until a detector cannot tell, so looking real proves little. — correct. A generator is set against a discriminator until the discriminator can no longer separate them.
  • C. Video is harder to fabricate than audio, so a recording of this kind is unlikely. — Faces and audio are both mapped and swapped by the same family of techniques.
  • D. A denial from the person shown settles it, because the footage is about her alone. — A denial is a claim like any other, and it does not tell a viewer the footage was fabricated.

33. 7.4 A retiring engineer's handover file records the calibration tolerance for a press. She also has a knack for hearing when a bearing is about to go. Which asset is at risk of leaving?

  • A. Both of them equally, because everything the engineer holds departs on the same day. — The tolerance stays behind in writing, which is precisely what makes it an explicit asset.
  • B. Neither, because a handover file captures whatever the engineer can describe. — What she can describe is the explicit part, and the knack is the part she cannot state.
  • C. The tolerance, because a single number is easily mislaid in a large handover file. — A number can be documented, archived and looked up again by anybody who needs it.
  • D. The knack, because it is held as how to do a task well and resists writing down. — correct. Tacit assets live in a person's mind as how to perform a task, so they leave with the person.

34. 7.4 A firm proposes to capture a veteran buyer's negotiating judgement by having her fill in a template. What is the flaw in that plan?

  • A. Templates are only a storage technology, and knowledge also needs a communication one. — Storage and communication tools are both needed, and neither of them captures a judgement.
  • B. A template collects what she can already state, the part that was least at risk. — correct. Tacit assets are not handed over as documents, so capture means mentoring and recorded conversation.
  • C. Filling in a template costs an afternoon she would rather spend with customers. — The cost of contributing is a real obstacle elsewhere, and it is not why this method fails.
  • D. Her judgement is explicit, so it belongs in a database rather than a template. — Negotiating judgement is the textbook tacit asset, so no database holds it whole.

35. 7.4 A map of who works with whom shows one person holding the most ties inside a team, and another holding the two ties to a second team. Who is the bridge?

  • A. The second, whose departure leaves the two teams with no route to each other. — correct. Holding the ties across a gap is what makes somebody a bridge, whatever the total is.
  • B. The first, because the number of working relationships is what defines a bridge. — The most ties makes a connector, and those ties may all sit inside a single team.
  • C. Both, because a bridge and a connector are two readings of one position. — The two are usually different people, which is why the map is measured rather than guessed.
  • D. Neither, since a bridge links organizations rather than teams inside one firm. — Social network analysis maps people inside an organization as readily as across firms.

36. 7.4 A director says the reporting chart already shows who collaborates, so mapping contacts adds nothing. What does the mapping find that the chart cannot?

  • A. Which teams are over-resourced, since the ties are counted per person not per team. — Counting ties says who talks to whom and nothing about how a team has been staffed.
  • B. Which employees have been longest in post, since ties accumulate over the years. — Length of service sits on a personnel record, and a long-serving person may hold few ties.
  • C. Where two parts of the firm solve one problem without hearing of each other. — correct. A structural hole is the missing link that a reporting chart has no way of showing.
  • D. Who is senior to whom, which the chart records at every level of the firm. — Seniority is the one thing a reporting chart already records, which is the point being made.

37. 7.4 Two of a chain's own branches sit close enough that their twenty-minute drive-time rings largely overlap. What does that overlap tell the property team?

  • A. Drive time is the wrong measure here, so straight-line distance should be used. — Drive time is the honest measure of reach, because distance and travel time disagree badly.
  • B. The area is proven, so a third branch inside the same ring would also succeed. — Ground that two branches already reach is served, so a third would divide it further.
  • C. The rings are a display choice, so the overlap says nothing about the trade. — A drive-time ring is computed from the road network and describes real reachable ground.
  • D. One branch is drawing its customers from ground the other already reaches. — correct. Trade area analysis exists partly to show where a firm's own sites eat into each other.

38. 7.4 A council colours a map by the total potholes reported in each ward. The largest rural ward, covering far more ground than any other, comes out darkest. What has the map mostly shown?

  • A. Where the ground is largest, because a raw count rises with the area covered. — correct. A quantity mapped raw mostly shows where the ground and the people are, not where the rate is.
  • B. Where reporting is most diligent, since a count reflects who bothers to report. — Reporting habits do affect a count, and the stem points at the ward covering the most ground.
  • C. Where repairs run slowest, because unrepaired faults accumulate into the count. — Repair speed changes the backlog, and it does not explain why the biggest ward leads.
  • D. Where the road surface is worst, since a count of faults measures condition. — Condition is a rate per kilometre of road, which is exactly what this map has not divided by.

39. 7.5 Fairmount's three largest customers together take a large share of its volume, and each now demands a named delivery slot at renewal. Which force is that, and where would a system answering it land?

  • A. Supplier power, answered by a knowledge system landing on human resources. — Suppliers set the price of an input, and these customers are buying rather than supplying.
  • B. New entrants, answered by a map of layers landing on outbound logistics. — Nobody new has arrived, and the pressure comes from customers who already buy here.
  • C. Buyer power, answered by forecasting that lands on the spine of the chain. — correct. Concentration gives customers leverage, and delivering the slot is work on the spine.
  • D. Rivalry, answered by a dashboard landing on the firm's own infrastructure. — Rivalry needs a competitor taking the business, and no competitor appears in this account.

40. 7.5 Fairmount ships a forecasting system that works, and a rival licenses the same package the next quarter. What does the firm's advantage have to rest on?

  • A. The labelled history, the agreed definitions, and the habit of acting on it. — correct. Software can be bought, where a labelled history and a working habit take years to assemble.
  • B. The package itself, since the rival will take a year to configure it properly. — A configuration gap closes quickly, so an installation date is not a durable barrier.
  • C. The industry's structure, which a working decision system has now altered. — One firm's better decisions leave the industry's structure exactly where it was before.
  • D. The vendor's contract, which can be written to exclude any competing buyer. — A widely sold package reaches rivals whatever one buyer's contract happens to say.

41. 7.6 Two proposals: one predicts which members will let a gym subscription lapse, the other predicts weekly towel demand. Membership already telephones every member each month. Which is the weaker recommendation?

  • A. The towel forecast, because towels matter far less to the gym than its members do. — Importance is not the test; the test is whether anybody changes what they do about it.
  • B. The lapse model, because nobody would act differently when its score is right. — correct. An accurate prediction with no decision attached buys a running cost and no change.
  • C. The lapse model, because member behaviour cannot be forecast from past data. — Behaviour is forecast from past data routinely, and the stem says this model scores well.
  • D. The towel forecast, because demand for a consumable is too steady to model. — Steady demand is easy to forecast, and the forecast still changes the weekly order.

42. 7.6 A board paper opens: our claims handling has been unacceptable for two years. What does rewriting that as a count with a denominator buy?

  • A. A stronger tone, which is usually what persuades a committee to release money. — Tone cannot be priced or reviewed, and the committee is being asked to approve money.
  • B. A defence, since naming a figure moves the blame away from the handlers. — Blame is not what a problem statement is for, and a count does not shift it anywhere.
  • C. A sentence anyone in the room can check, and a figure to measure against later. — correct. A count with a denominator can be verified now and compared against at the review date.
  • D. A shorter paper, since a figure takes less space on the page than a description. — Brevity is incidental; what changes is that the claim becomes checkable and measurable.

Continue the practice. Take a decision somebody in your own workplace makes every week. Write down what they decide, what they look at before deciding, and what they would have to see to decide differently. Then say honestly which part of it a report could settle, which part a prediction could inform, and which part is a judgement no system will make for them — and who carries it when the answer is wrong.
