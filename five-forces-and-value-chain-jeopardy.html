Porter’s five forces · the value chain model

Fifty clues on two frameworks

The first board is the vocabulary you have to have: the five forces by name, the nine activities of the chain, and the words that decide how strong a force is. The second board is the work you have to do with them — diagnosing pressure from evidence, placing an initiative on the chain, matching a system to the force it answers, and the shape of the written analysis. Pick a category and a dollar value, decide your answer, then reveal it and grade yourself honestly.

How to play

  • Goal: clear both boards and bank as much money as you can. You choose the category and the value, the same way the real game works.
  • Every clue is a statement. Your answer is phrased as a question — “What is buyer power?” not “Buyer power.”
  • Answer honestly. Nothing is typed and nothing is graded for you. Decide your answer, tap Reveal answer, then say whether you actually had it. The board cannot tell, and a score you did not earn teaches you nothing.
  • No penalty for a miss on the boards. This is a study tool, not an elimination game: right answers add the tile’s value and misses simply move on. Final Jeopardy is the one place a wrong answer costs you, because you chose the wager.
  • Every answer explains itself. Under each answer is a sentence saying why it is that one and not the term next to it, because the near-miss is what the exam tests.
  • The score is not the point. At the end you get a breakdown by course objective and by topic, so a weak area shows up as a named thing to go back to instead of disappearing into one number.

The last category is the written analysis, not more vocabulary Writing the Analysis asks about the shape of the deliverable itself: how many forces get rated, how many value chain areas count as genuinely different, how many initiatives you recommend, what a comparison with a real company has to take from it, and the two things that make a recommendation checkable a year later. Those clues are worth playing even if you already know every definition on the first board.

Which course objectives this board covers

Every clue is tagged with the course objectives it serves, and the results screen reports them separately. This board is deliberately narrow — two frameworks, not a chapter — so it claims four objectives and says plainly which ones it leaves alone.

Double Jeopardy

The first board is clear. Your score is $0. The second board is worth double, and it stops asking what the terms mean and starts asking what you would actually do.

What changes

  • Values double — $400 through $2,000.
  • The clues become situations. Most of them describe a firm with a problem and ask you to name the force, the activity, or the system, which is the form the material takes once you are the person being asked.
  • Two of the categories are about the mistakes rather than the definitions, because the near-miss is where the marks go.
  • Your score carries over. Nothing from round one is lost.
