Section 4–1a

What electronic commerce actually is

Most people meet electronic commerce as a shopping site. The definition is wider and older than that, and the wider version explains why a business changes shape when it starts selling online.

It started with the data, not the shopping

In 1948 the United States Air Force in Europe was flying supplies into Berlin around the clock. The cargo mattered, and so did the information about it: what a shipment held, where it was, and when it would land.

To move that information the operation devised standard universal codes and sent them by teletype. Transactions between organizations became electronic there, in a records problem, decades before anybody bought anything from a web page.

The modern definition keeps that shape. Electronic commerce is electronic transactions over digital networks: the exchange of goods, services and money among firms, between firms and their customers, and between customers. Four parts of that sentence each do work.

  • Electronic transactions — the agreement, the money and the record all move as data rather than as paper handed across a counter.
  • Over digital networks — the two parties never have to be in the same place, or awake at the same hour, for the exchange to complete.
  • Goods, services and money — what is exchanged may be a physical object, something delivered entirely as data, or the payment itself.
  • Among firms, with customers, between customers — all three directions are inside the definition, so one private seller trading with one private buyer counts.

More than the moment of payment

Notice what the definition does not say: it does not say buying. Electronic commerce covers what leads up to a purchase and the service that follows it: a buyer who cannot find a specification never reaches the payment step. Sort the nine moments below into the part of the arc each belongs to.

Before the sale — everything that helps a buyer decide, up to the moment of commitment

  • Comparing the specifications of two models side by side on a seller’s site — Comparison is where most purchases are won or lost, and the definition covers it: electronic commerce includes the events leading up to a purchase, not only the purchase.
  • An email reminding a shopper about the basket they left without paying — The basket was abandoned, so no sale has happened. The reminder is an attempt to finish a purchase that is still ahead of the buyer rather than behind them.
  • A price quotation sent to a business buyer who asked what 200 units would cost — A quotation is an offer, not a sale. It is one of the pre-purchase events the definition covers, and a great deal of selling between organizations happens at this step.

The transaction itself — the exchange of commitment and money, and the record that closes it

  • The card network authorizing the amount as the buyer pays — Authorization is the moment the money is committed. It is the narrow event most people mean by electronic commerce, and it is the smallest part of the whole arc.
  • The order confirmation the buyer receives seconds later — The confirmation is the record that closes the exchange. The boundary is genuinely close here, so read it as the receipt belonging to the transaction rather than as later service.

After the sale — everything the seller still owes the buyer once the money has moved

  • A notice that the parcel has left the depot and will arrive on Thursday — Delivery tracking is service after the sale. The money has already moved, and the seller is now keeping a promise rather than trying to win one.
  • A printable label for returning an item that did not fit — Returns are the clearest post-sale obligation, and an awkward one is a common reason a buyer does not come back, which is why sellers treat returns as part of commerce.
  • A registration page for the two-year warranty on a repaired item — Warranty registration happens once the item is owned, and it exists to serve the buyer later on, which places it firmly on the far side of the sale.
  • A review left three weeks after delivery — The review is written long after the money moved, and it then feeds the comparison step of the next buyer, which is how the arc loops back to its own beginning.

Cutting out the middleman

The internet and the web then widened all of that, and selling to individual consumers became practical. The web changed how firms create and capture value: a producer that once needed somebody else to reach buyers could now reach them itself, which broke business models that had been stable for a century.

Disintermediation is the name for that: cutting out the middleman — the wholesalers and retailers that traditionally sat between a producer and its customers — and reaching customers directly. Airlines selling seats on their own sites are one example, and direct-to-consumer brands are another.

Selling direct is not the only outcome. Many producers who leave a wholesaler behind list inside an electronic marketplace instead — a shared venue such as Amazon or Etsy, where many sellers reach many buyers and an almost limitless range of services can be offered. It supplies the reach and charges for it.

The chain before the web — Each step buys from the one before it, adds a margin, and sells on.

Producer (Makes the product) → Wholesaler (Buys in bulk, holds stock) → Retailer (Sells in ones and twos) → Customer (Known only to the retailer)

  • Each intermediary is paid for something real: reach, storage, credit and the work of selling in small quantities.
  • The producer’s own selling cost is low, because the two middlemen carry the cost of reaching the customer.
  • The producer learns almost nothing about the buyer, since every conversation happens two steps away from it.
  • Margin is lost at each handover, which is the figure that makes selling direct look attractive.

Disintermediated — The producer sells straight to the customer over its own site.

Producer (Makes, markets, ships, supports) → Customer (Buys from the producer itself)

  • Disintermediation removes the intermediaries and their margin, and hands the producer everything they were doing.
  • The cost of reaching the customer now sits with the producer, as advertising, parcels, returns and support.
  • In exchange the producer gains the relationship and the data, which the longer chain never allowed it.
  • The chapter’s examples are airlines selling seats on their own sites and the rise of direct-to-consumer brands.

Re-intermediated — A different intermediary takes the place of the old one.

Producer (Makes and ships the product) → Online marketplace (Finds the buyer, takes a cut) → Customer (Buys inside the marketplace)

  • The marketplace performs the old intermediary’s function — reach and trust — and charges a fee for it.
  • The producer still packs and ships, but the buyer arrives through somebody else’s front door.
  • Removing one middleman often installs another, because the work of finding buyers does not vanish with the wholesaler.
  • Whether this is the better deal depends on the fee set against what reaching those buyers alone would cost.

Whichever arrangement a producer ends up in, the middleman’s work does not disappear along with the middleman. It moves, and a distributor usually does more than its invoice suggests.

  • Reach — it already sells to hundreds of shops, so a product arrives in front of buyers the producer has never met.
  • Stock and delivery — it holds goods, consolidates orders and ships in bulk, which costs far less per unit than posting single parcels.
  • Money and risk — it pays on invoice and carries the loss when a shop pays late or not at all, so the producer is not financing the sale.
  • Returns and questions — it absorbs damaged goods, replacements and the enquiries that follow a sale, none of which reach the producer at all.

Every one of those is work, and disintermediation hands it to the producer along with the margin. The case below is a hypothetical workshop weighing exactly that move, with invented figures.

2. The owners agree the fully costed margin is better direct. What does disintermediating transfer to the workshop along with that margin?

  • A. The distributor’s work as well — stock, parcels, chasing payment, returns, questions — correct. Every line the exhibit marks as absorbed is a task somebody still has to perform, and after the change that somebody is the workshop, which is most of why the margin is larger.
  • B. The distributor’s existing relationships with the bike shops that stock the product — Shop relationships belong to the distributor and do not travel. Selling direct reaches a different buyer entirely, and the shops may well treat the new store as competition.
  • C. The risk of damage in transit, which now sits with the shipping carrier instead — A carrier does take on some transit risk, but the workshop still replaces the damaged pannier and still answers the rider who received it, whatever the carrier eventually pays.
  • D. The distributor’s buying data, which transfers with the account when it closes — The distributor has never shared its buying data, and an ended contract does not oblige it to. That data is precisely what the workshop has been missing all along.

3. Set the money aside. What does the direct route give the workshop that it has never had before?

  • A. A lower cost of getting a single pannier into a buyer’s hands than today — Shipping single parcels costs the workshop more per unit than a share of a pallet did, so the cost of one delivery moves in the wrong direction here.
  • B. Contact with the riders themselves, and a record of what they ask for — correct. The facts note that the workshop has never spoken to a rider; selling direct produces both the relationship and a record of what buyers want, and no distributor invoice contains either.
  • C. Freedom from the fees that come with taking payment online — Taking payment online adds a fee rather than removing one, and the exhibit prices it at a small but real amount on every direct order the workshop takes.
  • D. An assurance that the total volume sold each quarter will rise — Nothing here promises more units. The last row of the exhibit says the direct volume is untested, which is the largest unknown in the whole decision.

What this case was really about. This is disintermediation in miniature. Removing the middleman moves its margin to the producer and its work along with it, so an honest comparison prices both columns fully and then asks whether the direct volume even exists. What the workshop gains that no distributor ever gave it is the customer relationship and the data attached to it, which is the deeper reason an online presence became a strategic necessity rather than a cost saving.

Why an online presence stopped being optional

Two properties explain why an online presence became a strategic necessity for most companies, and neither of them is about fashion.

  • Market reach — a site is open to anyone who can reach it, so the market is no longer only the people who can travel to a building.
  • Scalability — serving the thousandth visitor costs far less than serving the first, so growth need not mean a matching increase in premises and staff.

Neither property says the online route is cheaper to run. It is a different way to reach a market, and it arrives carrying all of the work the earlier list described.

Electronic commerce is not the payment. It is every electronic step from the first comparison to the last replacement, and disintermediation moves all of those steps — not only the margin — onto whoever now stands closest to the customer.

Section 4–1b

Who is on each side of the transaction

Electronic commerce is usually sorted into types, and the sorting rule is simpler than it looks: name who stands on each side of the exchange. Not the website, not the product, not the size of the order. This section works through the types the chapter names, and through the cases where the obvious answer is the wrong one.

Naming the two sides

The convention is a pair of letters with a “to” between them. The first letter is the side that sells or provides. The second is the side that buys or receives. The chapter names three types built on that pattern.

  • Business-to-consumer (B2C) — transactions between businesses and their customers, which covers online retailing, the sale of digital products, and streaming services.
  • Business-to-business (B2B) — transactions among businesses, such as a manufacturer trading over the web with its suppliers, with no end consumer in the exchange.
  • Consumer-to-consumer (C2C) — transactions between people who are not necessarily working together, such as one person buying memorabilia from another through an auction site.

When neither side is a business

Some exchanges have no firm on either side. One person sells to another person, both of them are consumers, and the transaction is C2C.

The marketplace hosting them is doing something else. It is not the seller. It runs a platform business model: it enables transactions between other parties and takes a fee for that service. The goods on offer are not its goods.

A fourth category sits outside all three, because it involves services neither sold by nor to companies. E-government is the use of information systems to give citizens, organizations and other government agencies information about public services, and to let them interact with the government.

  • An agency publishes its spending records for anyone to download. — Providing electronic access to public government data is listed by the chapter as e-government, even though nobody buys or sells anything at all.

Where the money actually is

Most people meet electronic commerce as B2C, because that is the part of it they can see. By revenue it is the smaller half, and not by a little. B2B is the largest form of all, because a great many firms trade with other firms and never with a consumer.

The chapter reports 2022 figures for United States manufacturers’ electronic-commerce shipments and for wholesalers’ electronic-commerce sales, each measured in trillions of dollars. Learn the ordering rather than the digits: business-to-business volume dwarfs the consumer-facing volume, and a figure like that is a snapshot of one year.

The second thing to carry away is that one firm is rarely one type. Take a consumer-facing company — the chapter’s example is a clothing and home furnishing retailer — and follow a single product through it.

  1. It sources raw materials from a range of specialized suppliers, and every one of those purchases is business to business.
  2. It sells the finished product to a distributor or a wholesaler, which is business to business a second time.
  3. Or it sells that product directly to the end consumer, and only this last step is business to consumer.

The shopper sees the last step and assumes it is the business. The steps before it were larger, and they were the other type. The case below is built on that mismatch, inside an invented company.

Section 4–1c

Fintech: money without the middle

Buying something online is only half a transaction. The other half is the money: how it leaves one account, how long it takes to arrive, and who takes a slice on the way. This section is about the firms that rebuilt that half, and about what they did not rebuild.

One service, done very well

Fintech is the chapter’s word for innovative technologies that transform financial services. In plainer terms it is software doing a job a bank used to do. The chapter credits it with accelerating electronic commerce three ways: simpler online payments, easier financing for online sellers, and new products such as digital wallets and peer-to-peer payments.

One structural difference explains most of what follows. A traditional bank offers a wide range of services under one roof — an account, a card, a loan, somewhere to invest. A fintech firm typically takes one of those and does it very well.

Depth in one service lets a young firm compete with an institution a century old. Narrowness obliges its customers to hold several relationships instead of one. The chapter’s own categories all follow that shape.

  • Crowdfunding platforms — many small backers fund one project directly, instead of one lender deciding on everybody’s behalf.
  • Online-only banks — everyday accounts, cards and transfers run entirely through an app, with no branch anywhere behind them.
  • Mobile payment processors — a phone or a small reader becomes a way for almost any seller to accept cards.
  • Peer-to-peer lenders — people with money to lend are matched against people who want to borrow it.
  • Small-business lenders — credit for firms too small or too quick-moving for a traditional commercial loan process.
  • Digital investment managers — a portfolio built and rebalanced by software from a short questionnaire about goals.

Crowdfunding platforms · Many small backers, one project

  • What it does: Lets a project raise money from a large number of small contributors, each deciding for themselves, instead of from one institution deciding on behalf of many.
  • Who it displaces: The lender or investor who would otherwise have assessed the project and committed other people’s money to it, along with the meetings that assessment required.
  • What it does not solve: It does not judge whether the project can actually be delivered. Backers carry that risk themselves, and a funded project is not a finished one.
  • Why it can be cheaper: The platform runs one listing process and one payment flow for every campaign, so the cost of administering a single deal is spread across all of them.

Online-only banks · An account with no branch

  • What it does: Provides everyday banking — holding money, moving it, and a card to spend it with — through an app, with no counter anywhere to walk up to.
  • Who it displaces: The branch network, and with it the premises, the staff and the local opening hours that used to be how a customer reached their own money.
  • What it does not solve: Anything that still wants a physical presence: paying in cash, or a conversation with somebody who has the authority to make an exception for you.
  • Why it can be cheaper: Premises and branch staff are among a traditional bank’s largest fixed costs, and an app serves one more customer at close to no extra cost.

Mobile payment processors · Card acceptance for a stall

  • What it does: Turns an ordinary phone or a small reader into a way of accepting card payments, so a seller can take a card without a contract negotiated in advance.
  • Who it displaces: The bank arrangement and the fixed terminal that once stood between a small seller and the ability to accept anything other than cash.
  • What it does not solve: The fee on each sale, which is often above a large retailer’s negotiated rate; the convenience is paid for out of a slice of every transaction.
  • Why it can be cheaper: Signing up is automated and the hardware is inexpensive, so the processor spends almost nothing acquiring a seller who may take only a few payments a week.

Peer-to-peer lenders · Savers lending to borrowers

  • What it does: Matches people who have money to lend against people who want to borrow it and prices the loan, taking a fee for running the match rather than lending its own funds.
  • Who it displaces: The bank in the middle, which traditionally took deposits at one rate, lent that money out at a higher one, and kept the difference.
  • What it does not solve: Default. If a borrower stops paying, the loss falls on the people who funded that particular loan rather than on an institution absorbing it.
  • Why it can be cheaper: There is no branch, no deposit base to fund and a credit decision made largely by software, so the gap between what a lender earns and a borrower pays can be narrower.

Small-business lenders · Credit judged on trading data

  • What it does: Lends to small firms, often deciding from trading records such as card takings and sales history rather than from a long written application and a meeting.
  • Who it displaces: The commercial loan officer, and the weeks of assessment a small borrower used to sit through for an amount the bank considered marginal.
  • What it does not solve: The cost of borrowing for a risky business. A fast decision is not a cheap one, and speed is frequently priced into the rate.
  • Why it can be cheaper: The assessment runs automatically against data the business already generates, so a small loan stops being uneconomic to underwrite.

Digital investment managers · A portfolio run by software

  • What it does: Builds and rebalances an investment portfolio from a few answers about goals and tolerance for loss, and increasingly uses artificial intelligence to advise or to manage it.
  • Who it displaces: The human adviser whose time made small portfolios uneconomic to serve, together with the annual review appointment that came with the relationship.
  • What it does not solve: Market risk, and anything about the customer that the questionnaire did not think to ask about in the first place.
  • Why it can be cheaper: One model serves every account at once, so the extra cost of managing one more portfolio is close to nothing.

A seventh belongs beside those six: artificial intelligence giving investment advice or managing a portfolio, which applies the same move to judgement rather than to paperwork. Firm names are left out on purpose. Companies of this kind are bought, merged and renamed often, and the category outlives the brand.

Cutting out the middle, again

Section 4–1a introduced disintermediation: reaching customers directly by removing the middle, as an airline does by selling a seat on its own site rather than through an agent. Fintech is that same move, aimed at financial intermediaries instead of wholesalers and retailers.

An online-only bank has no branch network standing between it and the customer. A direct investing app has no broker taking the order. The intermediary is not talked into charging less. It is taken out of the path, and what replaces it costs almost nothing to run one more time.

So electronic commerce is no longer only trade. It once meant firms buying from firms and consumers buying from firms. It now takes in the financial transaction itself — banking, brokerage, lending, investment — and most major banks and investment firms provide those services online as well.

Expectations do not stay where they were set

Fintech improves the experience of paying, which is the visible effect. The quieter one is that it resets what a customer counts as normal. Once money moves between two people in seconds, a two-day wait somewhere else stops reading as ordinary and starts reading as a fault.

Expectations set in one industry are then applied to every other. Somebody who splits a restaurant bill instantly on a phone brings that standard to an insurer, a landlord and a payroll department, none of which ever competed with a payments app and all of which are now measured against one.

Four money jobs in one small business

Strip a small trading business down and its money handling is four separate jobs. They feel like one job because a single bank account has always covered all four of them.

  • Taking payment in person — a card tapped at a stall, which needs a reader, an account to settle into and a fee per sale.
  • Taking payment online — a checkout that works on a phone and accepts money from somebody the seller will never meet.
  • Financing stock — borrowing now to buy in bulk against sales that will not arrive for weeks, judged on the business rather than the owner.
  • Parking a surplus — putting cash that is not needed this month somewhere it earns a return and stays reachable.

For each of those four there is now a specialist that does only that job. Splitting them buys a better price or a faster settlement on at least one. It also buys four logins, four fee schedules and four sets of records that somebody has to reconcile into one picture.

The questions underneath do not move either. Whoever holds the money is regulated by somebody, a balance is protected under some scheme or under none, and a lending decision made by software is still a lending decision. The business below is invented and its figures with it.

Section 4–5a

Paying online without handing over everything

Every online sale ends the same way: a stranger is asked to type a card number into a page they have never seen before. This section is about that moment — why it is where a sale is won or lost, and how a payment service changes who ends up holding the dangerous part.

Handing the sensitive part to somebody else

Concern about that moment produced independent payment services — PayPal, Apple Pay and Square among them — which stand between the shopper and the seller. The shopper pays using an account held with the service instead of typing card details into the seller’s page.

The consequence is structural rather than cosmetic, and it is worth setting out item by item.

  • The card number — goes to the payment service and stops there, so it never reaches the seller’s order system in the first place.
  • The account and its password — belong to the service, which means the seller is not defending a login that guards somebody’s money.
  • The email address and purchase history — are kept by the service alongside the payment details, and are not shared with the merchant.
  • What is left with the seller — is an order, a name and an address: what it needs to ship the parcel, and nothing beyond that.

Turn that around and the point becomes plain. The merchant stops holding the thing a thief would want. The order still exists and the shipment still happens; the card number is simply somewhere else.

  3. Inside the service, the shopper sees who is being paid and how much, and approves the payment. — The approval happens where the payment details already live, so the seller receives a decision rather than the card number that produced it.

  5. The service tells the seller the payment succeeded and returns the shopper to the shop’s confirmation page. — What crosses to the seller is a confirmation and an order reference. Somebody who later breaks into the seller’s records finds an order list rather than a set of card numbers.
  6. The seller ships the order, keeping the details it needs to deliver it and to answer questions about it. — A name and an address stay with the seller, because no parcel can be posted without them. The part a thief would have wanted was removed four steps earlier and never arrives.

The moment the sale is lost

Keeping transactions secure is one of the most important parts of selling to consumers, of consumers trading with each other, and of any purchase made on a phone. Money moves at that moment, and so does everything needed to move it.

The chapter is blunt about the human half of the problem. People are reluctant to change their habits online, and they hand sensitive information to sites they know nothing about. As a snapshot of what that costs, the chapter reports that identity fraud cost Americans about US$23 billion in 2023.

Shoppers who feel that unease respond by leaving. The chapter reports that more than half of online shopping carts are abandoned, and it names four reasons sitting behind most of it.

  • Security concerns — the shopper is not sure this seller can be trusted with a card number, and walking away from a cart costs them nothing at all.
  • Impatience — the purchase was a passing intention rather than a plan, and every extra second gives that intention time to fade.
  • Lengthy checkout procedures — each additional field and page is one more chance to decide the item was not worth this much effort.
  • Comparison shopping — the cart was being used as somewhere to park a candidate while the same item was priced somewhere else.

Read those four together and one management idea follows, and the rest of this objective turns on it. A seller has to strike a balance between providing security and reducing friction. Tighten the checkout and more shoppers leave; loosen it and the seller carries more risk.

Where these services went next

Google linked its payment service to its search results, so that somebody searching for a product can see immediately whether a merchant offers that way of paying. The stated intention was to ease the shopping experience and, by doing so, to reduce the number of abandoned carts.

PayPal went in a different direction, letting anyone with an email address send and receive money rather than only pay a shop. One person could now pay another, which was instrumental in the rise of the auction marketplace eBay, where buyer and seller are both ordinary people.

Phones then extended the same idea in two directions at once.

  • Paying inside a shop by holding a phone to a terminal, which is an alternative to carrying cash or cards.
  • Paying a person rather than a business, so a table of friends can settle a restaurant bill between them.

The Chinese messaging platform WeChat shows how far that can run: online payments, in-person payments and transfers between people, all inside a service people already open for other reasons.

The balance is the whole objective in one sentence. A payment service is attractive because it moves both sides of it at once: fewer steps for the shopper to abandon, and less sensitive data left sitting with the seller.

Section 4–5b

Cryptocurrency, blockchain, and trust without a middleman

Every online payment you have ever made passed through somebody you were trusting to make it right. This section takes that arrangement apart: what a trusted middleman actually sells, what it charges for the service, and what a payment looks like when there is nobody in the middle at all.

What the fee is buying

When you give a card number to a familiar retailer, or pay a small seller through a payment service, you are relying on a company you have never met to stand behind the transaction. The chapter calls those companies trusted middlemen. The trust is the product.

Two things in particular are being bought, and it helps to separate them.

  • A safety net — confidence that the money you send will produce the good or service you were promised, and a route to a refund when it does not.
  • Confidentiality — your financial information stays with the middleman rather than being handed to every seller you buy from, which is the whole reason payment services exist.

Neither comes free. The chapter puts card processing at roughly one to three percent of every purchase, charged to the vendor and normally passed on to everybody in higher prices. Payment services charge as well, sometimes several percentage points of the total.

A charge like that disappears inside a large purchase and dominates a small one. The case below works out where that line falls for a hypothetical firm sending many small payments to many countries.

A payment with nobody in the middle

Cryptocurrencies are virtual currencies that no central bank issues, using cryptographic techniques to secure transactions and to generate new units of the currency. The chapter is even-handed about them: they are often described as serving mainly illicit trade, and they have various legitimate applications too.

The claim worth testing is a narrow one. The technology under the best-known cryptocurrency requires no trusted middleman, which cuts the cost of a transfer to a negligible amount and makes both very small payments and international transfers practical.

It was launched around 2008 by a developer working under a pseudonym. Payment processing is handled by thousands of computers around the world, each running the same open source software, so there is no head office, no opening hours and no national holidays.

A transfer is announced to that network, and any computer that wants to verify it may. Two words are worth having in advance: a digital signature proves that the holder of a sending address authorised that particular transfer, and a block is the batch of verified transfers added to the record in one go.

Blocks are added only when a majority of the machines holding the record accept them. The sequence below is one payment, from request to permanent record.

One payment, followed from the moment it is requested to the moment it becomes permanent.

  1. The payer prepares a transfer from their own address to the payee’s address — A bank would look up an account holder in records it keeps privately. Here the sending and receiving addresses are the only identities involved, and neither is a name.
  2. The payer signs the transfer with the digital signature belonging to the sending address — This replaces the bank’s check that you are who you claim to be. The signature shows that whoever controls that address authorised this particular transfer.
  3. The transfer is broadcast to the whole decentralized peer-to-peer network — A bank would pass an instruction privately to one other institution. The announcement instead reaches thousands of computers, none of which is in charge of the others.
  4. Any computer running the open source software verifies the signature and the transfer — Checking is done many times over by strangers rather than once by a clearing department, which is what removes the need to trust any single participant.
  5. The verified transfer is bundled with others into a block and added to the ledger — A bank would post a line into its own private book. This block joins one public book that every participating computer holds its own copy of.
  6. A majority of the network accepts the block and the entry becomes permanent — There is no ledger department that can amend an entry afterwards. Changing it would need consensus from a majority of nodes, and entries once made cannot be deleted.

What indelible and public mean together

The record those computers keep is a blockchain: an indelible, decentralized public ledger to which transactions are added in blocks, standing as proof of every transaction ever made.

Two descriptions in common use are worth correcting here, because both hide what the design is actually doing.

  • Pseudonymous, not anonymous — no names appear on the ledger, only addresses, but every transaction sits there permanently for anyone to read, and an address can often be tied to a person by other means. A permanent public record cannot also be a private one.
  • Hashing and signatures, not secrecy — hashing links each block to the one before it, so the history cannot be quietly rewritten by anybody. Nothing is concealed, because a ledger has to be readable by everybody to be checkable by everybody.

Entries once made cannot be deleted. That is the property that makes tampering hard, and it is the same property that makes a mistake permanent: there is no middleman left to reverse a payment sent to the wrong address.

Working and being adopted are different questions

The mechanism works. The chapter then supplies the evidence against its own enthusiasm: in 2014 several major online companies announced that they would accept the currency, those trials were terminated, and use for ordinary purchase transactions has remained negligible.

That gap is worth carrying out of this chapter. A technology can do exactly what it claims and still not be taken up, for reasons that have little to do with whether it works.

Interest has moved instead towards the ledger itself. Organizations across banking and technology are exploring blockchain for other kinds of transaction, the chapter reports, wherever three properties are needed at once.

  • Trust — the parties need a record none of them can quietly edit, which is what a ledger held by many participants provides without anybody having to vouch for anybody.
  • Accountability — every entry carries a signature, so who added what is settled by the record rather than argued over afterwards from two sets of private books.
  • Transparency — the participants read one shared history instead of reconciling separate copies, which is where most of the saving in a business setting comes from.

Remove the middleman and you remove the fee, the dispute process and the confidentiality in one motion. The question is never only whether the ledger works. It is which of those three you can afford to lose for the payment in front of you.

Section 4–5c

Card-not-present: chargebacks, screening, and fraud signals

Two people are nervous in every online sale. The buyer is sending a card number to a seller they may know nothing about. The seller is taking one from somebody they cannot see. This section is the seller’s half of that worry.

Authorization is not identification

Every card transaction is authorized by the issuer. Be exact about what that answer contains.

Authorization means the card was not reported lost or stolen. It does not mean the person placing the order is the cardholder.

In a card-not-present sale there is no imprint and no signature, so nothing establishes identity. One protection is time: the transaction date is the shipping date, which leaves a day or more to check. Sell a download and the window is gone.

Another is the card security code, the three-digit number on the back of the card and deliberately kept out of the magnetic stripe. A match implies the buyer held the card. Card company rules forbid storing it.

Signals, scores, and the cost of being wrong

A fraud-screening service turns an order into a risk score, and the merchant decides what that number is worth. The chapter names the variables it weighs.

  • Address and telephone agreement — whether shipping address, billing address and phone number match what the issuer holds.
  • Timing — the hour the order arrived, read against the time zone the customer appears to be in.
  • Transaction volume — how much is being bought, set against what this shop and this buyer normally do.
  • Network address — the customer’s address on the network and the geographic location it resolves to.

The chapter also passes on one card network’s published indicators; it names Visa. Two groups concern appearance: meaningless email strings at free providers, and addresses that disagree, reach high-risk destinations, or misspell street names.

The third is pattern: orders larger than usual, several of the same item, mostly big-ticket goods, repeated orders on one card, several cards shipping to one address, and overnight delivery, which shortens the seller’s window.

None of these is a test. Each moves a score, and most describe enormous numbers of ordinary customers. The cost of refusing a real buyer — the sale, the margin, the customer — belongs beside the cost of fraud.

  • Billing address in one country, shipping address in another, with a misspelled street name — The chapter names shipments to high-risk destinations and misspellings of common words or street names together. Neither settles anything: people travel, forward parcels to family, and mistype their own street.
  • Six of the same lens ordered at once, more than the shop usually sells in a month — Orders larger than normal and multiple items of the same type are both listed patterns, because quantity is easier to resell. A rental company placing a genuine order looks exactly the same.
  • The buyer’s network address resolves to a country the shop rarely ships to — The customer’s network address and its geographic location are among the variables a screening service weighs. Travellers, expatriates and anyone using a privacy service produce the same reading, so treat it as a nudge.
  • The delivery address is a hotel in a city the buyer does not live in — The chapter’s figure shows a shipment to a public location as one of the flags an analyst notices. Professionals working away from home have parcels sent to hotels constantly, so it raises the score and settles nothing.
  • A meaningless string at a free mail provider, with nothing else unusual about the order — The published indicators name exactly this: a handle of meaningless characters at a free provider. It nudges the score up and does no more than that, because an enormous number of ordinary people pick a handle that means nothing to a stranger.

Lowers the risk score — makes the order look more like the ordinary customers this shop already has

  • The card security code entered with the order matches — The code is not stored in the magnetic stripe, so a match implies somebody had the physical card in hand. That lowers the score without answering the question, because a stolen card is also a card in hand.
  • The buyer has ordered four times in two years and never disputed a charge — History with your own shop is one of the few signals that is about this person rather than about the shape of this order. It still cannot rule out an account somebody else has taken over.
  • Shipping address, billing address and telephone number all match the issuer’s records — That match is the first variable the chapter lists for a screening service, and it genuinely lowers the score. It is also the easiest of the variables for a determined fraudster to satisfy.

Tells you nothing on its own — true of fraud and of very large numbers of honest buyers alike

  • The card issuer authorized the transaction — Authorization means the card was not reported lost or stolen. It is not evidence about who is placing the order, so it belongs on neither side of a fraud score.
  • The shopper spent eleven minutes reading the shipping policy before checking out — It feels reassuring, and it is not one of the variables a screening service weighs. Time on a page tells you about interest in the terms, not about who owns the card.

What a chargeback takes

When a cardholder disputes a transaction the merchant is financially responsible, and issuers typically charge it back. The chapter counts that loss carefully, because sellers count only the first item.

  • The transaction amount — the money for the sale is pulled back, which is the loss most sellers think of first.
  • The merchandise — the goods have already shipped and are rarely recovered, so the item goes with the payment.
  • The processing costs — the fees paid to move the money are not returned when the sale is unwound.
  • The chargeback fee — the bank charges for handling the dispute, and a high rate can raise fees further or close the merchant account.

Minimizing chargebacks is therefore a first-order concern. Several common causes are not crime at all: unclear policies, vague descriptions, shipping terms nobody can find and prices in an unexpected currency. Those are answered by rewriting a store, not by screening a buyer.

Section 4–5d

Legal ground: consumers, tax, and intellectual property

A storefront anybody can reach also reaches into places whose rules you have never read. The chapter picks out three issues that follow, and this section walks each to the question it puts to a small seller.

Three issues, and one standing caution

The chapter names consumer protection, taxation of online purchases, and intellectual property as the three that matter most, especially for digital products. Non-compliance brings penalties and damages customer relationships.

One caution stands over all of it. Particulars differ by jurisdiction and they change, so what follows is the shape of each obligation. Whether a duty reaches your business is a question for somebody qualified.

Selling to somebody who cannot touch the goods

Offline a customer picks the thing up. Online that interaction is missing, so the buyer leans on what the seller says. The chapter puts transparency and accuracy at the centre of this.

Two duties come up in most places, in the chapter’s account.

  • A clear return and refund policy — written so an ordinary reader understands it, and displayed prominently rather than buried three clicks down.
  • No deceptive practice — false advertising and misleading claims about a product’s quality, benefits or price are commonly prohibited, whatever the seller intended.

Compliance keeps you out of trouble, but the chapter’s reason for going further is commercial. Its example is a retailer whose generous returns cost a great deal in reverse logistics and raise revenue anyway.

Then the borderless part. The chapter reports an attempt to harmonize these rules across the European Union, while in the United States fraudulent practices sit with a federal trade regulator and other matters sit at state level.

Because many of these rules apply based on where the customer is, not where the seller sits, many sellers write one policy to the strictest set they expect to meet.

Where a tax can attach

The trigger column the studio just read has a name behind it. Online sales were once treated like mail order: tax was collected only from customers in a state where the business had substantial presence, so firms chose home bases offering effectively tax-free shopping.

The chapter reports that a 2018 Supreme Court ruling changed that shape. States may collect from sellers with no physical location once certain sales thresholds are reached, known as economic nexus. Tracking volume and collecting the tax becomes the seller’s work.

Crossing a border makes it harder again, and three patterns are worth holding.

  • A duty can follow the buyer home — a customer ordering from abroad may owe use tax in their own state on what they bought.
  • Arrival can trigger a charge — a customer outside the country pays no sales tax to that country, yet may owe tax or an import duty when the shipment lands.
  • Digital products slip through — the movement of a download is difficult to track, and the chapter notes that the tax revenue is easily lost.

Owning content, and not borrowing it by accident

Ownership of content and trademarks is, in the chapter’s phrase, a virtual minefield. It runs in two directions, and sellers usually think about only one.

  • Do not infringe — posting product photographs without permission, or putting somebody else’s trademarked name inside your own domain, are the chapter’s examples.
  • Settle ownership in advance — when a third party builds content for your site, agree who will own the copyright before the work starts.
  • Protect your own — copyright notices and the like, because property nobody manages can mean lost revenue, brand dilution or legal action.

The chapter closes with a housekeeping instruction that is easy to skip: state your jurisdiction clearly, and comply with it.

Digital rights management is the neighbour, and the difference is the whole point: restrictions try to prevent a copy, while a mark only says where an existing copy came from.

The argument, kept even

Both sides are worth stating plainly, and the chapter settles nothing.

  • The industry says the restrictions minimize sales losses from unauthorized duplication.
  • Critics rename it digital restriction management and call enforcement arbitrary.
  • They add that it infringes existing consumer rights and stifles innovation.
  • Activation and device limits inconvenience honest buyers, as on a new computer.
  • That inconvenience, critics argue, can breed the piracy it was built to stop.
  • And sellers who dropped the restrictions entirely have been very successful.

Legal ground is not a wall you hit once. It is a set of questions that attach to where your customer is, what you sold them, and whose work is on the page — and most answers begin with finding out.

Application supplement. This section assembles the chapter’s own Technology Today and Industry Analysis features, which sit outside the two learning objectives the supplied chapter carries. It is practice with chapter material, not a further objective.

Four layers behind a storefront

Competition and customer expectations have both risen, and almost nobody answers them alone. The chapter describes an ecosystem of key players supplying the infrastructure for online sales, payments and logistics — four layers, in the order a seller meets them.

  • Storefront platform — software that creates and manages the store itself, ranging from intuitive tools aimed at small and medium businesses to customizable stores that slot into a website a company already runs.
  • Marketplace — somebody else’s shopfront, which lends a seller an existing customer base and technology and connects a new listing with millions of shoppers on the day it goes up.
  • Payment provider — the layer that takes the money: a widely trusted first mover, an integration a developer can shape, card readers for a small counter, or one-touch checkout on a phone.
  • Fulfillment provider — warehousing, stock, shipping and returns handled by an outside firm, offering speed and scale without the upfront investment of a building and the staff to run it.

Notice what each layer leaves standing. A platform builds the store but brings nobody to it, which is hardest for a fledgling business. A marketplace fills that gap, and rents it.

Marketplace · somebody else’s crowd

  • What it costs you: A larger share of every sale, and a buyer who belongs to the marketplace rather than to you.

  • What it does not solve: It does not judge whether a transaction is genuine, and it does not remove the merchant’s exposure when a cardholder later disputes the sale.

  • What it gives you: Warehousing, stock handling, shipping and returns run by a firm that already has the buildings, the vans and the people standing by.
  • What it costs you: A fee for each unit stored and shipped, and much less direct contact with how the parcel actually arrives.
  • What it does not solve: It does not fix a product people do not want, and it does not take your name off a delivery that turns up late or broken.
  • When to add it: When packing has outgrown the space and the hours available — the chapter’s image of a successful seller outgrowing the garage.

The store on the street

Now the shop you can walk into. Decades ago, large chains installed computerized point-of-sale inventory systems: a bar code scan captures the sale and deducts the item from stock, so the retailer sees in real time what to reorder.

Two things follow from that scan. Checkout gets quicker and shelves run empty less often, which customers feel as satisfaction rather than as a system. Every later change moved the scan.

  • Self-checkout, saving time and labour cost
  • Scanning into the cart, paying at the counter
  • Tags read by radio at a distance
  • A store that watches the shelf instead

A Swiss grocer built the second: customers scan into the cart, then pay at the counter. The third totals a full cart in about a second with no scanning, but only where an item is worth the cost of a tag.

The fourth skips tags entirely. Radar and computer vision detect what a customer takes off the shelf; the customer taps a card to enter, then leaves.

Payment changed on the shop floor too, in three directions.

  • Multi-factor authorization — more than one proof of who is paying, drawn from a card, phone, code, signature, fingerprint or face.
  • Chip and touchless cards — the card carries a small processor rather than only a magnetic stripe, and it no longer has to be handed over or swiped.
  • Near-field communication — the customer’s phone and the retailer’s terminal talk across a few centimetres, and the amount goes to the payment method already stored on the phone.

What the shop still has to answer

That third journey has a name. In showrooming, a customer examines a product in person, then leaves to order it online for less. Storefronts cost billions to build and maintain, an online seller carries no such cost, and the store never recoups it.

Two large chains named in the chapter encouraged the comparison rather than fighting it: browse the shelves, check prices online, and they will match the price and add personal service, keeping the customer and selling something else alongside.

Other in-store technology pushes the same way. Fitting rooms use augmented reality to show how an item would look or to suggest something to go with it, and small radio beacons hand a shopper an offer while the goods are in front of them.

The chapter closes with three questions rather than answers. How can technology help a physical retailer compete with an online seller? How should it answer the criticism that tags track purchasing habits too closely? And how does a store avoid feeling sterile as people are replaced?

What this chapter supplies to the frameworks

Each decision asked for the same two moves: name the pressure an observation belongs to, then say where in the firm a fix would land. A symptom is not a force. Six ideas from this chapter make that translation for you.

  • Disintermediation — when a maker sells direct to the end customer an intermediary disappears, which changes the threat of new entrants and the bargaining power of buyers rather than merely adding a channel.
  • Selling through a marketplace — the marketplace lends you its customers and keeps the relationship, which makes it a supplier whose power over you grows with every order it carries.
  • Cart abandonment and checkout friction — the point where a visitor stops is a marketing and outbound logistics problem inside the value chain, and payment choice sits in the same place.
  • Chargebacks, screening and false declines — disputes are a service and operations cost, and the screening that reduces them turns paying customers away, so the same activity has a revenue side.
  • Compliance across jurisdictions — consumer protection duties, tax obligations and intellectual property all sit in firm infrastructure, and that cost grows with every place the firm now reaches.
  • Showrooming — price transparency turns a trade counter into a display case for a cheaper seller, which is buyer power acting on a firm that has already paid for the building.

Now the two columns disagree, and the disagreement is the finding. The marketplace takes more than twice as many orders as the website, and the account book takes the fewest orders of any channel here, yet the account book brings in more money than both new channels together.

3. Column H: mark any channel with more than five disputed orders for a closer look, and mark the rest as clear.

=IF(E2>5,"Look","Clear")

A flag is a decision rule written down, which is the honest way to use a threshold. Notice that it selects a channel to examine rather than a customer to refuse: the same logic applied to individual orders is what produces false declines.

A conversion rate and a revenue figure tell you where the money is. They do not tell you what the marketplace listing leaves once its commission is taken, or where the disputes are concentrated. Those are questions for the order records, and this chapter gives you every reason to ask them.

Choosing columns and choosing rows are separate decisions. Read the result and the pattern is already visible: the marketplace carries small, light goods, which is exactly what an entrant can post cheaply.

2. How much revenue did each channel produce in this week? Show the largest first.

SELECT channel, SUM(total) AS revenue FROM orders GROUP BY channel ORDER BY revenue DESC

Grouping turns a list of orders into a description of the channels. The account book carries the fewest orders and the most money, and the marketplace does the reverse, which is the first hint that order counts are a poor way to judge a channel.

3. Which channels carried more than one disputed order? Show the channel and how many disputes it carried.

SELECT channel, COUNT(*) AS disputes FROM orders WHERE disputed = 'yes' GROUP BY channel HAVING COUNT(*) > 1

WHERE filters rows and HAVING filters groups, because at the WHERE stage nothing has been counted yet. The answer also tells you where any screening effort should be aimed, and where it would only cost you good customers.

4. What does each channel leave the supplier after its fee? Show the channel, the gross total and the amount left once the fee is taken, best first.

SELECT o.channel, SUM(o.total) AS gross, SUM(o.total - o.total * c.fee_percent / 100) AS net FROM orders o JOIN channels c ON o.channel = c.channel GROUP BY o.channel ORDER BY net DESC

This answer exists in neither table. The order totals sit on one and the fee sits on the other, and only the join shows the marketplace handing back fifteen percent of every order while the firm’s own channels hand back under three. In this week it grossed fifty more than the website and kept five. The same table records who keeps the customer relationship, which is the supplier-power half of the story.

The method, in four steps

The analysis itself is short. Almost all of the difficulty lies in doing each step properly rather than in knowing what the steps are.

  1. Gather the evidence. Collect what can be counted: prices, visits, orders, disputes, the fee each channel takes, the share of revenue each one carries.
  2. Name the pressure. Say which force is acting and on which side of the firm. The framework earns its keep here or nowhere.
  3. Locate the fix. Put the pressure somewhere in the value chain, because that is what turns a diagnosis into a place where work can actually be done.
  4. Recommend one thing. Say what it changes, what it costs, and what you would watch afterwards to know whether it worked.

What separates a strong version of each step from a weak one is small and repeatable, which is why the two are worth seeing side by side.

You are writing the analysis for the hypothetical consumables supplier. The four moments below are where this piece of work usually goes wrong, and the weaker options are the mistakes people actually make rather than obviously silly ones.

Decision 1. You have a week with the supplier’s records before you write anything. What do you come back with?

  • Delivered competitor prices, basket abandonment, dispute counts, channel fees and the revenue split — the stronger call. Right. Each of these can be counted, each attaches to a force or to a value-chain activity, and each can be checked by somebody who doubts you. Evidence is what separates an analysis from an opinion.
  • A written summary of what the counter staff and the sales reps believe is going wrong — Staff judgement is worth having and will help you read the numbers later. On its own a list of beliefs cannot be checked, and it tends to describe the day rather than locate a pressure.
  • A review of the marketplace storefront, noting every feature the firm’s own site lacks — This produces a feature list, and a feature list quietly becomes the recommendation. You end up proposing technology because a rival has it rather than because a force demands it.
  • The whole order file exported, so that nothing at all is left out of the analysis — Completeness is not evidence. Without a question in mind you cannot tell which columns matter, and the work stalls in the data rather than in the argument you were asked for.

Decision 2. Your first draft says: visitors leave the site before paying, and the maker now sells the same cases direct. What is missing?

  • It names no force. Say which pressure each of those two observations is evidence of — the stronger call. Right. As drafted the sentence describes the day. Naming buyer power and disintermediation, and placing abandonment inside the value chain, is what lets a reader argue with you and what makes a recommendation follow from something.
  • Nothing. It states two observed facts, and an analysis should stay close to its evidence — Staying close to the evidence is right, but an analysis is more than the evidence. A description that names no pressure gives the reader nowhere to go and no way to test whether you are correct.
  • A stronger opening claim, such as calling this an existential threat to the firm — Force of language is not force in the framework sense. Escalating the wording adds urgency without adding a diagnosis, and it makes the whole piece harder to trust.
  • More figures, so add the abandonment share and the dispute count to that sentence — More numbers in the same sentence still leave it a description. The figures belong in the evidence; the sentence exists to say which pressure they are evidence of.

Decision 3. You have three candidate initiatives you like, and the brief asks for one. What do you write?

  • One initiative, with the force it answers, what it costs, and what you would watch — the stronger call. Right. A single recommendation can be costed, argued and later judged. The other two can be named in a sentence as deliberately not chosen, which is itself an analytical move rather than a hedge.
  • All three, ranked in order, so the owners can pick whichever suits the budget — Ranking looks generous and quietly hands the decision back to the reader. It also hides which pressure you were answering, and no single option ever gets costed properly.
  • The most ambitious of the three, since a bolder call shows a firmer grasp — Ambition is not a criterion. The recommendation has to answer the pressure the evidence showed, and a bold initiative aimed at the wrong force is worse than a modest one aimed at the right one.
  • A new customer application, because the marketplace has one and the firm does not — This is technology with no force behind it. If you cannot say which pressure the application relieves and where it sits in the value chain, the reader has no reason to accept it.

Decision 4. The last piece is a comparison of 150 to 200 words against a company you choose. Which draft does the work?

  • Name what that company does differently, then what the firm should do or refuse — the stronger call. Right. The comparison exists to produce a lesson for the firm you are advising. Naming the difference in the vocabulary of the frameworks is what makes it a lesson rather than a story about somebody else.
  • A short profile of the company, its history, its scale and its best-known work — This is the commonest failure of the whole piece. It reads well and says nothing about the supplier, and by the end the reader knows more about a company nobody asked you to advise.
  • A list of every difference between the two firms, so nothing relevant is left out — Length is not coverage. Most differences do not bear on the pressure you diagnosed, and burying the one that matters among twenty that do not is how a reader misses your point.
  • An argument that the firm should copy the company as closely as its budget allows — Copying ignores what your own evidence said about this firm, which holds ground the comparison company may not hold at all. A lesson is not an instruction to imitate.

The comparison summary

The last piece is a short written comparison, 150 to 200 words, against a company you choose. It is not a profile of that company. It is a lesson drawn back to the firm you are advising.

Three things make it work: pick a comparison with a real similarity or a real contrast; name what that company does differently in the vocabulary of the frameworks; and end on what your firm should therefore do, or deliberately refuse to do.

A summary that spends its 200 words admiring somebody else has answered a different question. Every sentence should be recoverable as advice to the firm in front of you.

Chapter vocabulary

Every term the chapter defines, written so it makes sense on its own, with an example beside it. Search by word, or filter by the objective the term belongs to. Watch for the pairs that sound alike and are not: a payment service and a cryptocurrency, a chargeback and a refund, the card security code and the card number, a copyright and a trademark.

4.5authorization
The check a card issuer runs before a sale is allowed to go through, confirming that the card has not been reported lost or stolen and that credit is available. It is easily mistaken for proof of identity, which it is not: an approval says the card is usable, not that the person using it owns it.For example: A stolen card number can be approved in a second and still produce a disputed charge weeks later, because nothing in the approval asked who was typing.
4.5blockchain
An indelible, decentralized public ledger to which transactions are added in groups called blocks, each block standing as proof of everything recorded before it. Because copies live on a large number of computers, changing an old entry would need agreement from a majority of them, and entries once written are not deleted.For example: Organizations well outside payments, from technology firms to banks, have tested the same structure for records that several parties must trust without trusting each other.
4.1business-to-business (B2B) electronic commerce
Electronic trade between organizations rather than with an end consumer, covering suppliers, manufacturers, distributors and wholesalers. Measured by revenue it is by far the largest form of electronic commerce, and most consumer-facing firms take part in it as buyers even though shoppers never see that side.For example: A clothing retailer buys fabric, packaging and freight capacity through supplier systems long before a single shirt appears on its own website.
4.1business-to-consumer (B2C) electronic commerce
Electronic trade between a business and the individual people who use what it sells. It covers online retailing, digital products and streaming services, and it includes the events leading up to a purchase and the customer service that follows it.For example: Buying a book from a large online retailer, together with the recommendations that led to it and the return that may follow.
4.5card-not-present transaction
A sale in which no physical card is handed over, so there is no imprint of the card and no cardholder signature. The merchant carries the risk if the charge is later disputed, and has to judge from data alone whether the person paying is the cardholder.For example: An order placed on a website at two in the morning, where everything the merchant knows about the buyer arrived through a form.
4.5card security code
The three-digit number printed on the back of a payment card, used as evidence that whoever is paying has the physical card in hand. It is not held in the magnetic stripe, and card company rules forbid a merchant from storing it after the sale.For example: A checkout page asks for those three digits and must then discard them, which is why a returning customer is asked for them again.
4.5chargeback
The reversal of a card payment by the issuer after the cardholder disputes it. The merchant normally loses the transaction amount, the merchandise already shipped, the processing costs and a fee on top, and a high rate of them can lead its bank to raise fees or close the account.For example: Vague shipping terms on a product page produce disputes that were never fraud at all, only a customer who expected something different.
4.5consumer protection law
Rules requiring sellers to deal with customers fairly, transparently and honestly. They matter more online because the buyer cannot handle the goods before deciding, and they often apply according to where the customer lives rather than where the seller is based.For example: A seller writes its refund terms to satisfy the strictest state it ships into, because that is the standard it will be measured against.
4.1consumer-to-consumer (C2C) electronic commerce
Electronic trade between individuals who are not in business together, generally carried out on a site that introduces them and handles the payment. No firm owns the goods being sold; the operator supplies the meeting place and the rules.For example: One person buys a piece of memorabilia from another through an auction site, and neither of them is a company.
4.5copyright
Legal ownership of created work such as text, photographs, music or software. An online business has two duties at once: not to publish work owned by somebody else, and to settle in advance who will own material an outside contractor creates for it.For example: A product page decorated with a photograph taken from another site is an infringement even though the file was free to download.
4.1crowdfunding
Raising money for a project by collecting small amounts from many people through a website, in place of one loan or one investor. It was among the first fintech categories to reach the public, and it bypasses the institutions that used to decide which projects got funded.For example: A designer collects enough small pledges to pay for a first production run without ever meeting a lender.
4.5cryptocurrency
A virtual currency issued by no central bank, recorded on a shared public ledger and transferred directly between parties. The common description of such transfers as anonymous is not accurate: they are pseudonymous, because the addresses carry no names while every transfer stays permanently visible and addresses can often be tied to people.For example: Bitcoin, the most widely used example, was launched around 2008 by a developer known only by the pseudonym Satoshi Nakamoto.
4.5cryptographic hash
A short fixed-length value calculated from a block of data, which changes completely if any part of that data changes. Chaining these values together is what makes a ledger tamper-evident. It is a different job from encryption, which hides contents; a public ledger is meant to be readable by everyone.For example: Altering one old transaction would change the value calculated over it and break every value calculated after it, which is how the alteration is noticed.
4.5decentralized peer-to-peer network
An arrangement in which thousands of ordinary computers running the same software do the work a central operator would otherwise do. There is no head office that closes for a national holiday, and no single machine whose failure stops the system.For example: A transfer is broadcast to the whole network and verified by whichever machines choose to check it, at any hour of any day.
4.5deceptive practice
Selling conduct that misleads a buyer, such as false advertising or untrue claims about a product’s quality, benefits or price. Laws in many countries prohibit it; in the United States such conduct is pursued at the federal level while other consumer questions are handled state by state.For example: A listing showing a struck-through price the item was never actually sold at is misleading whether or not any buyer complains.
4.1digital investment manager
A fintech service that builds and rebalances an investment portfolio through software rather than through a human adviser. It is one narrow service done cheaply, and increasingly the recommendations are produced with artificial intelligence.For example: Somebody with a few hundred dollars to invest receives an allocated portfolio from an application, at a fee no human adviser could work for.
4.5digital rights management (DRM)
A technological control that lets a publisher limit how digital media are copied, shared or played, including on which devices and how many times. Supporters say it protects sales; critics call it digital restriction management and argue that it inconveniences lawful buyers and can encourage the copying it was meant to prevent.For example: A game that can be activated only a fixed number of times becomes unplayable to its lawful owner after a new computer, while an unauthorized copy carries no such limit.
4.5digital signature
A value attached to a transaction proving it was authorized by whoever controls a particular address, without revealing the secret used to produce it. On a public ledger this is what establishes authenticity, because no bank is standing behind the transfer.For example: A payment is accepted by strangers around the world because the signature could only have been produced by the holder of the sending address.
4.5digital wallet
An application that stores payment credentials so a person can pay without retyping a card number or carrying the card itself. It stands between payer and merchant, so what the merchant receives is permission to charge rather than the card details.For example: Paying for a cup of coffee by holding a phone near the terminal, with the card number never leaving the wallet.
4.5digital watermark
An identifying mark buried inside a digital file, the electronic equivalent of the watermark placed on paper currency to deter counterfeiting. It does not prevent copying; it makes an unauthorized copy traceable back to the account that originally bought it.For example: Music files bought from one store carry the purchaser’s email address inside the file, invisible until somebody goes looking for it.
4.1direct-to-consumer brand
A firm that sells its own products straight to the people who use them, with no wholesaler or retailer in between. It is the clearest commercial result of disintermediation, trading a distributor’s reach for control of price, data and the customer relationship.For example: A mattress company selling only through its own site knows exactly who bought what, which a company selling through shops does not.
4.1disintermediation
The removal of the traditional parties in the middle — wholesalers, distributors, retailers — so that a producer reaches the end customer directly. The web made it practical at scale, and the same move later reached financial services, where fintech firms bypass banks and brokers.For example: Airlines selling seats through their own sites rather than through travel agents is the standard example.
4.1dynamic pricing
Setting a price that moves with demand instead of staying fixed on a published list. It captures more revenue for the seller when demand is high, and it is one of the practices customers most often name when they say a market has stopped feeling fair.For example: The chapter describes a ticketing company using it on popular concerts, with prices climbing while a queue of buyers waits.
4.5economic nexus
A tax connection to a place created by how much a seller sells into it rather than by having property or staff there. A 2018 United States Supreme Court decision established that crossing a threshold of sales can oblige a seller to collect that state’s tax with no physical location in it. Thresholds differ by state and change, so the specifics are a question for somebody qualified in that jurisdiction.For example: A small online seller shipping steadily into one state can cross its threshold in a quiet month and acquire a duty it never applied for.
4.1e-government
The use of information systems to give citizens, organizations and other agencies information about public services, and to let them interact with government online. It has the shape of electronic commerce without a commercial sale at the centre of it.For example: Filing a tax return over the internet rather than posting forms, which saves the filer time and the agency paper.
4.1electronic commerce (EC)
Transactions carried out over digital networks, covering the exchange of goods, services and money among firms, between firms and their customers, and between customers themselves. It reaches beyond the moment of purchase to the research before it and the service after it.For example: Its roots are older than the web: standard codes for transmitting cargo information by teletype were devised during the Berlin airlift in 1948.
4.1electronic marketplace
The digital trading space created once buying and selling moved onto networks, in which a practically limitless range of services, features and functionality can be offered. Its reach and scalability are why an online presence became a strategic necessity for most companies rather than an option.For example: A specialist seller working out of one small workshop can be found by buyers on other continents, which no physical shop window allows.
4.5false decline
A legitimate order a merchant refuses because its screening treated the customer as risky. It costs the sale and often the customer, and it never shows up in a fraud figure, which is why tightening the rules can look like success while quietly losing money.For example: A student ordering to a campus address while travelling abroad trips several risk signals and is turned away, though the card is genuinely theirs.
4.1fintech
Innovative technology that transforms financial services, usually delivered by firms that do one thing very well rather than the full range a bank offers. It has accelerated electronic commerce by simplifying payment, financing and investment, and it reshapes what customers then expect everywhere else.For example: The chapter’s examples run from crowdfunding sites to online-only banks, mobile payment processors, peer-to-peer lenders and automated investment services.
4.5fraud indicator
A characteristic of an order that experience associates with fraud, such as a mismatch between billing and shipping addresses, an email address of meaningless characters, or a burst of unusually large orders. These are signals published as guidance by a card network, not tests that establish anything: many of them describe very large numbers of entirely honest customers.For example: Overnight shipping on a big order raises the score, and it is also what somebody does when a birthday is tomorrow.
4.5fraud-screening service
An automated service that weighs an order against many variables and returns a measure of how risky it looks. It works better the more data the merchant collects at checkout, which is exactly the data collection that leads some shoppers to give up on the purchase.For example: Adding a phone number and a billing address to a checkout improves screening and loses some buyers in the same moment.
4.5identity fraud
The use of another person’s personal or financial details to make transactions in their name. The chapter reports a cost to Americans running to tens of billions of dollars in a single year; treat that as a snapshot of one country at one time, and learn instead why online sales carry so much of the exposure.For example: Card details lifted from one breached database are tested in small online orders somewhere else entirely.
4.5import duty
A charge a country levies on goods arriving from abroad, usually payable by the recipient when the shipment lands. It is one reason a cross-border order can cost more than the checkout total suggested, and it sits alongside any tax the buyer’s own jurisdiction charges.For example: A parcel ordered from overseas is held at delivery until the recipient pays a charge that no line of the original order mentioned.
4.5intellectual property
Creations the law treats as ownable, including written content, images, brand names and software. An online business has to work in two directions at once: not infringing what others own, and taking visible steps to protect what it owns before it loses revenue or brand distinctiveness.For example: A firm that never posts a copyright notice and never registers its name finds an imitator trading under something very similar.
4.5micropayment
A payment so small that ordinary processing fees would swallow it. Card networks charge a merchant a percentage of every sale, so a few cents cannot be collected profitably that way, which is one of the legitimate uses put forward for currencies whose transfer costs are negligible.For example: Charging a reader a fraction of a cent for one article is impossible through a card network and conceivable without one.
4.1mobile commerce
Buying and selling conducted from phones and tablets rather than from a desk. It is not simply a smaller screen: it changes where a purchase happens, how long a checkout can reasonably take, and which payment methods are practical.For example: A shopper compares prices while standing in a store aisle and completes the order before leaving the building.
4.5mobile payment service
A payment service built for paying in person from a phone or a small card reader rather than from a website. It offers an alternative to carrying cash or cards, and the transfers people now make to each other grew out of the same infrastructure.For example: A market stall takes card payments through a reader plugged into a phone, with no till and no merchant terminal.
4.5near-field communication (NFC)
A short-range radio technology, similar in spirit to Bluetooth, that lets a phone and a payment terminal exchange data when held close together. The stored payment method is charged without the card being handled, which is why contactless payment spread as quickly as handsets supported it.For example: Holding a phone above a terminal for a second completes the payment, and the phone never displays a card number.
4.1online marketplace
A site that hosts many independent sellers and brings its own audience to them. A seller trades margin and control for immediate reach, which is the opposite bargain from building a storefront and attracting every visitor alone.For example: A new brand lists on a large marketplace to reach buyers on its first day, and pays the operator a share of every sale for that.
4.1online-only bank
A bank that operates without branches, delivering accounts and payments entirely through software. Removing the branch network removes much of the cost that set the price of traditional banking, and it puts the whole relationship inside an application.For example: An account opened from a phone in a few minutes, with no signature, no appointment and no building to visit.
4.5payment service
An intermediary that holds a customer’s payment details and pays merchants on their behalf, so the sensitive information is given once to one party instead of to every seller. The service keeps the card data, the purchase history and the contact details, and does not pass them to the merchant.For example: One of the best known of these grew up alongside an online auction site and let strangers pay each other; the two firms were separated into independent businesses in 2015.
4.1peer-to-peer lending
An arrangement in which people lend money directly to other people or to small businesses through a platform, rather than depositing it with a bank that lends it on. The platform matches the two sides and prices the risk, and the money never becomes a bank deposit.For example: A borrower turned down by a bank is funded instead by a hundred individuals each putting up a small share of the loan.
4.5peer-to-peer payment
A transfer of money directly between two individuals through an application, with neither of them acting as a merchant. It grew out of mobile payment services and is now routine for the small settlements people once handled in cash.For example: Four friends split the bill for a meal on the spot, three of them sending their share to the one who paid the restaurant.
4.1platform business model
A business that earns its money by enabling transactions between other parties rather than by owning what is sold. Its value grows with the number of participants on each side, which is why an established platform is hard for a newcomer to displace.For example: A marketplace for handmade goods holds no stock at all and earns from the sales its buyers and sellers make to each other.
4.5point-of-sale (POS) system
The combination of checkout computers and inventory control that captures a sale as it happens and deducts the item from stock. Because the record is made at the moment of sale, a retailer can see what is moving and reorder before the shelf empties.For example: A bar code scan at a till both charges the customer and tells the ordering system that one more unit has left the shelf.
4.1price matching
A policy under which a physical retailer agrees to sell at a competitor’s advertised price. It is a direct answer to customers comparing online prices while standing in the store, and it protects the visit at the cost of some margin.For example: Two large chains invite shoppers to check prices in the aisle, betting they will buy on the spot and add something else to the basket.
4.5public ledger
A shared record of transactions that any computer may inspect and verify. Its openness is the point: with no central authority vouching for a transfer, correctness has to be something anyone can check, which is also the reason it cannot be private.For example: Every transfer an address has ever made can be read by a stranger years later, which is what separates pseudonymous from anonymous.
4.5radio frequency identification (RFID)
A tag that can be read wirelessly at a short distance, so items do not have to be scanned one at a time. In retail it would let a whole cart be totalled at once, but adoption is uneven and turns on whether an item is valuable enough to justify the cost of tagging it.For example: A cart of tagged goods priced in a second as it passes a reader, which is practical for coats and not for tins of beans.
4.5return and refund policy
The published terms on which a seller will take goods back and return the money. Many countries require the terms to be clear, understandable and prominently displayed, and a generous policy can raise revenue over time even though handling the returned goods is expensive.For example: One large retailer accepts returns freely, absorbing high shipping and restocking costs because buyers order more when the risk is reversible.
4.5risk score
A number a screening service assigns to an order to summarize how likely it looks to be fraudulent. It is an input to a decision rather than a verdict, and the merchant chooses the point at which an order is held, checked by telephone or refused.For example: The same score might be approved by a seller of low-value goods and refused by one shipping expensive electronics overnight.
4.5sales beacon
A small Bluetooth transmitter in a store that detects a nearby phone and triggers a real-time promotional offer. It brings the targeting familiar from online shopping into the aisle, and it raises the same tracking questions shoppers ask of websites.For example: An offer for the coffee counter appears on a phone as its owner walks past the entrance.
4.5sales tax
A tax on a sale that the seller collects from the buyer and passes to the authority. Online sales were long treated like mail order, meaning tax was collected only where the seller had substantial presence, and that rule has since changed. What is owed differs by jurisdiction and changes over time, so the specifics are a question for somebody qualified there.For example: Early online sellers chose where to place warehouses partly for the tax position it gave most of their customers.
4.5secure sockets layer (SSL) encryption
Protection applied to data moving between a browser and a website so that it cannot be read along the way. The padlock shown by the browser is the sign that it is in use, and it is the first thing to look for before sending anything sensitive.For example: A card number typed into a page with no padlock crosses several unknown networks in a form anyone on the path could read.
4.5self-checkout
A checkout the customer operates themselves, scanning and paying without a cashier. It saves the retailer labour costs and the shopper queueing time, and one variation lets items be scanned into the cart as they are picked up, leaving only the payment for the counter.For example: A grocery shopper scans items while walking the aisles and finishes the visit by tapping a card on the way out.
4.5shopping cart abandonment
The very common outcome in which a shopper fills a basket online and then leaves without buying. The chapter reports that more than half of online carts end this way, and the causes it lists include security concerns, impatience, a lengthy checkout and comparison shopping.For example: Adding one more required field to a checkout form measurably raises the number of people who never finish the order.
4.1showrooming
The practice of examining a product in person at a store and then ordering the same item online for less. The retailer pays for the building, the stock and the staff time while a different seller collects the sale, which is why physical retailers have had to find an answer to it.For example: A shopper handles three televisions, asks a member of staff which is better, and orders the winner from a phone in the car park.
4.1social commerce
Buying and selling that happens inside social platforms, where the recommendation, the discussion and the purchase sit in one place. It is one of the directions electronic commerce spread into once a transaction no longer needed a dedicated store around it.For example: A product is discovered in a friend’s post and bought without ever leaving the application it was seen in.
4.1storefront platform
A hosted service that lets a business build and run its own online store without writing the software. It gives the seller control of branding, pricing and customer data, and it leaves the hardest part — persuading anybody to visit — with the seller.For example: A small maker has a working store online in an afternoon, and then discovers that nobody yet knows the address.
4.5substantial presence
The older test for whether a seller owed a state’s sales tax: an office, a shipping warehouse or some other physical footing in that state. It is worth knowing because it explains where early online sellers chose to build, and because economic nexus has since added a second route to the same obligation.For example: A seller with a single warehouse collected tax from customers in that state and, for years, from nowhere else.
4.1third-party fulfillment
Paying an outside company to hold stock, pick orders, ship them and process returns. It converts a large investment in warehousing into a cost per order, which is how small sellers offer delivery speeds they could not build for themselves.For example: A brand working out of a rented unit ships next day nationwide because somebody else’s warehouse network is holding its stock.
4.5trademark
A name, logo or mark identifying whose goods or services something is. Online it is easy to infringe one without meaning to, including by placing somebody else’s protected name inside a domain name or a product listing.For example: A reseller registers a web address containing a manufacturer’s brand name and receives a legal letter rather than the traffic it hoped for.
4.5use tax
A tax a buyer may owe their own state on something bought elsewhere and brought in, when no sales tax was collected at the point of purchase. It exists so that buying across a border does not simply remove the tax, and it is widely unknown to the people who owe it.For example: Someone ordering an item from another country may owe their home state a tax that no part of the checkout mentioned.

The chapter says the entertainment industry argues these controls minimize sales losses by preventing unauthorized duplication, while critics say publishers are arbitrary in how they enforce them, that limits on devices and activations inconvenience users and can breed piracy, and that some businesses do very well without them.
  • B. For: locked files load faster on a registered device. Against: the encoding cost is high and rises with the size of the catalog — Loading speed is not part of the argument. The chapter frames the technology as a control on copying and distribution rather than as a performance feature.
  • C. For: the law requires publishers to restrict copying. Against: enforcement is impossible once a file has left the publisher’s servers — The chapter presents this as a commercial choice publishers make, not a legal requirement, and it names successful sellers who have chosen the other way.
  • D. For: watermarking each file identifies the buyer. Against: watermarks reduce audio quality enough for listeners to notice the difference — Watermarking is the chapter’s alternative for unlocked files, used to trace an illegal copy back to the original purchaser rather than to prevent playback.

23. 4.6 A hypothetical maker of wooden toys builds a storefront on a hosted commerce platform, and after three months has had forty visitors. Its founder concludes that the platform was badly built. What has the founder misread about the layers the supplement describes?

  • A. Nothing at all: a storefront platform is sold as a complete solution to selling online, so weak traffic is a defect in the product the maker paid for — The supplement separates the layers precisely because no single one of them covers the whole job of selling something online.
  • B. A storefront platform builds and runs the shop; attracting customers to it is a separate problem, which is why sellers turn to marketplaces — correct. Building and running your own online store still requires attracting customers to your website, which is a challenge for a fledgling business; that gap is what a marketplace with an existing customer base fills.
  • C. The platform layer includes fulfillment, so the founder should be measuring deliveries rather than visits during a first quarter of trading — Fulfillment is a separate layer in the supplement, handled by third-party providers managing warehousing, shipping and returns rather than by the storefront software.
  • D. Payment providers are the layer that brings demand, so the founder should change the checkout options before touching anything else — Payment providers reduce friction at checkout for buyers who have already arrived. They do not bring anyone to a shop that nobody has found.

24. 4.6 A hypothetical soap maker lists on a large marketplace and its orders multiply. A year later it wants to email those buyers about a new range, and finds that it cannot reach most of them. What was the trade it made?

  • A. It borrowed the marketplace’s audience and technology, and the marketplace kept the customer relationship that the audience produced — correct. A marketplace lends a seller its customer base and its technology, connecting a small seller with a very large audience at once; what it does not hand over is the relationship with the people who buy.
  • B. It bought advertising space, so the customers were rented for the length of the campaign and returned to the marketplace afterwards — Advertising buys attention for a period. A marketplace listing is a sales channel, and the limit here is on contact afterwards rather than on the length of a campaign.
  • C. It became a business-to-business seller, so the buyers are the marketplace’s customers and the soap maker’s single customer is the marketplace — The people buying soap are consumers. Paying a marketplace a fee does not turn a retail sale into trade between two businesses.
  • D. It disintermediated its own shop, so the marketplace is now the one route by which any of its products can reach a buyer — Disintermediation means removing a middleman. The soap maker added one, and nothing stops it from continuing to sell through its own site as well.

25. 4.6 A hypothetical seller of climbing holds packs orders in a rented garage and turns away a spike of demand every autumn, because it cannot pick and post fast enough. A third-party provider quotes a per-order fee higher than the seller’s own handling cost. When does that fee stop being simply an expense?

  • A. It does not, because a fee above the seller’s own cost per order is a worse deal on every single order it touches — Cost per order is not the whole comparison. The supplement points at speed, at scale, and at the orders the seller currently cannot accept at all.
  • B. When the provider agrees to hold the stock at no charge, since warehousing is the part of fulfillment worth handing over — Warehousing is one part of the service. The providers described also handle stock, shipping and returns, and speed of delivery is a large part of the value.
  • C. Once the seller has grown enough to build its own warehouse, at which point the fee becomes an investment in the eventual move — Building a warehouse is the significant upfront investment the arrangement is meant to avoid, so a fee paid now is not a deposit against one.
  • D. When it stops being a comparison of fees and becomes the purchase of delivery speed and capacity the seller cannot fund itself — correct. Third-party providers offer rapid delivery, warehousing, shipping and returns handling, and scalable logistics without significant upfront investment — which is what a garage cannot supply at the autumn peak.

26. 4.6 A hypothetical piano dealer finds customers playing instruments in its showroom and then ordering the same model online for less. It is weighing three responses: matching online prices, offering fitting and tuning that a website cannot, and putting sensors in the aisles to track how long shoppers linger. What does the supplement say about the third one?

  • A. Tracking is the strongest of the three, because measurement is what lets the other two responses be judged and adjusted over time — Measurement can be useful, but the supplement presents service and price matching as the answers to showrooming and treats in-store tracking as a separate question with objections attached to it.
  • B. Price matching and personal service address the price gap directly, while tracking raises a privacy objection the dealer has to answer openly — correct. Retailers answer showrooming with price-matching policies and superior, personal customer service, benefiting by selling additional products, while privacy advocates criticize the tracking of purchasing habits, so the dealer has to say plainly what is collected and why.
  • C. Tracking is equivalent to price matching, because both are ways of collecting more value from a shopper who is already inside the shop — The two do different work. One closes the price gap the shopper walked in with; the other gathers information about the shopper and needs consent to be defensible.
  • D. Tracking is required before price matching can be offered, since a dealer has to know which shoppers were comparing before it can match anything — A price-matching policy is published and applies to anyone who asks for it. It does not depend on identifying which shoppers have been comparing prices.

27. 4.7 A hypothetical bicycle shop’s written analysis opens with one line: sales are down, and customers complain about the website. The writer then moves straight on to a recommendation. Which step of the method has been skipped, and what would repair it?

  • A. It names no numbers, so setting last year’s revenue beside each complaint would repair it — Counting belongs to step one, whose stronger version is a delivered competitor price set beside your own shelf price. A number attached to a symptom is still a symptom.
  • B. It is written in the first person, so restating the two observations in a neutral voice would repair it — Voice is a matter of style. What is missing is the pressure itself, which is what the second step of the method exists to supply.
  • C. These are symptoms: step two asks which pressure is acting on the shop, and on what evidence — correct. Step two is to name the pressure and say which side of the firm it is acting on. The weak-against-stronger table sets “customers are leaving the basket” against “buyer power is high, because comparing prices costs the customer nothing”, and this analysis has written only the weak version.
  • D. It covers one force only, so a sentence about each of the other four would repair it — Four more sentences would still name no pressure a reader could argue with. The step is finished when a force is named with evidence behind it, not when five headings have been filled in.

28. 4.7 A hypothetical dairy’s analysis names two areas where a system would help: the website, and the online shop. Each description runs to a single sentence, and both of them point at the same ordering screen. What is the defect?

  • A. Two entries, one place: the chain has been located once, so nothing has been learned about the rest of it — correct. Step three is to locate the pressure somewhere in the value chain, and two names for one ordering screen locate it in a single place rather than in two.
  • B. The entries are too short, so expanding each of them into a paragraph would repair it without changing what they name — Length is not the failure here. A longer description of the same ordering screen would still point at a single place in the chain.
  • C. Neither entry is a system, so the writer should have named the software product it intends to buy for each of the two areas — Naming a product belongs to the recommendation. Locating is about where in the chain the pressure sits, which is settled before anyone decides what to buy.
  • D. Ordering is a support activity rather than a primary one, so it sits outside the part of the chain worth locating — Support activities carry pressures too: this supplement maps compliance duties into firm infrastructure. The category of the activity is not what makes this pair weak.

29. 4.7 A hypothetical optician’s analysis ends with three ideas — online booking, a loyalty scheme, and a stock system — and invites the reader to choose between them. Each idea is described in a phrase. What does the analysis need instead?

  • A. The same three ideas ranked by cost, so that the reader can see which is cheapest to start with and choose on that basis — Ranking still hands the decision back to the reader, and the fourth step of the method asks the writer to make the call and then stand behind it.
  • B. A fourth idea drawn from a different part of the chain, so that the reader has a wider set of options to weigh before deciding for themselves — Adding an option moves in the wrong direction. The difficulty is that no choice has been made, not that too few were listed.
  • C. The three ideas described at greater length, since the weakness is the phrase-length treatment rather than the number of ideas offered — Length would improve each description, but three described ideas with no recommendation among them is still a menu rather than a recommendation.
  • D. One initiative, with the pressure it answers, what it changes, what it costs, and what you would watch afterwards — correct. Step four is to recommend one thing and say what it changes, what it costs and what you would watch afterwards to know whether it worked. The table sets three uncosted initiatives against exactly that, and the two ideas you drop can be named as deliberately not chosen.

30. 4.7 A hypothetical bookbinder’s comparison section says that a very large retailer built a network of automated warehouses, and concludes that the bookbinder should build automated warehouses too. The section runs to ninety words. Name what is wrong with it.

  • A. The lesson only works at the larger firm’s budget and footprint, and the section sits well below the length the task sets — correct. A lesson that only holds at the larger company’s budget or footprint is not a lesson that transfers, and the comparison is set at a hundred and fifty to two hundred words, which ninety does not reach.
  • B. The comparison is with a real company, which the task does not permit, so an invented example of a similar size would have been safer — Nothing in the method turns on whether the comparison company is real. What has to work is the lesson drawn back to the firm you are advising.
  • C. The lesson is sound and the single defect is length, so extending the section past the lower bound would finish the job — Length is one of the two faults. A longer passage recommending warehouse automation to a bookbinder would still be recommending something out of reach.
  • D. The company chosen is in a different industry, and a comparison has to be drawn from the same trade to carry any weight — Industry is not what decides it. A comparison fails when the lesson does not transfer, and warehouse automation does not survive the move to a bookbinder’s scale and footprint.
