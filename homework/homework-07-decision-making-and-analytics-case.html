Scenario

A retailer holding four years of records it has asked almost nothing

Corwin Cycle Company is not a real company. It is invented for this assignment, the way the practice examples in the lecture modules are, and every organisation, figure and quotation in it is hypothetical.

You have been hired as an analyst at Corwin Cycle Company, a mid-sized regional retailer selling bicycles, e-bikes, parts and servicing. Corwin runs 14 stores, each with a workshop, and one webshop. Over the last three years it has lost share steadily.

Leadership is uneasy, and for an unusual reason. Corwin holds four years of sales, service and webshop records, and has asked them almost nothing: one margin spreadsheet opened once a quarter, and one prediction pilot that failed. Before it spends anything, the executive team wants to understand how information systems could strengthen Corwin’s competitive position and support its long-term strategy.

What the executives have told you

  • Five regional chains and about thirty independents sell the same ranges in the same area, and two direct-to-consumer brands now ship an assembled e-bike with a two-year home-service contract. Prices sit within a few percent and nobody is discounting heavily; between them the two direct sellers have taken about 6 percent of Corwin’s e-bike volume this year.
  • Renting a webshop and drop-shipping parts costs almost nothing. Four parts-only sellers started in the region this year and none of them holds any stock. Parts are 21 percent of Corwin’s revenue.
  • No single customer is more than half a percent of revenue and none of them negotiates. But a shopper can compare every price in the region from a phone, and 14 percent of the ninety daily enquiries are answered after the customer has already bought elsewhere.
  • One manufacturer is the only supplier certified for the battery packs in Corwin’s own-brand e-bikes, which are 34 percent of revenue and the highest-margin line in the business. It raised prices 9 percent and now requires orders a quarter ahead; moving to a different pack would mean recertifying the frame, which takes about a year and has been quoted at more than the range earns in one.
  • A bike-share scheme in the two largest towns passed 9,000 memberships against roughly 240,000 adults in those towns, and it meets the same need without anybody owning a bicycle at all.
  • Servicing cannot be bought that way. Workshop revenue is 19 percent of the total and the only line that is growing.
  • Corwin has never been the cheapest. It wins on having a bike ready to ride the day it was promised, and on a workshop that will still see that bike three years later.
  • The monthly pack is one revenue figure per store. When a figure moves, no manager can open it up to see which range, which region or which month is behind it.
  • The buying team has one spreadsheet model of margin. It is opened once a quarter, one number is changed, the answer is written down, and it is closed again.
  • 40,000 workshop notes a year are written as free text by the mechanic who did the job. Nobody has ever labelled one, and nothing reads them.
  • “Have you a medium gravel bike near the Ashford store on Saturday” arrives about 90 times a day. Writing a reply takes a coordinator about eight minutes, but the average enquiry waits 41 minutes before anyone reaches it.
  • Nobody can say which customers are drifting away. The one attempt reported 97 percent accuracy and then failed in use: it had been trained and tested on the same records, and no accuracy threshold had been agreed before the result arrived.
  • Two stores opened in the last three years. Both were sited on the cheapest available lease, and neither decision used a map of anything but rent.
  • The three longest-serving workshop managers, who between them settle most repair-or-replace calls, retire within eighteen months. None of their reasoning is written down anywhere.
Nothing here is drawn from a real company. If a detail sounds familiar for reasons outside this assignment, that is a coincidence — analyse this Corwin and only this one, using the evidence above plus any assumption you are willing to state in writing.

Before you start

Choose a company to compare against

Choose one real retail or e-commerce company to use as a point of comparison with Corwin. Take a close look at its competitive landscape, what it has done with technology, and the difficulties it is working through now. The goal is not admiration; it is to gather enough understanding to draw a clear parallel, and then to use that parallel to sharpen your view of where information systems would make the greatest strategic difference at Corwin.

Pick a company whose relevant practice is actually documented somewhere you can read. A famous company whose inner workings are undescribed gives you a name and nothing else, and a lesson that only works at that company’s budget or footprint is not a lesson that transfers to a regional chain.
Your tasks

Four tasks, one analysis

Work them in order. Each depends on the one before it, which is also the order a real analyst is obliged to work in: name the pressure before naming the fix, and name the activity before naming the system. Task 1 · Apply the five competitive forces

Evaluate the competitive pressures facing Corwin. One row per force. In each row say whether the force is weak, moderate or strong, and give the evidence from the scenario — or a reasonable assumption you state plainly — that supports that rating. Each row has to name something a reader could count, compare or verify: a share of revenue, a number of competitors, a switching cost, a price move. “A lot of competition” is not evidence, and it is not a different answer in each of the five rows.

Task 1b · Name the single strongest force

In two or three sentences, name the one force that puts the most pressure on Corwin and say why it beats the force you would rank second. Rating all five and naming none leaves the decision to your reader, and the decision was the job.

Task 2 · Use the value chain to find the opportunities

Using the value chain, identify at least two internal areas — primary activities or support bands — where information systems could create an operational or customer-focused advantage. They have to be two genuinely different activities, not one activity described twice: the second is what shows you scanned the chain instead of stopping at your first idea. It may turn out to be the alternative your recommendation was preferred over, or the place a single initiative lands a second saving — both are good answers, and the second is the better one when you can show it. For each of the two give all three of these:

  1. The activity, named in the value chain’s own words.
  2. The avoidable figure exactly as the exhibit prints it, so a reader can trace it. Describe the driver in your own words — it is the figure that has to be quoted, not the sentence beside it.
  3. One or two sentences saying what the weakness actually is, and what a system would change there.

Task 3 · Recommend exactly one IT initiative

Recommend one initiative, drawn from one of the areas you just named, that would directly support Corwin’s strategic goals. Do not list options and let the reader choose — one initiative is a decision, three are a wish list. Cover all six of these:

  1. What it does, concretely enough that somebody could cost it and later judge whether it shipped.
  2. What kind of system it is, named in the reading’s own words rather than by a product name.
  3. Which activity it belongs to, and where the saving appears. These are frequently not the same activity, and naming both is what answers the obvious objection.
  4. Which force it answers, named explicitly. It does not have to be the force you called strongest in Task 1b — the biggest pressure is not always the one worth spending on first — but if it is a different one, say why you are spending there instead.
  5. How it would run: built and hosted by Corwin, or bought as a service — and one sentence on what that choice costs you in control. (If you want to name a cloud service model, Module 3 covers them; neither reading for this case does, so it is not required.)
  6. How you would know it worked: the number as it stands today, the number you expect instead, and the date somebody should look at it.

Task 3b · Name the method, and settle what a generic answer leaves open

A recommendation can name a tool and still not show that you have read the chapter. Name the method, then answer the set that belongs to it — one set, not all four.

  1. A decision support model: the one input you can actually control, the constraint you are working inside, and one what-if question you would put to it in words a buyer would use.
  2. A predictive model: what one row represents, what the label is, how you hold data back to test it, and the threshold you fix before you have seen any result. The scenario says what happens when that last step is skipped.
  3. A generative assistant: which live source answers the question, what it does when it does not know, and who it escalates to.
  4. Knowledge management: whose judgement you are capturing, whether it is tacit or explicit, and how anyone checks a year later that it is still right.

Task 4 · The written summary, in 150 to 200 words

This is the second deliverable, and it is one piece of writing rather than a set of answers. Explain how the two companies complement each other and what Corwin can draw from the comparison. All five of these have to be in it:

  1. The company you chose, named, and what it actually did — the mechanism, not its size or its reputation.
  2. What makes it worth comparing even though the two are almost certainly a different size.
  3. The one lesson that would still make sense at Corwin’s scale, and one thing from that company you would explicitly decline to copy.
  4. How your Task 3 initiative aligns with what Corwin has chosen to compete on. The scenario says what the firm sells itself on; say how the initiative serves that.
  5. What it would mean if the money went somewhere that did not.

Stay inside the band, and give the source for what you say the company did — a link or a reference, written underneath and not counted in the word total. A mechanism nobody can check is a story.
