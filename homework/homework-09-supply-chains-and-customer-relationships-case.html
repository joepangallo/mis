Scenario

A distributor whose troubles all sit on the two boundaries it does not control

Pentworth Workplace Company is not a real company. It is invented for this assignment, the way the practice examples in the lecture modules are, and every organisation, figure and quotation in it is hypothetical.

You have been hired as an analyst at Pentworth Workplace Company, a mid-sized regional distributor of office and workplace supplies — paper, print consumables, desks and chairs, catering and cleaning — selling to businesses through a webshop, a telephone desk and 240 contract accounts, served out of four depots. Over the last three years it has lost share steadily.

Leadership is uneasy, and for an unusual reason. Almost nothing that hurts Pentworth happens inside Pentworth. Its trouble sits on its two boundaries: the suppliers above it, who are shown nothing but its orders, and the customers below it, who exist four times over in four systems. Before it spends anything, the executive team wants to understand how information systems could strengthen Pentworth’s competitive position and support its long-term strategy.

What the executives have told you

  • Three regional distributors and two national ones sell much the same catalog in the same towns. Contract business is won on three-year tenders that fall due at different times, so only about a third of the market is contestable in any one year, and list prices sit within a few percent of each other.
  • A reseller webshop that drop-ships from a wholesaler can be trading inside a weekend, and five of them appeared in the region this year holding no stock at all. None of them can deliver next day out of local stock or sign a service level, so they reach only the single-order online business, which is 14 percent of Pentworth’s revenue.
  • Contract accounts are 61 percent of revenue across 240 accounts, the largest of them 4 percent. Every one of them retenders, and 19 of the 240 did not renew last year. Moving, though, means a customer republishing its own approved catalog and reapproving every user who buys from it, which takes a purchasing team about a month.
  • Three manufacturers hold 48 percent of the lines Pentworth stocks, and one of them raised prices 7 percent in the spring. Substitutable equivalents exist for most of those lines, and two of the four depots already stock them.
  • The work is being done a different way, and it needs less of what Pentworth sells. Print volume across its contract accounts fell 34 percent in four years as documents stopped being printed at all; hybrid working left desks empty and took the catering and cleaning lines down with them; and four of Pentworth’s ten largest accounts moved to a managed print service that buys consumables direct from the manufacturer and bills by the page.
  • Next-day delivery out of local stock, and a named person who answers the phone, cannot be drop-shipped. The service and account-management side of the business is 13 percent of revenue and the only line that is growing.
  • Pentworth has never been the cheapest. It wins on next-day delivery out of a depot twenty miles away, and on one named account manager who knows what a customer ordered last time.
  • Pentworth sends about 1,900 purchase orders a month to 86 suppliers by email and PDF attachment, and every one of them is keyed in again at the other end. Eleven of those suppliers have asked for an electronic link and been told there is not one.
  • A March promotion raised customer demand for one paper line 18 percent. The order Pentworth placed on its wholesaler rose 41 percent, and the order that wholesaler placed on the mill rose 86 percent. Forty-two pallets of that paper were written off four months later.
  • No supplier is shown anything but Pentworth’s orders. Nothing about what Pentworth expects to sell is shared with any of them, and lead times run from three weeks on paper to eleven weeks on the imported furniture line.
  • There is no plan, only a reaction. One buyer purchases from last month’s sales in a spreadsheet, and nothing anywhere in the firm states what it expects to sell next quarter.
  • A customer exists four times. The webshop, the contract catalog, the delivery system and the ledger each hold their own record, and about 2,900 of 11,400 business customers appear in more than one of them. A customer who moves premises has to be asked to change the address in each.
  • 54 percent of calls to the trade desk are settled on the first contact. The rest call back an average of 2.4 times, and nobody has ever counted what the other 46 percent costs to handle.
  • Account managers keep their accounts in their own spreadsheets. Two left last year; their notes left with them, and 9 of their 61 accounts did not renew at the next tender.
  • Nobody can say which contract account is about to leave until the tender documents arrive. The firm has never put the question in a form that anything it owns could answer.
Nothing here is drawn from a real company. If a detail sounds familiar for reasons outside this assignment, that is a coincidence — analyse this Pentworth and only this one, using the evidence above plus any assumption you are willing to state in writing.

Before you start

Choose a company to compare against

Choose one real retail or e-commerce company to use as a point of comparison with Pentworth. Take a close look at its competitive landscape, what it has done with technology, and the difficulties it is working through now. The goal is not admiration; it is to gather enough understanding to draw a clear parallel, and then to use that parallel to sharpen your view of where information systems would make the greatest strategic difference at Pentworth.

Pick a company whose relevant practice is actually documented somewhere you can read. A famous company whose inner workings are undescribed gives you a name and nothing else, and a lesson that only works at that company’s budget or footprint is not a lesson that transfers to a regional chain.
Your tasks

Four tasks, one analysis

Work them in order. Each depends on the one before it, which is also the order a real analyst is obliged to work in: name the pressure before naming the fix, and name the activity before naming the system. Task 1 · Apply the five competitive forces

Evaluate the competitive pressures facing Pentworth. One row per force. In each row say whether the force is weak, moderate or strong, and give the evidence from the scenario — or a reasonable assumption you state plainly — that supports that rating. Each row has to name something a reader could count, compare or verify: a share of revenue, a number of competitors, a switching cost, a price move. “A lot of competition” is not evidence, and it is not a different answer in each of the five rows.

Task 1b · Name the single strongest force

In two or three sentences, name the one force that puts the most pressure on Pentworth and say why it beats the force you would rank second. Rating all five and naming none leaves the decision to your reader, and the decision was the job.

Task 2 · Use the value chain to find the opportunities

Using the value chain, identify at least two internal areas — primary activities or support bands — where information systems could create an operational or customer-focused advantage. They have to be two genuinely different activities, not one activity described twice: the second is what shows you scanned the chain instead of stopping at your first idea. It may turn out to be the alternative your recommendation was preferred over, or the place a single initiative lands a second saving — both are good answers, and the second is the better one when you can show it. For each of the two give all three of these:

  1. The activity, named in the value chain’s own words.
  2. The avoidable figure exactly as the exhibit prints it, so a reader can trace it. Describe the driver in your own words — it is the figure that has to be quoted, not the sentence beside it.
  3. One or two sentences saying what the weakness actually is, and what a system would change there.

Task 3 · Recommend exactly one IT initiative

Recommend one initiative, drawn from one of the areas you just named, that would directly support Pentworth’s strategic goals. Do not list options and let the reader choose — one initiative is a decision, three are a wish list. Cover all six of these:

  1. What it does, concretely enough that somebody could cost it and later judge whether it shipped.
  2. What kind of system it is, named in the reading’s own words rather than by a product name.
  3. Which activity it belongs to, and where the saving appears. These are frequently not the same activity, and naming both is what answers the obvious objection.
  4. Which force it answers, named explicitly. It does not have to be the force you called strongest in Task 1b — the biggest pressure is not always the one worth spending on first — but if it is a different one, say why you are spending there instead.
  5. How it would run: built and hosted by Pentworth, or bought as a service — and one sentence on what that choice costs you in control. (If you want to name a cloud service model, Module 3 covers them; neither reading for this case does, so it is not required.)
  6. How you would know it worked: the number as it stands today, the number you expect instead, and the date somebody should look at it.

Task 3b · Name the component, and settle what a generic answer leaves open

A recommendation can name a system and still not show that you have read the chapter. Say which extended component you are switching on — supply chain management or customer relationship management — and then answer the set that belongs to the part of it you chose, plus the last item, which applies whichever you named. One set, not all four.

  1. Supply chain planning: which plan you are making — demand, production or stock, or distribution — the forecast it starts from and who outside Pentworth is allowed to see it, and the lead time it has to respect. The brief says what happens up the chain when a supplier is shown nothing but orders.
  2. Supply chain execution: which of the three flows — product, information, financial — you are fixing first, what replaces the emailed purchase order across the firm’s boundary, and what a supplier would be able to see that it cannot see today. Say which of the eighty-six you would start with and why.
  3. Operational CRM: which touch point it serves — sales, marketing or support — which of the four records becomes the one record of a customer and who inside Pentworth owns it, and what the person on the trade desk does differently on the morning it goes live.
  4. Analytical CRM: the one question you are putting to the customer data, what would count as an answer somebody could act on before a tender rather than after it, and who acts on it. A number nobody is obliged to do anything about is a report, not a decision.
  5. Whichever you named: say whether that component is internally or externally focused, and name who outside Pentworth has to agree before it works at all. This is the item that separates the two halves of the chapter, and the answer is not the same for both.

Task 4 · The written summary, in 150 to 200 words

This is the second deliverable, and it is one piece of writing rather than a set of answers. Explain how the two companies complement each other and what Pentworth can draw from the comparison. All five of these have to be in it:

  1. The company you chose, named, and what it actually did — the mechanism, not its size or its reputation.
  2. What makes it worth comparing even though the two are almost certainly a different size.
  3. The one lesson that would still make sense at Pentworth’s scale, and one thing from that company you would explicitly decline to copy.
  4. How your Task 3 initiative aligns with what Pentworth has chosen to compete on. The scenario says what the firm sells itself on; say how the initiative serves that.
  5. What it would mean if the money went somewhere that did not.

Stay inside the band, and give the source for what you say the company did — a link or a reference, written underneath and not counted in the word total. A mechanism nobody can check is a story.
