Objective 8.1 · Part 1

Core business processes

When you order a book online and it arrives two days later, you do not wonder which department checked your card, which one found the book on a shelf, or which one printed the label. You care that the right book arrived at the right price. The work still had to be divided among departments, and you still judged the whole thing as one event.

Customers buy outcomes, not departments

Companies are traditionally organized around five functional areas: marketing and sales, supply chain management, manufacturing and operations, accounting and finance, and human resources. Each owns well-defined functions such as sales forecasting, procuring raw materials and components, planning and budgeting, or recruiting and training. Drawn on a chart, they look like five independent silos.

They are not independent. Most business processes cross the boundaries of those functions, so it helps managers to think in processes seen from a customer’s point of view rather than in departments. Three such processes are common to most organizations, and you should be able to name them before anyone mentions software:

  • Order-to-cash covers everything from a customer’s order to the money landing in your bank account.
  • Procure-to-pay covers acquiring goods from outside vendors, from negotiating terms to settling the vendor’s invoice.
  • Make-to-stock and make-to-order cover producing the goods, and differ over whether production waits for an order.

Order-to-cash: from a stranger to settled cash

Selling products or services is the main way a business generates revenue, so this is the process that pays for the others. At a large online retailer you create an account, add items to a cart, enter shipping and billing information, and submit the order. The seller confirms your address, checks your card, assembles and ships the order, and charges you.

How long each step takes depends on the sale. A convenience store sale runs in seconds, and several of the subprocesses are not needed at all — creating a customer record, for one — although the clerk may still ask for a ZIP code. A large business-to-business order can take months or years and involve many more steps. The sequence itself is stubbornly similar. Put it in order before you read on.

Each subprocess exists because something can go wrong without it, and the position of a step matters as much as the list:

  • Create a customer record so later shipments, invoices, and disputes all attach to one identifiable buyer.
  • Check creditworthiness before you promise anything, because goods shipped to a bad payer are a loss rather than a sale.
  • Create the order to fix what was bought, at what price, and on which delivery terms.
  • Check and allocate stock so that two orders are not promised the same unit on the same shelf.
  • Pick, pack, and ship accurately, since a wrong item comes back as a return, a refund, and a dispute.
  • Prepare and send the invoice promptly, because the clock on collection does not start until the bill arrives.
  • Collect the payment to close the transaction, which is the step that finally turns all this work into cash.

An ineffective order-to-cash process is expensive in ordinary ways. Manual input of order information causes errors, and so do suboptimal picking and shipping. Those errors turn into a high rate of disputes to resolve, collection that drags, and eventually customers who defect. An effective one creates customer satisfaction, speeds up collection, and feeds clean inputs into business intelligence and machine learning applications.

Procure-to-pay: the same transaction from the other side

To sell anything, you first have to buy something. A large retailer manages tens of thousands of suppliers, places purchase orders, receives the products, allocates warehouse space, receives and pays invoices, and handles the disputes that follow. Those activities together are the procure-to-pay process.

It has five subprocesses, and each one is a place where money leaks when it is handled loosely:

  • Negotiate price and terms first, because everything downstream is priced by the agreement you sign here.
  • Issue the purchase order so the vendor holds a written, numbered commitment to ship against.
  • Receive the goods and record what actually arrived, not what the paperwork said would arrive.
  • Receive the invoice and match it against the purchase order and the receipt before approving it.
  • Settle the payment on the agreed terms, which is how a vendor decides whether to favor you next time.

Run this badly and error rates rise in purchase order and invoice processing, while the firm fails to develop close relationships with preferred vendors. Cost per transaction climbs, disputes multiply, and the most favorable conditions go to somebody else. Run it well and you obtain better conditions, reduce transaction costs, and earn the goodwill that comes from filling your own customers’ orders on time. Make-to-stock and make-to-order: push and pull

The third core process produces the goods. In the make-to-stock process, goods are produced based on forecasts and stocked in a warehouse, and customer orders are then filled from inventory. This is a push-based approach: you build first and push the product out to buyers. Mass-produced goods such as television sets and home appliances are typically made this way.

In the make-to-order process, raw materials, subcomponents, and accessories are still procured based on forecasts, but actual manufacturing does not start until an order is received; in extreme cases, even design and engineering wait. This is a pull-based approach, and it suits highly customizable or expensive low-volume goods such as custom furniture or commercial aircraft. The steps differ:

  • Make-to-stock runs procure inputs, schedule production, produce, run quality control, and stock the finished product.
  • Make-to-order runs process the sales order, design and engineer, procure inputs, schedule, produce, inspect, and ship.
  • What waits for the order separates them: make-to-stock fills orders from inventory already in the warehouse, while make-to-order waits for the order before manufacturing starts.

Where one firm’s sale is another firm’s purchase

Together, these core processes create supply chains. A supply chain resembles a river: raw materials start at the source and move downstream toward the end customer, transformed at each step. The term names the whole collection of companies and processes involved, from extracting raw materials through intermediate components to final production and delivery.

Here is the part worth remembering. Within a supply chain, one company’s sales-related processes overlap with the downstream company’s procurement-related processes. The same shipment is order-to-cash on one side of the loading dock and procure-to-pay on the other. Two firms, two processes, one event.

  • B. It is order-to-cash for both firms, because goods and cash each move. — Just one firm is collecting cash on this shipment; the other one is releasing it instead.
  • C. It is procure-to-pay for both firms, because each one settles an invoice. — Just one firm pays for this shipment, and the other is the party issuing the invoice.

What this case was really about. Two lessons sit in this case. The position of a subprocess is part of the process: a credit check that runs after the truck leaves protects nobody, and stock allocated before an order exists blocks a sale that is real. And the same transaction wears two names, because the mill’s order-to-cash is Ridgeway’s procure-to-pay. That overlap is exactly where one firm’s supply chain joins the next one’s.

The word “chain” is a little misleading. Companies procure from many upstream suppliers, who work with their own suppliers in turn, and downstream the products move to many different customers, so the flow is better described as a supply network. Because they depend on a steady source of key supplies, most companies seek long-term business-to-business relationships with a limited number of carefully selected suppliers rather than one-time deals, and they assess those suppliers on trustworthiness, commitment, and viability as well as on price and quality.

Learn the three processes as sequences, not as lists. A step performed out of position — shipping before the credit check, allocating stock before the order exists — costs real money. And the process that ends at your loading dock starts again as somebody else’s.

Objective 8.1 · Part 2

The value chain, and value systems between firms

A business process crosses departments, so somebody has to say which department does the work and which one makes the work possible. The value chain is that map. Once you can place an activity on it, you can say what a system is actually for, and who feels it on a Tuesday afternoon when that system is wrong.

Core activities and support activities

A value chain is the set of an organization’s activities that add value to the end product, with information flowing through the functional areas that carry the business processes. The framework splits those activities in two, and that split is the whole point of it.

Core activities are performed by the functional areas that process inputs and produce outputs. Support activities are the activities that enable the core ones to take place. A shipping clerk moves a box toward a customer. A recruiter does not, but without the recruiter there is no clerk.

Five core activities run left to right across the framework, in this order:

  • Inbound logistics receives and stocks the raw materials, parts, and products arriving from suppliers and shippers.
  • Operations and manufacturing transforms those stocked inputs into outputs, through order processing or the assembly of finished goods.
  • Outbound logistics mirrors inbound logistics and handles distribution: picking, packaging, and shipping the finished end product.
  • Marketing and sales covers presales work, including marketing literature, communication with customers, and the pricing of goods.
  • Customer service covers post-sales work, from order status and invoices through repairs, questions, and tracked service requests.

That last group is worth naming carefully: customer service activities are a family of work rather than one desk. The split from marketing and sales is a timing split, not a topic split. Both groups talk to customers all day. Marketing and sales activities happen before the money changes hands; customer service begins once it has. A new price list is presales work; a question about an order that already arrived is post-sales work.

The five that make the five possible

Support activities sit beneath the whole core row, which is why the framework draws them as layers under all of it rather than as steps in a line. Each one answers a different question about how the core work gets done at all.

  • Administration orchestrates day-to-day operations through the processes and decisions that span organizational functions and levels.
  • Firm infrastructure is the buildings, machinery, and IS infrastructure components that everything else is implemented on top of.
  • Human resources covers employee management: hiring, interview scheduling, payroll, and benefits, because primary activities need people.
  • Technology development is the design, selection, and development of the applications that support the primary business activities.
  • Procurement receives, approves, and processes requests for goods and services, then coordinates the actual purchase of them.

Two placements people get wrong

The first is procurement against inbound logistics. Buying is not receiving. Procurement approves the request, combines it with other departments’ requests to earn a volume discount, and places the purchase order, so it is a support activity. Inbound logistics opens the crate, counts what came, and stocks it, so it is a core activity. The same box of parts touches both, a week apart.

The second is firm infrastructure against technology development. Technology development is where an application gets designed, selected, and built. Firm infrastructure is the buildings, machinery, and network the finished application runs on. Neither of them is an application serving a customer: once the order entry screen goes live, the work it carries belongs to a core activity.

Value systems: where one chain ends, the next begins

Information can be streamlined inside one company and across company boundaries too. When firms connect their internal value chains so that information flows from one chain into the next, the result is a value system. Three firms in a row form one; add suppliers, partners, and consumers and it grows complicated quickly.

A supply chain moves physical goods the way a river moves water, from a source toward a destination. A value system moves information along the same course, and its upstream and downstream information flows are named for who produced the information, not for the direction the paperwork travels:

  • Upstream information flow is information a company receives from another organization standing further back along the value system, such as the confirmations and shipping notices a supplier sends it.
  • Downstream information flow is information a company produces itself and sends along to the next organization in line, such as the delivery dates it passes to a distributor or a retailer.

A company in the middle does both. It receives information from its supplier, processes that information through its own value chain, and passes information downstream to its distributors and customers. Any information feeding into a company’s value chain counts as part of the value system, whether its source is another chain or an end consumer.

Objective 8.2 · Part 1

Why standalone applications stopped working

No firm sat down and chose to run on nine disconnected systems. Each one was bought for a good reason, by a department with a real problem, in a year when nobody was asking what would happen once the ninth arrived.

How a firm ends up here

Companies began using information systems by fulfilling the needs of a particular business activity in a particular department. Payroll needed to issue paychecks. Accounting needed a ledger. So the firm purchased proprietary software from different software vendors, or built department-specific software, one problem at a time.

Those systems focused on the specific needs of individual departments. They were not designed to communicate with the other systems in the organization; they are, in effect, speaking different languages. A system like that is a standalone application.

Standalone applications are not failures. They let a department conduct its daily business activities efficiently. The trouble starts the moment somebody in one part of the firm needs information from another part of the firm.

Three terms describe the situation most firms are actually in:

  • Standalone application serves one department's needs and was never built to exchange data with the rest of the firm.
  • Legacy system names an application at or beyond the end of its useful life that the business still depends on daily.
  • Best-of-breed describes running independent systems that are each the strongest in their own area, chosen deliberately to keep the firm agile.

What the separation costs

Consider order entry. If the applications for inbound logistics and operations are not integrated, personnel may have to open two separate applications to verify that components are in inventory before the order can be processed. Picture it as rocks in a river: information is generated by one activity and does not flow through to the next.

To use data held in separate standalone systems, the data must be reentered from one system to the next, by typing it again, copying and pasting, or downloading it into a spreadsheet. The alternative is to have a third system consolidate it, which adds a system to run rather than removing the split.

Put one number on the retyping. If a clerk reenters 40 order lines a day and each takes 30 seconds, that is 20 minutes a day, or about 7 hours across a 21-day work month, spent moving data the firm already had.

Joining the systems properly is not cheap either. Applications running on different computing platforms are difficult to integrate, so IS managers end up knitting together a hodgepodge of discordant proprietary applications with custom interfaces, and that integration is typically very costly.

The damage lands in four places you can name out loud:

  • Redundancy means the same fact is stored in two systems, so the firm pays twice to enter, store and update it.
  • Inconsistency follows when one copy is updated and the other is not, and two departments then quote two different numbers.
  • Lost time accumulates as staff reenter data and open a second application instead of doing the work they were hired to do.
  • Compliance exposure grows because demonstrating conformance with standards, rules or regulations takes integrated data the firm cannot readily assemble.

What an enterprise system changes

An enterprise-wide information system, or enterprise system, is an integrated suite of business applications for virtually every business process, allowing a company to integrate data across functional areas on a company-wide basis.

The change that matters is not the size of the suite. Rather than storing data in separate places throughout the organization, an enterprise system uses an integrated database as a central repository common to all users. One place holds the order, the inventory figure and the invoice.

That central database answers the multiple-platform problem directly, and a common user interface lets personnel share information seamlessly, no matter where the user sits or which department the user belongs to.

Objective 8.2 · Part 2

Internally and externally focused systems

A purchase order leaves your building and arrives somewhere else as a sales order. The document did not change. What changed is who has to agree with it. That question — who has to agree — is what separates an internally focused system from an externally focused one, and it is not the same question as where the software runs.

Inside the firm, information accumulates

Internally focused systems support functional areas, business processes, and decision making within an organization. Picture the work as a series of links in a chain. At each link, people add value through the work they perform, and new, useful information is generated as a by-product of doing it.

Follow one order. Order entry creates the order record. Fulfillment reads that record and produces a packing list. Shipping reads the packing list and produces the invoice. Information begins accumulating at the point of entry and travels forward. Nobody retypes it, and each step inherits what the step before it settled.

Four things about that flow matter more than they look:

  • Every link adds information, which is why the invoice ends up knowing more about the order than order entry ever did.
  • Every link trusts the one before it, so a wrong address typed at order entry is printed, packed, and then shipped.
  • Everyone who must agree works here — sales, the warehouse, accounting — all of them inside one organization’s boundary.
  • Managers read the same trail, which is why an internally focused system supports decision making and not only the work itself.

Across the boundary, somebody else has to agree

Externally focused systems streamline communications and coordinate business processes with customers, suppliers, business partners, and others operating outside the organization’s boundaries. A system that communicates across those boundaries is called an interorganizational system. Its purpose is to move information out of one company’s operations and into another’s.

Those flows run two directions. Upstream, supply chain management applications integrate the value chains of business partners, coordinating suppliers, production, and distribution. Downstream, customer relationship management applications handle promoting and selling, service after the sale, and long-term relationships. Later sections take each of them apart.

Where the server sits does not decide any of this. A payroll package hosted by an outside vendor is still internally focused, because the only people who must agree on a paycheck work for you. A terminal bolted to your own loading dock that confirms receipts into a supplier’s system is externally focused, because the supplier has to accept what it says.

A firm needs both

Internally focused systems serve internal operations across the whole organization very well. They are not necessarily designed to carry information past the firm’s boundaries, and global networks of suppliers and customers make that gap expensive. Component parts are made on one continent, fabricated on a second, and assembled and shipped to customers everywhere.

Customers also have more options than they used to. They want products customized to their own needs and they expect a higher level of service, and if you cannot deliver it a competitor will. Sharing information across the boundary is how a firm adapts quickly: if buyers begin demanding an extra component, the sales system can pass that signal straight to component suppliers, and the change gets managed instead of discovered.

Packaged or custom

Packaged software, also called off-the-shelf software, is written by third-party vendors for the needs of many different users and organizations. It suits standardized, repetitive tasks such as word processing, payroll, or preparing taxes. It can be quite cost effective, because the vendor spreads development cost across everyone who buys it.

Custom software is designed and developed exclusively for one organization and can accommodate that firm’s particular needs. It costs much more, because the firm bears all of it — time, money, and people. When something changes, the firm maintains the change itself. With packaged software the vendor makes the change and distributes a new version.

Either route raises a question about the work itself first, and the chapter gives that question a name. Business process management is a systematic, structured approach in which people critically examine, rethink, and redesign business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in something measurable, such as quality, cycle time, or cost. It is not steady incremental improvement; it is radical redesign, and the chapter warns that adopting an industry’s best practices can cost a firm the unique processes its advantage rested on.

So the question a manager actually answers is narrower than it first sounds:

  • Can the package meet the requirement? If it can, the argument is usually over, because somebody else already paid to build it.
  • If it cannot, what is the gap worth? Run a cost–benefit analysis on the custom route rather than settling it by preference.
  • Who carries the next change? A vendor spreads a new release across its whole customer base; a custom build has exactly one customer to fund it.
  • Is the process genuinely unique? Unique to the firm can justify custom work, while merely familiar to the firm cannot, and habit is easy to mistake for advantage.
  • What will the redesign itself cost? Rethinking a process is a project in its own right, so that effort belongs in the estimate beside the software rather than after it.

What integration makes possible, it also makes visible

The chapter stops here to raise an ethical question, and it belongs exactly here. Everything that makes an enterprise system valuable — one repository, one record, one coherent view of a thing — works just as well when the thing being viewed is a person.

This is the question of data consolidation: bringing records that used to sit apart into one place, where they can be read together. The chapter treats the gain and the cost as one subject, because a single view of a person is produced by exactly the move that makes a single view of an order possible.

The chapter’s first example is Amazon, which consolidated buyers and sellers into one source for household goods, electronics, books, media, reading devices, and digital assistants. The benefits are genuine: convenience, purchase from any device, and integration across products and services. Shopping history and buying preferences are used to help you find what you want.

The chapter then adds a wearable that monitored activity levels, health, body composition, and even emotional state, with that data kept apart from the shopping account by design — and notes that insurance companies had already begun working out how to harness such information. Its second example is Tencent’s WeChat, used in China to message, book medical appointments, pay utility bills, order food, and take payment as a shopkeeper.

Access to WeChat can be restricted: for a censorship violation, for being connected with the “wrong” people, or for reasons inside the platform’s own algorithms that are never explained. For a person locked out, “everything” becomes almost nothing. The chapter ends with two open questions rather than answers, and with the trade-off sitting under both of them:

  • What must a firm weigh when it offers everything, from selling household goods to monitoring somebody’s health and emotional state?
  • How far should legislation go in regulating how consolidated personal data may be used, given how much power sits with the consolidator?
  • Consolidation cuts both ways, trading convenience against narrower consumer choice, exposure of private information, and the cost of being on the wrong side of it.

Ask who has to agree, and the internal-or-external question answers itself. Ask what the record needs in order to do the work, and the ethical question gets easier as well — the field you collected because collecting it was easy is the one you will have trouble explaining later.

Objective 8.3 · Part 1

ERP core and extended components

A customer calls a distributor and asks one question: can you ship 900 units by the 12th, and what will they cost? Answering it takes the warehouse, the production schedule, and the customer's unpaid balance. In a company running three separate systems that is three phone calls and most of an afternoon. An enterprise resource planning system exists so that it is one screen.

What integration actually means

An enterprise resource planning (ERP) system replaces standalone applications with modules built on a common database and similar application interfaces, serving the entire enterprise rather than portions of it. Data sitting on older legacy systems is converted into one large, central repository.

Do not read too much into two of the three words. Resource and planning are partly misnomers, inherited from the manufacturing planning packages these systems grew out of; they do far more than plan or manage resources. The word carrying the meaning is enterprise.

Integration is not several copies of a figure kept in step by a nightly job. It is one record, written once, read by every module that needs it. Inventory data is available not only to inbound logistics and operations but also to accounting, sales, purchasing, and customer service.

That difference shows up in ordinary work rather than in the architecture diagram:

  • One version of a figure means the quantity sales quotes and the quantity the warehouse picks are the same number.
  • A central repository gives personnel accurate, up-to-date information without waiting on a nightly export between two systems.
  • A shared interface keeps screen layouts and menus comparable across modules, so a transfer between departments is not a retraining project.
  • Configuration, not copying adapts packaged software to how this company works, since the same product is sold to many different companies.

The three core components

ERP core components support the important internal activities of the organization for producing its products and services. The chapter names three of them, and each one owns a different kind of record.

  • Financial management supports accounting, financial reporting, performance management, and corporate governance, including credit limits, billing, and incoming payments.
  • Operations management simplifies, standardizes, and automates inbound and outbound logistics, product development, manufacturing, and sales and service.
  • Human resource management supports recruitment, assignment tracking, performance reviews, payroll, and the regulatory requirements that come with employing people.

The three are not equals in the value chain. Operations management enables the core value chain activities, which are the making and the moving of the product. Financial management and human resource management are associated with the activities that support those core activities.

Where the price quotation lives

Here is the placement most students get wrong, so learn it deliberately. A price quotation is an operations management function. The chapter files it with the sales and warehouse operations that component covers, not with financial management and not with customer relationship management.

The order-to-cash process at a wholesale distributor splits cleanly once you read it that way:

  • Operations management handles price quotation, stock allocation, picking, packing, and shipping, which are sales and warehouse operations.
  • Financial management handles the credit limit check, the billing, and the processing of the incoming payment.

Notice that the two components interleave inside one process. An order does not pass from operations to finance once and stop; it crosses back and forth between them. That is the argument for a shared database, stated in a single process.

A real application is usually mixed. The screen a sales representative opens to build a quote may look like a sales tool, carry customer history in from the extended components, and price the line out of operations. One screen can still draw on several modules, because the records behind its fields are owned in different places.

One further warning, about names. Vendors preconfigure their modules into industry solutions and then label them as they please, so the same function can appear under two different names in two systems. Work out what a module does with the data before trusting what it is called.

  • C. Human resource management, since the representative writing the quotation is on the payroll. — Payroll records the representative as an employee and has no bearing on pricing an order.

What this case was really about. Operations answered the date and financial answered the terms, and neither answer was usable alone. The honest quote is 900 units by the 12th with a deposit, a partial shipment, or a raised limit, because 990 units are available but only $8,600 of credit is. One shared record made that a single call rather than an afternoon of them.

Sort by the record, not by the job title of the person holding the phone. Core components own what happens inside the company, extended components own what crosses its edge, and the price quotation is an operations promise no matter who delivers it.

Objective 8.3 · Part 2

Choosing, installing, and living with an ERP

Buying the software is the short part. The long part is everything around it: deciding which system, deciding how much of it to change, settling several thousand small rules about how the company works, and getting people through the first month after the switch. This section follows that arc, from the reason these systems record so much to the drop in output that usually follows go-live.

Compliance is why the system records what it records

Beyond improving business processes, an ERP system improves and eases an organization's ability to implement audit controls and comply with government-imposed regulations. Compliance with mandates such as the Sarbanes-Oxley Act rests on implementing and documenting internal controls, procedures, and processes. ERP systems are designed to include an abundance of control features that can mirror the way the organization actually works.

Two of those features explain a great deal of ordinary friction at work:

  • Controlling access decides who may see or change a given record, so an approval cannot be granted by whoever happens to be sitting at the desk.
  • Segregating duties splits a task across job functions, so the person who enters a supplier invoice is not the person who releases its payment.

An ERP will not answer every regulatory requirement by itself. It has still been a central strategy for organizations working through the legal, regulatory, and supply chain mandates they live under.

One suite, or the best of each

A best-of-breed approach uses a suite of independent systems, each the strongest in its own area. Plenty of firms have built successful businesses that way, because a modular set of parts keeps them agile and quick to respond when the business environment changes. An enterprise-wide information system takes the other road, integrating data across functional areas in one shared database behind a common interface.

The honest objection to best of breed is arithmetic rather than principle. Integration is built and maintained between pairs of systems, and pairs multiply faster than systems do. Four systems have 6 possible pairs. Eight systems have 28. Every connection you actually build is a custom interface that somebody maintains, and each one has to survive the next upgrade at either end of it.

Neither road is automatically right. Three questions decide it:

  • How many seams you will owe counts the pairs of systems that must exchange data, not the systems themselves.
  • Where the advantage actually lives asks whether a distinctive process earns real money or is simply the habit the staff grew up with.
  • Who repairs a broken seam names the team or vendor on the hook when a nightly transfer fails and the warehouse opens at six.

Vanilla, configured, customized

The features and modules an enterprise system arrives with out of the box are the vanilla version. Configuration is not optional: the system has to be set up to reflect the way this organization does business and the business rules it follows. That means countless decisions about hundreds or thousands of database tables, and thousands more about the processes themselves.

Those configuration decisions sound small and are not:

  • What a customer identifier looks like fixes how every future record is matched, merged, and searched for.
  • When a bill is considered overdue drives the reminder letters, the credit holds, and the aging report finance reads each month.
  • What counts as the standard shipping method sets the default on thousands of orders nobody will stop to review.

Deciding all that needs someone who knows how the company really runs, which is why organizations hire experienced business analysts or outside consultants to help with configuration work.

Customization is a different thing, and it is often discouraged. It means programming company-specific modules, or changing the vanilla application itself. The reason for the caution is a bill that arrives again and again: a customization has to be reprogrammed when a new release is implemented, because later releases of the software do not carry your earlier changes forward. That is costly to redo, not impossible, and the cost lands at every release rather than once.

The next move is the dangerous one. Rather than re-fit the custom modules to the new release, many organizations stay on the old version so their custom features keep working. That leaves known flaws unpatched in a system that touches sales, inventory, and payroll.

The lines nobody quotes

Total cost of ownership is everything the system costs to acquire and then to keep, not just what the invoice says. It is the figure a business case needs, and many companies underestimate it and go over budget as a result.

Acquisition costs are the ones printed on the proposal: software licenses and maintenance, technical implementation, hardware. The costs that push these projects over budget are the ones estimated last, or not at all.

Four of them come up again and again:

  • Travel and training for personnel covers the hours people are paid to learn the system rather than to produce anything with it.
  • Ongoing customization and integration recurs at every release, which is what makes it an operating cost instead of a one-time project cost.
  • Business process studies pay for finding out how the work is really done before the software is told to assume something else.
  • Project governance funds the steering, reporting, and decision-making that keep a multi-year project from quietly drifting off course.

Leave them out of the estimate and you get unexpected budget increases, delayed timelines, and angry management. Put a figure on them instead.

What makes an implementation succeed

Horror stories about failed ERP implementations abound, and many of them are the same story: costlier and more time-consuming than anyone envisioned. Four recommendations raise the odds.

  • Secure executive sponsorship, because executives control the resources and can authorize the changes in how the company works.
  • Get help from outside experts to define the functionality before a vendor is chosen, rather than leaning on the vendor's own salespeople.
  • Thoroughly train users, the most overlooked, underestimated, and poorly budgeted expense in the entire plan.
  • Take a multidisciplinary approach by putting mid-level managers, IS staff, consultants, and end users on the implementation team.

Training earns that warning. An enterprise system is harder to learn than a standalone one, because a user has to absorb an unfamiliar interface and an unfamiliar set of business processes at the same time. Once the consultants leave, the staff do the work themselves.

Limitations, and the first month

These systems have real limits, and the one the chapter names is lock-in. An ERP typically requires the organization to modify various business processes, and once it is implemented the company is virtually locked in: further change is difficult, which costs flexibility and agility exactly when something outside the firm shifts. That is the real price of one integrated suite, and it is why the best-of-breed argument does not simply lose.

Then there is the dip. Many companies see a dramatic drop-off in productivity once the system goes live, together with dissatisfied users who would rather do the work the familiar way. Training people before go-live, and giving them room to practice, is what shrinks it.

Read that dip carefully, because two wrong readings are on offer:

  • A dip is not proof the system was the wrong choice. It is the expected cost of new screens and new processes landing on the same morning.
  • A dip is not proof of nothing either. If output stays flat for weeks and errors keep climbing, something is wrong past the learning curve.

Objective 8.4 · Part 1

Supply networks, B2B, and the bullwhip effect

A shopper buys one extra package of paper towels. Six weeks later a chemical plant four steps away schedules an unplanned weekend shift. Nobody along that route behaved foolishly. This section explains how a small change at the customer end turns into a large change at the raw-material end, and what firms exchange to keep it small.

Where the network fans out

Earlier in this chapter the supply chain stopped at a single loading dock, where a seller and a buyer recorded one shipment under two process names, order-to-cash and procure-to-pay. That join is the unit of everything below. This objective is about what a few hundred of those joins build when you look at them together.

The further out you look, the more firms are involved. A producer buys subassemblies from its Tier 1 suppliers, who buy components from Tier 2, who buy raw materials from Tier 3. The sourcing diagram for one product can start with five firms at the outer edge feeding four, feeding three, feeding a single plant.

Depth is the easy part. What makes the structure behave unlike a line is the shape of the links between one tier and the next:

  • Each supplier feeds several customers, so one small producer’s output ends up inside more than one component and more than one finished product.
  • Each firm buys from several suppliers, which is why a purchasing manager works a list of vendors rather than following a single line backwards.
  • The downstream side fans out as well, because finished products go to many distributors, retailers, and end customers rather than to one waiting buyer.
  • Nobody sees much past their own tier, because a firm deals with the suppliers it contracts with and rarely knows who supplies them.

  • The seller’s order-to-cash process and the buyer’s procure-to-pay process describe the same events from opposite sides of one invoice.
  • When the two firms exchange the same structured document, neither side retypes what the other already recorded, so neither side mistypes it.

Those links are many-to-many rather than one-to-one, so the interrelated parties moving these materials are described more accurately as a supply network than as a chain. The distinction is practical rather than cosmetic.

In a line, one supplier’s fire stops one product. In a network it can stop several at once, and a producer often does not learn that two of its product families share a Tier 3 firm until the shipment fails to arrive.

Exchanging the data

Transactions conducted electronically between businesses in a supply network, with no end consumer involved, are business-to-business electronic commerce. They carry proprietary information. An order for a particular component can hint at what a firm is about to build, so the channel has to be private as well as quick.

Three mechanisms do most of that work:

  • Electronic data interchange (EDI) is computer-to-computer communication with no human intervention, following standards set by recognized standards bodies.
  • XML uses tags to label the data inside a document, so the receiving application reads a number as an item number rather than a quantity.
  • Portals are access points through which a screened business partner reaches secured information held inside another firm, usually across an extranet.

EDI once ran over dedicated telecommunication networks that only large corporations could afford. The internet made the same exchange economical, so small and mid-sized firms can now take part. That lowered the cost of the line.

The larger saving is elsewhere. When both applications agree on what each field means, a purchase order can enter the seller’s system without a person reading it and typing it again. Nobody rekeys, so nobody mistypes, and the order is ready the minute it arrives.

  • Cut the buffer to zero and ask the plant to hold the same stock for you. — The units still exist, but they now sit eight days away from the customer who is waiting for them.
  • Raise the buffer to 900 units so that the question does not come back. — Two weeks of cover ties up capital, and rebuilding it makes your own orders lumpy for the plant to read.

Decision 4. Week 12. The retailer plans a two-week promotion and expects to sell 700 units a week while it runs.

  • Wait for the larger orders to arrive and react to them as they come. — You would learn about the promotion during its first week, and the plant would learn during its second.
  • Raise the standing buffer to cover any promotion the retailer might run. — A permanent buffer for an occasional event costs storage in every week of the year, promotion or not.
  • Get the dates and volume now, and build 520 units before it starts. — the stronger call. The gap is 260 units a week for two weeks. Planned in advance it is production; discovered late it is a shortage.
  • Ask the retailer to spread the promotion across its normal weekly orders. — Spreading a real 520-unit requirement over ordinary weeks guarantees empty shelves while the promotion is running.

The bullwhip effect

Every firm that forecasts demand adds a safety buffer so it does not run out of stock. That is sensible in isolation. The problem is that forecast errors and safety stocks multiply as they move up the network, so a small fluctuation in demand for a finished product becomes a large fluctuation in demand for the parts and raw materials farther upstream.

The image is a bullwhip. A tiny flick of the wrist at the handle produces a large movement at the far end. That is the bullwhip effect, and its cause deserves to be stated precisely.

The amplification is caused by information, not by anyone behaving irrationally. Each tier reacts to its own order book, because that is the only demand it can see. No tier upstream is looking at what customers actually bought.

Two remedies follow directly from that diagnosis:

  • Integrated business processes let a firm coordinate the whole network so each tier plans against consumption instead of against orders.
  • Simulation with machine learning algorithms lets a firm model the effect of a demand change before committing production capacity.

Objective 8.4 · Part 2

Planning, execution, visibility, and the ledger question

A supply chain management system does not run on good intentions. It runs on plans somebody wrote down, on flows somebody keeps moving, on numbers somebody can see, and on data somebody actually captured. This section takes those four in order, and it ends with the technology that gets promised the most and, on its own, delivers the least.

Plans first, then motion

Supply chain planning is the development of resource plans that support producing goods and services efficiently and effectively. It happens before anything moves. Each planning process ends in a named plan the rest of the chain works from, and those plans are interconnected: change the demand forecast and the sourcing plan changes with it.

Planning modules generally support four processes, and each one produces a document you can point to:

  • Demand planning and forecasting examines historical data, promotions, and point-of-sale figures to produce the overall demand forecast.
  • Distribution planning works out how finished goods reach distributors and customers, including warehousing, invoicing, and collection, and produces the transportation plan.
  • Production scheduling coordinates materials, equipment, labor, testing, and packaging into a workable sequence, and produces the production plan.
  • Inventory and safety stock planning balances holding costs against the service level you promised, and produces the sourcing plan with its supplier terms.

The forecast horizon follows the stability of the data. When historical demand is steady, plans can cover a longer period. When demand jumps around, the window has to narrow, because a long plan built on unstable history is a confident guess. Planning also never finishes. As new data arrives, the plans are updated.

Two well-known arrangements live or die on how good that shared signal is. Just-in-time ordering sizes each order so parts arrive exactly when production needs them, which keeps almost nothing in storage but leaves no cushion when a delivery slips. Under vendor-managed inventory the supplier watches your stock levels and sales data and refills your shelf itself, against service levels the two of you negotiated in advance. Both hand a partner more of your data, and both stop working the moment that data goes stale.

Execution moves three flows

Supply chain execution puts the planning into motion. It is the collaboration of suppliers, producers, distributors, and customers, and it manages three flows at once. Watch the direction of each one, because direction is where the distinction usually gets lost.

Each flow carries something different along the same chain:

  • Product flow moves goods from supplier to production, on to distribution, and finally to the person who consumes them.
  • Information flow moves orders, fulfillment records, billing, and delivery status, and it travels up or down the chain as needed.
  • Financial flow moves payments and the terms attached to them, including payment schedules and ownership of goods held on consignment.

Product flow is the one that surprises people. Goods mostly travel one way, but an effective system also supports reverse logistics, the processes for receiving products back from the point of consumption. Returns, refunds, replacements, recycling, and the proper disposal of hazardous material all run backwards along the same chain. A high-volume online retailer such as Amazon has to handle that flow cheaply, or returns eat the margin on the original sale.

Information flow has one goal worth naming: removing paper. When orders, fulfillment, billing, and consolidation are shared electronically, the saving is not just paperwork but time and money. Because the data sits in a central database, every partner reads the same current numbers.

Partners reach that data through named front doors, and the naming is what students reverse. A portal is named for who walks through it, not for who owns it:

  • Supplier portals are owned by the buying company and automate what it does with its many suppliers, from registering interest through ordering and payment.
  • Customer portals are owned by the selling company and let its business customers review products, enter orders, and get service on what they already bought.
  • Business-to-business marketplaces are run by a third party instead, bringing many buyers and many sellers together rather than serving one firm.

Efficiency against effectiveness

Supply chain effectiveness is the extent to which your supply activities meet the requirements of the external partners involved. Supply chain efficiency is the extent to which you optimize the use of resources in those activities. Chasing one alone produces either excessive cost or unmet needs, so the design is a set of trade-offs in procurement, production, and transportation.

The trade-off shows up as concrete choices, and your competitive strategy decides which side you lean toward:

  • Leaning effective means more inventory, multiple sources, general-purpose plants with spare capacity, more warehouses, and faster delivery.
  • Leaning efficient means a single source, less inventory, special-purpose plants with little slack, fewer warehouses, and longer delivery times.

A low-cost provider leans efficient. A firm competing on superior customer service leans effective. The system lets you price the trade one component at a time. If a hurricane threatens a shipment, you can simulate the delay, then decide whether air freight, a temporary second supplier, or a substitute part costs less than the contract penalty for missing the promised date.

Sustainability inside the same decisions

Green supply chain management applies the same areas you already know: green planning, green procurement, green distribution, and green logistics. Products can be engineered at the planning stage to use recycled material or to be recyclable at the end of their life. Better demand and supply planning cuts excess production.

Procurement can favor sustainable sources or give suppliers an incentive to change. Shipping can use cardboard rather than Styrofoam, reuse packaging, run alternative-fuel vehicles, or group shipments so fewer trips are needed. Nike is the chapter's example, aiming to double its business while halving its impact by sourcing reclaimed materials and using digital knitting and 3D printing to cut waste.

The structural point matters more than the examples. A green choice is usually an inventory, sourcing, or routing choice. It lands in the same modules and gets measured with the same indicators as every other supply decision.

Seeing the chain, then scoring it

Supply chain visibility is the ability to track products as they move and to foresee external events. Knowing where a shipment sits lets you expedite it and keep a sale. Knowing where a supplier's factory sits lets you ask whether an approaching typhoon will reach it. Some firms even track when key suppliers' labor contracts expire. Sharing at that level takes real trust between partners.

Sensors make visibility affordable. Telematics units report truck position, temperature and humidity sensors confirm that sensitive goods stayed in range, and a tamper alert can fire on its own. Supply chain analytics then uses key performance indicators to monitor sourcing, planning, production, and distribution, so a purchasing manager can name the supplier who keeps missing promised dates and spread the next order across two.

The ledger question

A blockchain is a distributed ledger on which transactions are recorded as linked blocks across many computers, which makes an existing record hard to alter after it is written. Two supply chain applications get the most attention: smart contracts, which execute a transfer automatically once a condition is met, and tracing a product back to its source, such as diamonds from mine to store or produce from farm to shelf.

The chapter then spends a long paragraph on what the ledger does not fix:

  • Trust moves rather than disappears, because instead of trusting people you now trust software that is harder to read than a person.
  • Hand-keyed entries stay as good as the person keying them, so a grower using a forbidden pesticide can still file an honest-looking record.
  • Automatic capture moves the trust again, because you are then relying on the sensor manufacturer and the software vendor, and virtually no software is free of bugs.
  • Irreversibility cuts both ways, since an executed transaction cannot be undone and there is no middleman left to appeal to.
  • Smart contracts still need auditing, and reviewing code is considerably harder than reviewing a policy written in plain English.

Say this one out loud before the case: a ledger cannot supply a field nobody captured. If the farm identifier was never recorded at the collection point, writing that same gap to a tamper-resistant chain makes the gap permanent, not visible. Garbage in, garbage out survives every new technology.

Objective 8.5 · Part 1

CRM strategy and its components

With the changes the web introduced, in most industries a company’s competition is simply a mouse click away. A disappointed customer usually does not complain first. They leave, and the alternative is already open in another tab. That is why keeping the customer you have is generally worth more than closing the sale in front of you.

Keeping a customer is worth more than the next sale

In today’s highly competitive markets, customers hold the balance of power. If the service they receive disappoints them, they have many alternatives readily available. The reverse is also true. When a company satisfies its customers and solves their problems, those customers become less price sensitive. Leveraging and managing the relationship is as important as developing the product.

Marketing researchers have found that winning back a customer who has gone elsewhere can cost many times what it costs to keep a current one satisfied. So companies work the relationship in three directions at once:

  • Widen means attracting new customers, which is the expensive direction and the one most firms think of first.
  • Lengthen means keeping existing profitable customers satisfied so the relationship continues instead of quietly ending.
  • Deepen means transforming a minor customer into a profitable one by selling more of what actually fits them.

Customers are not only end consumers. In business-to-business trade the customer is another company, and B2B commerce is many times larger than the business-to-consumer kind. To work any of the three directions, a company has to know who its customers are and weigh each one’s customer lifetime value, the worth of the whole relationship rather than of this month’s order. Deepening a minor customer pays only when that figure says the effort is worth it.

CRM is a strategy, not a purchase

A customer relationship management (CRM) system supports that work, and the chapter is blunt about what CRM is. It is not simply a technology but a corporate-level strategy to create and maintain lasting relationships with customers, through reliable systems, processes, and procedures, by concentrating on downstream information flows. Downstream means toward the customer.

Applications focused downstream have three objectives: attract potential customers, create customer loyalty, and portray a positive corporate image. Installed software delivers none of those on its own. A successful CRM strategy requires enterprise-wide changes:

  • Policies and business processes have to reflect a customer-focused culture rather than a poster in the break room.
  • Customer service needs key metrics that measure quality and satisfaction, plus process changes that improve the experience.
  • Employee training has to give marketing, sales, and support one consistent focus on service and satisfaction.
  • Data collection, analysis, and sharing must cover prospecting, sales, and support so the whole experience is tracked and shared.

There is a prior question too. Does this company need a comprehensive CRM system at all? The closer an organization sits to the end customer, and the larger its number of customers, the more value a CRM system provides. The strategy also has to weigh the ethical and privacy concerns of the customer data it collects.

The four components, and the job each one does

A comprehensive CRM system comprises four primary components. Read each as a job to be done, and notice that all four depend on one integrated system capturing data from retail stores, websites, social media, call centers, and the other channels a company uses.

  • Sales force automation supports day-to-day selling: tracking opportunities, managing the sales pipeline, processing and tracking orders.
  • Marketing automation manages campaigns and nurtures leads, deciding who is contacted, with which offer, and how often.
  • Customer service and support handles cases and supplies knowledge bases, so an issue can be resolved on first contact.
  • Analysis and reporting studies customer behavior and perceptions of quality, price, and satisfaction to segment customers and monitor performance.

Objective 8.5 · Part 2

CRM in practice, and where it goes wrong

You have been on the receiving end of these systems for years. The birthday discount, the email that arrives an hour after you abandon a full cart, the ad that names the thing you looked at once — each one is software acting on a record of you. This part turns the system around and looks at it from the inside: what it changes about the work, what it lets a firm conclude about a person, and where that crosses a line.

What changes for the people using it

Sales force automation supports the day-to-day work of selling: order processing and tracking, accounts, contacts, opportunities, and a record of each customer’s history and preferences. The change people notice first is less paperwork. The change that matters more is that the work stops living in one person’s head, so it can be handed over, reviewed, and taught.

Without an integrated view of the pipeline, two salespeople in the same firm can chase the same contract without knowing it. In a company with thousands of sales staff spread across several countries, nobody discovers that by walking down the hall.

The same record serves five people differently:

  • A salesperson loses fewer hours to paperwork and can hand an account over without its history walking out the door.
  • A sales manager sees each person’s pipeline, the ratio of orders to calls, the cost of acquiring a customer, and where deals stall.
  • A marketing manager learns which channel and which message a segment actually answered, and can route a new lead to the right salesperson.
  • A new hire is trained against the record rather than against whatever the person who left happened to remember.
  • A customer stops repeating the same account number to three different people in one afternoon.

First-contact resolution is a workload number

The help desk and the call center have widened into something else. A customer engagement center handles service requests, complaints, returns and information requests across whichever channel the customer prefers — the phone, the web, a self-service page, the firm’s social accounts — rather than making everyone queue in one line. Adding a channel is the easy part. Ending cases is the part that has to be measured.

A service desk reports two figures that look related and are not. Contacts handled counts how much work came through the door. First-contact resolution counts how often a case ended on the first try.

The second one multiplies. An unresolved case does not go away. It returns as a second contact, and that contact often produces a third. Ending a case the first time removes the follow-ups as well as the call. Hiring another agent adds capacity to the queue and leaves the arrivals exactly where they were.

This is why a chatbot placed in front of a desk can make the number worse. Give it something to answer from — a knowledge base, account status, the order record — and it closes cases itself or hands a person a case already understood. Give it nothing, and it collects a name and a problem and passes both along: one more contact per case, none of them resolved.

Three “service improvements” move work without ending any of it:

  • A new channel with nothing behind it gives the customer a second place to ask the question nobody has answered yet.
  • A faster greeting shortens the wait before the same case is escalated, which the caller experiences as one more hold.
  • A deflection target can be met by making a person harder to reach, which comes back later as an angrier second contact.

Buy the capability, not the logo

Enterprise software splits roughly two ways. Broad suites cover finance, people, manufacturing and customer work in one place, trading some fit for that coverage. Specialists do one job very well — campaign management, supply planning, a small firm’s sales pipeline — and leave you to connect them to everything else.

Which names sit in the front rank moves around, so read a system by what it does. Two capabilities now cut across all of them. Analytical tools model customer behavior — segments, retention, price sensitivity — and deliver the result to managers as dashboards. Generative tools help tailor the message to a new lead and shorten the time before a customer hears back.

Four questions separate a capability you need from a demonstration you enjoyed:

  • Which processes must it run? Name them as processes your firm already performs, not as features from the vendor’s list.
  • Whose data does it need? A model of customer demand runs on order history, and somebody has to keep that history clean.
  • What does the learning curve cost? Familiar interfaces and tight integration with daily tools cut training time, which is a real line in the budget.
  • What happens to the output? A forecast nobody is accountable for acting on is a report, not a capability.

What a firm may conclude about you

Firms want what the chapter calls a 360-degree view of the customer. They tie together what you handed over — the loyalty sign-up, the address, purchase and contact history, clicks on the site — with what is publicly posted on your social profiles. Machine learning then merges the records it judges to belong to the same person. Everything the firm concludes from that merged record rests on the match being right.

The chapter is blunt that not everyone views this positively. The two objections are that it invades customer privacy and that it makes coercive selling easier. A profile built from past behavior can file you in a category you would take exception to, and it keeps you there until something contradicts it.

Personalization also has a ceiling. The chapter offers Amazon’s recommendations as its example of personal service that works, and a message you can trace back to something you actually bought is easy to accept. Somewhere past that the message knows too much. Over-personalization is the point where a tailored message stops being service the customer welcomes and becomes surveillance they resent, and it backfires on the firm that paid for it.

Before acting on something the system inferred, ask four things:

  • Would the customer recognize where this came from? A purchase they made reads very differently from a profile assembled about them.
  • What happens when the inference is wrong? Someone has to be able to correct it, and being wrong should cost the customer little.
  • Does this serve the customer or only the sale? Answering the question they asked is not the same as pricing them.
  • Would you say it out loud? A sentence that would be strange spoken by a clerk in a shop is strange in an email.

Social media monitoring sits on the same line. The firm identifies and assesses the volume and sentiment of what is being said about it, its products and its brand. Most customers will never fill in a survey, but they will post when they are delighted or furious.

Then it has to decide whether to speak. Stepping into a discussion to answer a product question nobody else has answered is service. Praising your own product while appearing to be an ordinary customer is deception, and the chapter names it as such. That is why social work needs written policy: when to step in, how many posts to answer, and who the firm says it is when it speaks.

Application 8.6

Enterprise systems through the five forces and the value chain

You can now say what an ERP, an SCM system and a CRM system do. That is a description. A manager is paid for the next step: naming the pressure the firm is actually under, and saying which system relieves it. This section is that step.

Application supplement. This section applies two frameworks you met earlier in the course to this chapter’s material. It is not one of the chapter’s own objectives and it introduces no new framework. Every firm, order book and figure in it is hypothetical, invented for practice.

The two frameworks, in the course’s words

The five forces describe the pressure on a firm’s profits from outside it. A force is not good or bad. It is strong or weak, and your job is to say which, and on what evidence.

  • Rivalry among existing competitors is the contest among sellers already inside your industry, showing up in price, distribution and service.
  • Threat of new entrants is the chance that firms not competing with you today will start tomorrow, bringing added capacity.
  • Threat of substitutes is a different kind of product or service meeting the same underlying need, usually from an industry you do not watch.
  • Bargaining power of buyers is your customers’ ability to push back on your terms, through concentration or the ease of walking away.
  • Bargaining power of suppliers is the ability of whoever supplies your inputs to decide how the relationship runs, and it reaches you as cost.

The value chain answers a different question. Not what presses on the firm, but where inside it value is created. Five primary activities carry the product, and five support activities carry the firm.

  • Inbound logistics, operations and outbound logistics are the spine: receiving inputs, turning them into the product, and delivering the result.
  • Marketing and sales, and service are the customer-facing pair, covering the reason to buy and everything that happens after the money moves.
  • Procurement and technology development are two of the five supports: deciding what to buy and on what terms, and building the applications the primary work runs on.
  • Human resources is a third, covering hiring, interview scheduling, payroll and benefits, because no primary activity happens without the people who perform it.
  • Administration and firm infrastructure are the remaining two, and the framework counts them separately: decisions that span functions and levels, and the buildings, machinery and networks the rest is implemented on.

A recommendation that does not land on one of those ten activities is a recommendation nobody can act on.

Two confusions worth settling now

The first is substitutes against new entrants. Look at what the customer ends up with. If they stopped buying the product at all and met the need another way, that is a substitute. If they bought the same product from somebody new, that is entry.

Whether the seller owns a factory is not the test. A newcomer renting capacity by the month is still an entrant if it ships what you ship. A well-funded firm selling something that makes your product unnecessary is a substitute, however it is built.

The second confusion is about suppliers. A supplier is not only a firm that sends invoices. Anything your work depends on is an input, and that includes people.

  • Skilled employees leaving is supplier power when the rate for a replacement rises, because labor is an input the firm has to buy.
  • A sole approved supplier holds the same kind of power when it shortens payment terms mid-contract and you hold no priced alternative.
  • Buying nobody can see whole hands power away by default, because a firm that cannot state what it purchases in total never uses the leverage it already has.

Calling the first of those a human-resources problem is the common mistake. Human resources is where you do something about it. Supplier power is what it is.

The chapter already argued all five, without the word

Read the chapter’s account of the age of the customer with the framework in hand. In a few consecutive sentences it argues every one of the five forces, and it never once says the word “force”.

  • Barriers to entry fell when the internet freed customers from buying locally, which is the threat of new entrants rising.
  • Digital substitutes replaced or marginalized many products, which is substitution rather than a new competitor arriving in your industry.
  • Buyer power increased because a customer can find prices, reviews and a competitor’s stock in seconds and leave over a small difference.
  • Employees, an important source of supply, gained mobility and therefore power, which is supplier power showing up in the labor market.
  • Competitors can predict your next move from the information they hold, which sharpens rivalry among firms already selling what you sell.

That is the reading skill this section teaches. Evidence almost never arrives labeled with the force it belongs to.

What an enterprise system changes, and what it does not

Each of the three system families lands on particular activities, and through those activities it touches particular forces.

  • ERP lands on the support activities and the internal spine, so its usual target is cost and the speed of ordinary decisions.
  • SCM lands on inbound and outbound logistics and on procurement, so it moves supplier terms, delivery reliability and stock held.
  • CRM lands on marketing and sales and on service, so it is the family aimed most directly at the bargaining power of buyers.

Now the limit. A system that works improves this firm’s position inside the industry it is already in. It does not by itself change the industry’s structure. After your CRM ships there are still the same rivals, entry is still as easy or as hard as it was, and any substitute is doing whatever it was doing before.

What changed is how much of that pressure lands on you rather than on the firm next door. The distinction decides what you promise. “This will cut the credits we issue and give three concentrated buyers a reason to stay” can be tested in a quarter. “This will make our industry less competitive” is not a claim at all.

Application 8.7

Recommend one enterprise-systems initiative

A manager does not approve a system. A manager approves a change in how the work gets done, at a price, with a date on which somebody says whether it worked. This closing application turns the whole chapter into one recommendation you could defend in a meeting.

Application supplement. Every firm, figure, and quote below is hypothetical and invented for practice. The section applies Chapter 8's vocabulary and ordinary cost reasoning to a decision a manager really faces; it is not one of the chapter's own objectives.

Name the problem as a process failure

"We need an ERP" is not a problem. It is a purchase. A problem statement says what goes wrong, to whom, how often, and what it costs. Written the other way, the same situation reads: purchasing and the shop floor keep separate on-hand figures, so we promised stock we did not have on 168 of 2,400 orders last quarter, at about $310 each in expedited freight and credits.

That sentence does real work. It points at the order-to-cash process rather than at a department to blame. It describes the rocks in the river, two standalone applications holding the same inventory data, reentered by hand, drifting apart. And it can be checked. Anyone may go and count the 168.

Name the system and the component

Managers approve things they can price, and a class of software has no price. The chapter gives you a ladder of precision, and each rung narrows what you are actually asking for:

  • Enterprise system names the class: one integrated suite of applications sharing a single company-wide database.
  • ERP core component names the internal half, meaning financial management, operations management, or human resource management.
  • ERP extended component names the outward half, supply chain management upstream and customer relationship management downstream.
  • Module names the piece you actually switch on, selected and implemented as the business needs it.
  • Configuration names the setup work: the rules, tables, and identifiers that make a module match your firm.

Configuration is not customization, and the difference is money. Customization means new code, or direct changes to the vanilla version, and those changes have to be reprogrammed when the next release arrives. Ask for configured vanilla modules wherever the work allows it.

Price the whole thing, not the licence

The quoted subscription is the smallest number in the file. The chapter names the two costs everybody underestimates: configuring the system, and training, which it calls the most overlooked and poorly budgeted expense in the plan.

A year-one figure a finance committee can trust adds up at least these lines:

  • Licence or subscription pays for the software itself, up front on your own servers or per user in the cloud.
  • Configuration labour pays the analysts and consultants who settle thousands of decisions about tables, identifiers, and business rules.
  • Integration pays for the interfaces to whatever you are not replacing, which is where custom work quietly accumulates.
  • Training and change management pays to teach people a new interface and a new set of business processes together.
  • Recurring cost pays for the years after this one, when subscription fees and internal support continue without a project.

Then warn them about the first month. Companies often see a sharp drop in productivity right after go-live, while experienced people work slowly in an unfamiliar system. Say so in advance. Otherwise the earliest numbers look like failure and the project loses its sponsor in week three.

Say what would make it fail

The chapter's four recommendations for a successful implementation are also four ways to fail. Read them backwards, add the warning the chapter gives separately about customization, and the risk section writes itself:

  • No executive sponsor is the failure most often blamed, because nobody can authorize the process changes the software assumes.
  • Advice taken from the vendor alone risks a contract written around what a salesperson understood about your work.
  • Thin training leaves people running the old process inside a new screen, so the promised benefit misses the numbers.
  • A single-department team misses requirements end users could have named, and alienates the people who must use it.
  • Heavy customization buys a fit today and an expensive upgrade later, since those changes are rebuilt at each release.

Decision 4. The committee asks what would make this fail. What goes in the recommendation?

  • Little can go wrong, since the vendor's best-practice modules are proven across this industry. — Industry best practices smooth an implementation; they do not supply a sponsor, trained users or a team. The chapter's failure list starts with weak sponsorship.
  • A named sponsor, outside advice, trained users, and a cross-department team. — the stronger call. The chapter's four recommendations, read backwards as the four ways this fails, each with someone accountable for it.
  • A risk register will be opened after go-live, once the real problems have begun to appear. — Risks named after go-live cannot change the plan. The training budget is already spent or already missing by then.

Decision 5. Last question on the page: how will anyone know whether it worked?

  • Failed stock promises per 1,000 orders, from a baseline of 70, reviewed twelve weeks out. — the stronger call. Baseline, denominator, and date are fixed before the work starts, so the review can go either way honestly.
  • The plant managers will report back on whether the new screens feel faster than the old ones. — Impressions run high in the weeks after training and cannot separate a real gain from a quiet quarter.
  • We will settle on a measure once the first full quarter of figures has come in after go-live. — A measure chosen after the results arrive will be the one that flatters them, and everyone in the room knows it.

Choose the evidence before the work starts

Pick the measure now, while you can still be wrong in public. Fix the baseline: 70 failed stock promises per 1,000 orders this quarter. Fix the denominator, so a slow quarter cannot be mistaken for improvement. Fix the target and the date: under 30 per 1,000, reviewed twelve weeks after go-live.

Write the decision rule beside it. If the number does not move, what happens then? Name the guardrails as well, such as on-time shipment, order accuracy, and days to close the books. A headline number that improves while a guardrail collapses has not earned the next round of spending.

Does your recommendation have every part?

Hold your own one-page recommendation beside this list. Mark what you can defend without notes, and use each hint on what you cannot.

Lesson and answer summary — the interactive version of this activity loads when scripting is available.

  • I state the problem as a process failure with a count, not as missing software. Reread your first sentence. If it names a product before it names a broken process, write it again.
  • I name the system, the component, and the module I am asking for. Work down the chapter's levels: enterprise system, then core or extended component, then the module itself.
  • I separate configuration from customization in my own proposal. Configuration sets rules, tables, and identifiers; customization writes code that must be rebuilt at the next release.
  • I give a year-one total and the recurring cost that follows it. Add licence, configuration, integration, training, and change management, then state next year's figure on its own line.
  • I name the ways this implementation fails and who prevents each one. Turn the four success factors around: sponsorship, outside expertise, thorough training, and a multidisciplinary team.
  • I state a baseline, a denominator, a target, and a review date. A measure chosen after the results arrive settles nothing. Write all four down before go-live, not after.
  • I name the guardrails that must not get worse while the headline number improves. On-time shipment, order accuracy, and the time taken to close the books are the usual three.
  • I say what the firm gives up by adopting its industry's best practices. If one process is genuinely a source of advantage, name it and say why it is being protected.

A recommendation a manager can approve names a process failure with a count, one component of one system, the whole year-one cost and what recurs after it, the ways the work could fail, and the single number that will settle the argument on a date you set in advance.

Chapter vocabulary

Pay particular attention to the pairs that are easy to swap: order-to-cash against procure-to-pay, core against support activities, upstream against downstream, internally against externally focused systems, supply chain planning against execution, and a supplier portal against a customer portal.

8.1functional areas
The traditional departments a company is organized around: marketing and sales, supply chain management, manufacturing and operations, accounting and finance, and human resources.For example: One customer order passes through sales, the warehouse, and accounting before it is finished.
8.1order-to-cash
All the steps involved in selling a product or service, from creating the customer record to collecting the payment.For example: At Amazon, buying a book moves through a credit check, stock allocation, picking, packing, shipping, invoicing, and payment.
8.1procure-to-pay
All the steps involved in buying goods from outside vendors, from negotiating terms to settling the invoice.For example: A regional grocery chain negotiates prices, issues a purchase order, receives the produce, and pays the supplier.
8.1make-to-stock
Producing goods from a forecast and holding them in a warehouse until customer orders arrive.For example: A television maker builds sets to a forecast and ships them out of finished-goods inventory when stores order.
8.1make-to-order
Procuring inputs from a forecast but not starting manufacturing until an actual customer order arrives.For example: A custom furniture shop begins cutting wood only after a buyer approves the design.
8.1push-based approach
Producing ahead of demand and pushing the finished goods out to customers from inventory.For example: A cereal plant runs a full line in advance and pushes cases to grocery warehouses.
8.1pull-based approach
Waiting for a real order and letting that order pull the work through the production process.For example: A commercial aircraft assembly starts only after an airline has placed its order.
8.1supply chain
The collection of companies and processes that move a product from raw materials all the way to the end customer.For example: A book travels from the paper mill through the printer and distributor to the reader.
8.1supply network
A more accurate picture of a supply chain, in which many suppliers feed many customers instead of a single line.For example: A car plant buys steel, paint, and electronics from several first-tier suppliers who each have suppliers of their own.
8.1upstream and downstream information flows
Information a company receives from an organization earlier in the chain, and information it produces and sends on to an organization closer to the customer.For example: A distributor receives a shipping notice from the factory and sends a delivery date on to the store.
8.1value chain
The set of activities inside an organization that add value to the end product, with information flowing between them.For example: Receiving, order processing, shipping, marketing, and support each add something to a finished sale.
8.1core activities
The value chain activities performed by the functional areas that take in inputs and produce outputs.For example: Inbound logistics, operations and manufacturing, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and customer service are the five.
8.1inbound logistics
Receiving and stocking the raw materials, parts, and products that come into the company.For example: Amazon employees unwrap incoming crates and stock the products in the warehouse.
8.1operations and manufacturing
Transforming the inputs a company received into the products or completed orders it sells.For example: Rivian uses the details of an online order to assemble a customized vehicle.
8.1outbound logistics
Distributing finished products out to the customer.For example: Amazon picks a customer's items from inventory, packs them, and coordinates the shipment.
8.1marketing and sales activities
The before-the-sale work of promoting goods and services, communicating with customers, and setting prices.For example: An airline enters new fares once and they appear immediately on its website.
8.1customer service activities
The after-the-sale work of answering questions and helping customers with what they already bought.For example: An Amazon customer checks an order's status and prints an invoice without calling anyone.
8.1support activities
Activities that produce nothing for sale but make the core activities possible: administration, firm infrastructure, human resources, technology development, and procurement.For example: Payroll, the buildings, and the company network never touch the product, yet nothing ships without them.
8.1procurement
The support activity of purchasing the goods and services the core activities need as inputs.For example: One buying group combines identical requests from three departments into a single purchase order to earn a volume discount.
8.1value system
Several companies' value chains connected so that information flows out of one company's chain and into the next one's.For example: A parts maker, an assembler, and a dealer pass order and delivery information down the same line.
8.2standalone application
A system built for one department's needs that was never designed to exchange data with the organization's other systems.For example: The warehouse program cannot be read by the order-entry program, so staff check two screens for one order.
8.2legacy system
An older application at or past the end of its useful life that the organization still depends on.For example: A payroll program written decades ago still runs, but no other system can read what it stores.
8.2best-of-breed approach
Running a suite of independent systems, each the strongest in its own area, instead of one integrated suite.For example: A clothing retailer keeps a specialist warehouse system and a separate specialist scheduling system and connects the two.
8.2enterprise-wide information system
An integrated suite of applications covering nearly every business process, built on one shared central database; also called an enterprise system.For example: Shipping, accounting, and support all read the same order record from the same database.
8.2internally focused system
A system that supports functional areas, processes, and decisions inside a single organization.For example: An order moves from entry to fulfillment to shipping without ever leaving one company's system.
8.2externally focused system
A system that coordinates processes and communication with customers, suppliers, and partners outside the organization.For example: A parts maker's shipping system sends a notice straight into the receiving system of the plant expecting it.
8.2packaged software
Software written by an outside vendor for many different customers, also called off-the-shelf software.For example: A payroll product sold to thousands of employers handles standard tax withholding for all of them.
8.2custom software
Software designed and built exclusively for one organization's particular needs.For example: A freight company pays developers to build a routing tool that no vendor sells.
8.2best practices
Industry-standard ways of working, identified through experience and research, that enterprise software is designed around.For example: A vendor's hospital version already follows the admission steps most hospitals use.
8.2business process management
A structured approach in which people examine, rethink, and radically redesign business processes to achieve dramatic gains in quality, cycle time, or cost.For example: A team maps the current claims process, measures how long it takes, and redesigns it around a new system.
8.2data consolidation
This module's label for the chapter's ethical case on consolidation: one company gathering a person's data from many separate products and services into a single picture of that person. The chapter uses the plain word consolidation rather than this phrase.For example: Amazon sells household goods, devices, and media, and has also offered a wearable that tracks activity and health.
8.3enterprise resource planning
An enterprise system that replaces standalone applications with modules sharing one company-wide database.For example: Inventory recorded at the loading dock is immediately visible to accounting, sales, and customer service.
8.3ERP core components
The modules that support the important internal activities a company performs to produce its products and services.For example: Financial management, operations management, and human resource management make up this group.
8.3ERP extended components
The modules that support the company's main external activities with suppliers and customers.For example: Supply chain management and customer relationship management sit in this group.
8.3financial management
The ERP core component supporting accounting, financial reporting, performance management, and corporate governance.For example: It checks a buyer's credit limit, then bills the order and records the incoming payment.
8.3operations management
The ERP core component that simplifies and automates inbound and outbound logistics, product development, manufacturing, and sales and service.For example: It allocates stock for an order and then directs picking, packing, and shipping.
8.3human resource management
The ERP core component supporting recruitment, assignment tracking, performance reviews, payroll, and regulatory requirements.For example: It schedules the workforce, records attendance, and runs payroll for every location.
8.3vanilla version
The features and modules an enterprise system arrives with out of the box.For example: A distributor runs the software exactly as delivered and changes its own steps to match it.
8.3customization
Adding software to an enterprise system, or changing the delivered application itself, so it supports a process the vanilla version does not.For example: A rewritten screen has to be reprogrammed every time the vendor releases a new version.
8.3configuration
Setting the options, database tables, and business rules of an enterprise system to match how this organization works, without changing the software itself.For example: The team decides the format of the customer number and the day a bill becomes overdue.
8.3total cost of ownership
The full cost of a system over its life, including the items commonly left out of the original estimate.For example: Travel and training, ongoing customization, process studies, and project governance all add to the licence price.
8.3lock-in
The difficulty of changing direction once an enterprise system is running, because the company's processes have been reshaped around it.For example: A parts maker that rebuilt its workflow around its ERP finds a new line of business slow to add.
8.4supply chain management systems
Systems that improve the coordination of suppliers, production, and distribution across company boundaries.For example: A manufacturer's system shares forecasts and order status with the firms that supply its components.
8.4electronic data interchange
Computer-to-computer exchange of business documents between companies following agreed standards, with no human retyping.For example: Walmart requires suppliers to send advance shipping notices using web-based EDI.
8.4supplier portal
A secured entry point run by a buying company that automates the business processes it shares with its many suppliers.For example: On the HP Supplier Portal, firms register interest, read labeling and shipment guidelines, and manage ordering and payment.
8.4customer portal
A secured entry point run by a selling company that automates the business processes it shares with its many business customers.For example: MyBoeingFleet lets airplane owners and operators look up configurations, maintenance documents, and spare parts.
8.4business-to-business marketplace
A site run by a third party that brings many buyers and many sellers together for trade between companies rather than with consumers.For example: Alibaba.com connects buyers and suppliers worldwide across almost every industry.
8.4just-in-time
Ordering parts and materials so they arrive exactly when production needs them, keeping almost nothing in storage.For example: Toyota pioneered the approach, taking frequent small deliveries instead of filling a stockroom.
8.4vendor-managed inventory
An arrangement in which the supplier watches a customer's stock levels and sales data and replenishes the customer's inventory.For example: A beverage supplier sees a store's daily sales and restocks the shelf before it empties.
8.4bullwhip effect
The way a small change in customer demand grows into much larger swings in orders further up the supply chain.For example: A brief rush on paper towels leads the store to over-order, the maker to over-produce, and suppliers to over-buy raw stock.
8.4safety stock
Extra inventory held as a buffer so a forecasting error does not turn into a stockout.For example: A hardware chain keeps several extra pallets of its top-selling item in case a delivery runs late.
8.4supply chain effectiveness and efficiency
How well supply activities meet the requirements of external partners, versus how well the company minimizes the resources those activities use.For example: A firm competing on fast delivery holds more inventory in more warehouses, while a low-cost rival holds less in fewer.
8.4supply chain planning
Developing the resource plans that guide production, including forecasts, distribution plans, schedules, and inventory targets.For example: A demand forecast becomes a transportation plan, a production plan, and a sourcing plan for the coming season.
8.4supply chain execution
Putting the supply chain plans into motion through the product, information, and financial flows.For example: The sourcing plan turns into real purchase orders, real truckloads, and real payments.
8.4product flow
The movement of goods from supplier to production, from production to distribution, and from distribution to the consumer.For example: Fabric leaves the mill for a sewing plant, then a distribution center, then a store shelf.
8.4information flow
The movement of information such as orders and delivery status up and down the supply chain, ideally with no paper documents.For example: Order, fulfillment, and billing details are shared electronically instead of faxed between partners.
8.4financial flow
The movement of money and payment-related information along the supply chain, normally back toward the suppliers.For example: A consumer's purchase funds a payment to the distributor, which funds a payment to the producer.
8.4reverse logistics
The processes for efficiently receiving products back from the point of consumption for return, repair, recycling, or disposal.For example: Amazon has to handle a steady stream of returned items without losing their remaining value.
8.4supply chain visibility
Being able to track products as they move through the supply chain and to see outside events that may disrupt them.For example: Sensors report a refrigerated trailer's location and temperature, and a typhoon forecast flags a supplier's factory.
8.4supply chain analytics
Using key performance indicators to monitor how the whole chain is performing, from sourcing through distribution.For example: A purchasing manager identifies which suppliers repeatedly miss their promised delivery dates.
8.4blockchain
A distributed ledger of linked transaction records held on many computers, hard to tamper with and proposed as a way to trace goods through a supply chain.For example: De Beers tracks the movement of diamonds from the mine to the store to confirm their origin.
8.5customer relationship management systems
Systems, and a company-wide strategy, for building and keeping lasting customer relationships by managing downstream information flows.For example: Data from stores, the website, social media, and the call center is pulled into one view of each customer.
8.5CRM strategy
The enterprise-wide changes a company must make beyond installing software: policies and processes, customer service metrics, employee training, and data sharing.For example: Starbucks pairs a loyalty card with an open platform where customers submit product ideas.
8.5sales force automation
CRM functions that support the day-to-day work of selling, such as tracking opportunities and managing the sales pipeline.For example: Without one shared pipeline, Dell salespeople in different countries could chase the same contract unaware of each other.
8.5marketing automation
CRM functions that manage promotional campaigns across channels and nurture leads; also called enterprise marketing management.For example: A campaign sends the same offer by email and social post only to the customers who have not opted out.
8.5customer engagement center
The customer service side of CRM, using many communication channels to handle requests, complaints, and returns the way the customer prefers.For example: Southwest Airlines lets callers keep their place in line and be called back rather than wait on hold.
8.5analysis and reporting
CRM functions that analyze customer behavior and perceptions so the company can segment customers and monitor performance.For example: A dashboard shows the sales pipeline by stage, leads by campaign, and open cases by priority.
8.5customer lifetime value
The value a company expects from its entire relationship with one customer rather than from a single sale.For example: A bank weighs decades of accounts and loans from one household, not the fee on today's transaction.
8.5social media monitoring
Identifying and assessing the volume and sentiment of what is being said about a company, product, or brand online.For example: A team uses Hootsuite to follow real-time chatter about a brand across several social platforms.
8.5over-personalization
This module's name for the point where a tailored message stops feeling helpful and starts feeling like the company knows too much. The chapter never uses this phrase; it asks at what point communication gets too personal, and concludes that personalization could backfire on a company.For example: A shopper who receives offers implying the store knows about a private life event stops buying there.

1. 8.6 A shopfitter who has never built refrigeration hires two cabinet engineers and quotes against you for cabinet work. Which force is that?

  • A. The threat of substitutes, since the buyer changes supplier. — A substitute means the customer stops buying cabinets and meets the need another way, such as a cold room.
  • B. Supplier power, because the engineers moved to a rival. — The engineers leaving does raise supplier power, but the question asks about the firm now quoting against you.
  • C. The threat of new entrants offering the same product. — correct. The customer still ends up with a cabinet and simply buys it from somebody who was not competing before.
  • D. Rivalry, because every quote contest is rivalry. — Rivalry counts firms already inside the industry; this firm was outside it until it hired the engineers.

2. 8.7 A manager writes: “We need an enterprise resource planning system.” What is the first thing the sentence should say instead?

  • A. Which vendor was rated highest by an industry analyst this year. — An analyst rating recommends a product before anyone has agreed what problem the product is for.
  • B. How many months the installation is expected to take in total. — A schedule matters later; opening with it still leaves the funder asking what the work is meant to cure.
  • C. Which departments have asked for the system most often. — Demand from departments records who is frustrated, which is not the same as showing what is going wrong.
  • D. The process that is failing, stated with a count of the failures. — correct. Naming the failing process with a count gives the funder something they can check and later measure against.

3. 8.7 A recommendation promises “better customer service” once a system is live. What turns that into evidence a year later?

  • A. A baseline measured now, a target, and the date it is judged. — correct. Without a figure recorded beforehand there is nothing to compare the later measurement against.
  • B. A survey sent to customers after the system has been installed. — A survey taken only afterwards cannot show whether anything changed, because nobody measured before.
  • C. A clause requiring the vendor to confirm the work succeeded. — A vendor confirming its own work is not independent evidence about the customer's experience.
  • D. A list of the features the new service desk will make available. — A feature list describes what was bought rather than what changed for anybody using it.

4. 8.1 A print shop agrees terms with a paper mill, issues a purchase order, receives the rolls, and settles the invoice. Which core process is this?

  • A. Order-to-cash, because an invoice is part of the sequence. — An invoice appears in both processes; here the shop is paying one rather than issuing it.
  • B. Procure-to-pay, from negotiated terms through settlement. — correct. Negotiating terms, ordering, receiving goods, and paying the invoice are the procure-to-pay subprocesses.
  • C. Make-to-order, because production waits for the order. — Make-to-order describes producing goods after an order arrives, not acquiring inputs from a vendor.
  • D. Reverse logistics, because goods arrive at the shop. — Reverse logistics moves goods back from the point of consumption rather than in from a supplier.

5. 8.1 A kayak plant builds standard hulls to a forecast and stores them, but starts a custom touring hull only after an order is signed. Which reading fits?

  • A. Both lines are make-to-order, because each hull reaches a buyer. — Reaching a buyer is true of almost any product and does not identify when production starts.
  • B. Both lines are make-to-stock, because the plant holds inventory. — Holding some finished hulls does not change the fact that the custom hull waits for an order.
  • C. The standard line is push-based; the custom line is pull-based. — correct. Forecast-driven production pushes goods into stock, while an order pulls the custom hull through production.
  • D. Neither label fits, because one plant runs one process at a time. — A plant can run different processes for different product lines, which is common with customizable goods.

6. 8.1 A tool distributor says its order-to-cash process ends when the crate leaves the loading dock. What is missing?

  • A. Checking the buyer's credit, which belongs after shipping. — A creditworthiness check belongs early in the process, before stock is allocated and shipped.
  • B. Negotiating the price of the goods with the supplier. — Negotiating with a supplier is a procure-to-pay step and sits outside the selling process.
  • C. Scheduling the production run that made the goods. — Production scheduling belongs to the make processes rather than to selling and collecting.
  • D. Invoicing the buyer and collecting the payment owed. — correct. The process is named for cash, so it closes only when the invoice is paid and the money arrives.

7. 8.1 A furniture maker's in-house team writes the software that plans cutting patterns for the saws. Where does that work sit?

  • A. Technology development, a support activity behind the work. — correct. Designing and developing applications that improve the primary activities is technology development.
  • B. Operations and manufacturing, because the saws cut the panels. — The saws performing the cut are operations; writing the planning software is not the cutting itself.
  • C. Inbound logistics, because the panels arrive from a supplier. — Receiving and stocking materials is inbound logistics, which the software supports but does not perform.
  • D. Customer service, because buyers benefit from tighter cuts. — Customer service handles post-sale questions and problems rather than the design of internal tooling.

8. 8.1 A claims office argues the value chain cannot describe it because nothing physical moves. What is the better response?

  • A. The framework suits firms that receive and ship goods. — Inbound and outbound logistics matter less in services, yet the other activities still describe the work.
  • B. The office should count each claim as an item of inventory. — Calling a claim inventory forces a physical metaphor onto work that is really information handling.
  • C. The activities hold, but operations here handles information. — correct. In services, operations manipulates data and information, and optimizing means improving that flow.
  • D. Service firms replace the chain with a single support activity. — Service firms still perform sales, operations, and customer service rather than collapsing into one activity.

9. 8.1 A bottler receives a resin specification from its supplier and sends a delivery schedule on to its distributor. Which labels fit?

  • A. The specification is downstream; the schedule is upstream. — The labels are reversed: information received from another firm arrives upstream, not downstream.
  • B. The specification is upstream; the schedule is downstream. — correct. Information received from another organization is upstream; information produced and passed on is downstream.
  • C. Both are upstream, because both cross a company boundary. — Crossing a boundary is what makes a value system, but direction still depends on who produced the document.
  • D. Neither is a flow, because no goods move with the documents. — Information flows are part of the value system whether or not goods travel alongside the documents.

10. 8.2 Receiving and order entry run separate systems, so stock figures are retyped each morning and the two now disagree. What is this?

  • A. An enterprise system whose central database is queried twice. — A shared central database would remove the retyping instead of requiring it every morning.
  • B. A value system linking two organizations that share data. — A value system spans organizations; these two departments sit inside the same company.
  • C. A best-of-breed design chosen for a faster response. — Best-of-breed is a deliberate choice with planned interfaces, not daily re-entry by hand and drift.
  • D. Standalone applications not built to exchange data. — correct. Department-level systems built separately cannot pass data, so people move it by hand and copies diverge.

11. 8.2 A payroll program written decades ago still runs, but it cannot exchange data and the firm now pays specialists to keep it alive. What is the term?

  • A. A legacy system at or beyond its useful life. — correct. Standalone applications nearing or past their useful life inside the organization are called legacy systems.
  • B. A vanilla module shipped by an enterprise vendor. — A vanilla version means an enterprise system's out-of-the-box features, not an aging in-house program.
  • C. An extranet connecting the firm to its partners. — An extranet gives outside partners controlled access; this program serves only internal payroll work.
  • D. A supplier portal used by the purchasing group. — A supplier portal automates purchasing with many suppliers and has nothing to do with payroll.

12. 8.2 A firm keeps the strongest system in each area and joins them through interfaces rather than buying one suite. How should this be described?

  • A. A failure to modernize that will become legacy debt. — Many organizations build successful businesses this way, so separate systems are not automatically obsolete.
  • B. A best-of-breed approach trading integration work for agility. — correct. Independent leading systems can keep a firm agile, but the interfaces between them must be built and maintained.
  • C. An enterprise system, since the parts exchange data with each other. — An enterprise system integrates modules on one shared database, which is not what these interfaces create.
  • D. A customization of one vendor's product across the departments. — Customization changes one vendor's application; here the firm is running several vendors' products.

13. 8.2 A manufacturer opens a site where its dealers check warranty status and file claims themselves. How is the system best classified, and for which reason?

  • A. Internally focused, because the warranty records are the firm's own data. — Owning the data does not decide whom a system serves, and the claim is filed by a dealer outside the firm.
  • B. Internally focused, because the dealers all trade under the firm's brand. — A shared brand does not bring the dealers inside the organization, so the process still runs between two firms.
  • C. Externally focused, because it coordinates a process across the boundary. — correct. Systems that streamline processes with customers, suppliers, or partners cross organizational boundaries.
  • D. Externally focused, because the dealers reach the site over the public internet. — The label is right and the reason is not: staff reach an internal portal the same way, so access decides nothing.

14. 8.2 A laboratory's sample-tracking rules are unlike anything a vendor sells. What does a careful manager do before commissioning custom software?

  • A. Check whether a package can meet the requirement, then cost it. — correct. Managers first ask whether a package fits, then run a cost-benefit case for the custom route.
  • B. Assume packaged software is cheaper and change the lab's rules. — Cost alone cannot settle it, because a package that does not fit the work is rebuilt in spreadsheets.
  • C. Commission the custom build, since unusual work demands it. — Custom software puts every development and maintenance cost on the firm, so uniqueness is not enough.
  • D. Buy both products and let the laboratory decide which survives. — Running two systems doubles the expense and creates exactly the redundancy the lab wants to avoid.

15. 8.3 An ERP shortlist includes modules for payroll, the general ledger, warehouse picking, and supplier collaboration. Which one is an extended component?

  • A. Payroll, because employees are external to the production process. — Extended means outside the company, not outside the factory floor; employees sit inside that boundary, so payroll is core human resource management.
  • B. General ledger, because auditors outside the firm read the reports. — Financial management is a core component even though outsiders read what it produces.
  • C. Supplier collaboration, because it reaches outside the firm. — correct. Extended components handle the external work of dealing with suppliers and customers.
  • D. Warehouse picking, because carriers collect the finished goods. — A carrier collecting the goods does not move the module across the boundary; picking is your own warehouse's work, so it stays core operations management.

16. 8.3 A distributor wants overdue invoices flagged at forty-five days rather than thirty. Which activity does that request call for?

  • A. Customization, because the vendor's code has to be rewritten. — Rewriting code would be customization, but a due-date rule is one of the settings the system expects.
  • B. Configuration, because the rule is a setting the system expects. — correct. Configuration sets business rules such as identifier formats and when a bill is treated as overdue.
  • C. A best-practice study, because the vendor decides the term. — Best practices guide process design and do not prevent a firm from setting its own payment terms.
  • D. A new module, because the finance component cannot age invoices. — Aging invoices is ordinary financial management work that the core component already performs.

17. 8.3 A firm has rewritten several ERP screens to match its own workflow. What should it expect at the next release?

  • A. The vendor carries the rewritten screens forward at no charge. — Later releases do not include a customer's previous customizations, so the vendor cannot simply carry them.
  • B. The rewrites disappear quietly and the workflow is unchanged. — Losing the rewrites would change how staff work, which is precisely why upgrades get postponed.
  • C. The release refuses to install until the rewrites are deleted. — The obstacle is the cost of reworking customizations rather than a technical refusal to install.
  • D. The rewrites must be redone and retested before upgrading. — correct. Customizations must be reprogrammed for each new release, which is why firms delay updates.

18. 8.3 A budget for an ERP project lists licenses, hardware, and technical implementation. Which omission most often causes the overrun?

  • A. Training the users, and the business process studies behind it. — correct. Training and change management are the most overlooked and poorly budgeted line, and the process studies sit beside them.
  • B. Backfilling the clerks seconded full time to the implementation team. — Seconded clerks appear on a headcount somebody already tracks, so that gap is visible long before the go-live date.
  • C. Moving the historical records out of the systems being retired. — Moving records is technical work this budget already funds; what is missing is the effort of changing how people work.
  • D. The subscription increase in year two, once the discount lapses. — A later price rise is written into the contract you sign, so it can be read off rather than discovered midway.

19. 8.3 A regional carrier's unusual routing method is why shippers choose it, and the vendor's best-practice module would replace it. What is the trade-off?

  • A. Keeping the method may shorten the implementation, since the vendor has less to configure. — A process the module does not contain has to be built instead, so rejecting the built-in practices lengthens the work.
  • B. Keeping the method may leave the vendor's other best practices unusable to the firm. — Protecting one distinctive process does not spoil the rest, and the vendor's practices still fit the ordinary work.
  • C. Adopting the module may leave the firm running the same process as every competitor. — correct. Industry best practices standardize work, so the firm can lose the routing that made shippers choose it.
  • D. Adopting the module may end the running cost, since standard code is supported by the vendor. — A standard module still has to be configured, supported, and paid for, so the running cost does not end.

20. 8.3 An ERP project team is drawn entirely from the technology group, and the clerks first see the system at go-live. Which recommendation was skipped?

  • A. Secure the sponsorship of the executives who fund the work. — Executive sponsorship matters, but the missing group here is the staff who do the daily work.
  • B. Get help from experts outside the organization. — Outside consultants can help, though they cannot supply the knowledge the clerks already hold.
  • C. Choose an industry-specific version of the product. — An industry version reduces the selection effort and does not substitute for involving end users.
  • D. Include people from the departments that will use it. — correct. End users see the daily work, and leaving them out risks poor fit and open resistance.

21. 8.3 A team argues a cloud ERP will be quick to adopt because no servers are purchased. What is wrong with the reasoning?

  • A. Cloud systems cannot be configured to a firm's business rules. — Cloud systems are configured too, though the customization options are usually fewer.
  • B. Configuration and retraining are unchanged by where it runs. — correct. Thousands of configuration decisions and the change-management effort remain wherever the software is hosted.
  • C. Subscription pricing removes the need to compare the vendors. — Subscription fees still differ between vendors and can raise the total cost of ownership.
  • D. Hosting elsewhere means the data no longer belongs to the firm. — Data hosted by a provider raises security and privacy questions rather than transferring ownership.

22. 8.4 A cable maker buys from nine direct suppliers, who in turn buy from more than forty of their own. Which description is more accurate?

  • A. A supply network, because the links are not one to one. — correct. Each tier draws on many suppliers, so the branching structure is better described as a network.
  • B. A supply chain, because materials travel in one direction. — Materials do flow toward the customer, yet the word chain implies a single line of one-to-one links.
  • C. A value system, because each party is a paying customer. — A value system describes linked value chains and information flows rather than the branching of suppliers.
  • D. A distribution plan, because materials reach the maker. — Distribution planning is a planning activity, not a name for the structure of the supplier base.

23. 8.4 One appliance manufacturer wants its many parts vendors to register, read its labeling rules, and track their own purchase orders. What fits?

  • A. A customer portal, run upstream to serve many buyers. — A customer portal is run by a supplier to serve many buyers, which reverses the roles described here.
  • B. A marketplace run by a third party for many firms. — A marketplace is operated by an outside party to bring many buyers and many sellers together.
  • C. A supplier portal, run by the buyer for its vendors. — correct. Supplier portals are owned by the buying company and automate purchasing from multiple suppliers.
  • D. A consumer storefront, since orders and labels appear. — Consumer storefronts serve end consumers rather than prescreened partners with negotiated terms.

24. 8.4 A modest rise in shop-floor demand for one filter leaves the third-tier resin maker with orders several times larger. What is happening?

  • A. Reverse logistics, because material moves back upstream. — Reverse logistics covers returns and the recovery of used goods, not the swelling of forward orders.
  • B. The bullwhip effect amplifying a small change upstream. — correct. Safety buffers and forecast errors compound at each tier, so a small change grows as it travels upstream.
  • C. Vendor-managed inventory, because a supplier holds stock. — Vendor-managed inventory is an arrangement for who watches stock levels, not a distortion of demand.
  • D. Just-in-time ordering, because quantities arrive more often. — Just-in-time seeks smaller and more frequent deliveries, which does not explain orders several times larger.

25. 8.4 Two tasks: setting next quarter's safety stock targets, and crediting a customer's account for a returned pallet. How do they classify?

  • A. Both are planning, because each one is decided in advance. — Crediting an account happens as the return is handled and is not a plan made in advance.
  • B. Both are execution, because each involves a real shipment. — Setting target stock levels is a plan developed before any of those shipments take place.
  • C. The first is execution and the second is planning work. — The classifications are reversed: targets are planned, while returns are handled during execution.
  • D. The first is planning and the second is execution work. — correct. Safety stock planning produces a sourcing plan, and handling returns and credits is supply chain execution.

26. 8.4 A buyer pays for a delivered pallet and the carrier posts a status update. Which statement describes the flows correctly?

  • A. The payment moves upstream and the update moves either way. — correct. Financial flows travel back toward the supplier while information can move in both directions.
  • B. The payment moves downstream and the update moves upstream. — Payments do not follow the goods toward the consumer; they travel back the other way.
  • C. Both the payment and the update move toward the consumer. — Product flow moves toward the consumer, but money returns and information circulates both ways.
  • D. Goods are the flow; payments and updates are just records. — Supply chain execution manages three flows, so product, information, and finance all count.

27. 8.4 A grower wants to know whether a typhoon will strand its packaging supplier. What does that ambition require beyond tracking shipments?

  • A. A larger buffer of finished goods at each regional depot. — Extra stock cushions a disruption but does not tell the grower that one is coming.
  • B. Knowing where partners' plants are, which requires trust. — correct. Visibility includes foreseeing external events, and partners share site details where trust exists.
  • C. A faster carrier for every shipment leaving the region. — Speed helps once a shipment exists, and the question is whether the supplier can ship at all.
  • D. A rule that suppliers must sit in the grower's own country. — Concentrating suppliers in one country raises exposure to a single regional event rather than lowering it.

28. 8.4 A cooperative records each harvest on a shared ledger and tells buyers this proves the fruit was grown without banned sprays. What is the flaw?

  • A. Ledger entries can be edited later by whoever wrote them. — Entries are hard to alter once written, which is a strength here rather than the weakness in the claim.
  • B. Shared ledgers cannot record events that happen on a farm. — Ledgers can record farm events perfectly well, and the question is who vouches for what was recorded.
  • C. The ledger preserves the entry, not the truth of the entry. — correct. Someone still enters the data, so a false entry is preserved as faithfully as a true one.
  • D. Buyers are unable to read a ledger without the grower's help. — Access can be arranged, and the problem is the quality of the data rather than who may read it.

29. 8.5 A firm installs a CRM, changes nothing else, and a year later its service measures look the same. What best explains the result?

  • A. The vendor sold a version missing the reporting component. — A missing component is possible but would not explain the absence of change across the whole function.
  • B. Customer data must be deleted before the system can help. — Deleting customer history would remove the record the system depends on to personalize service.
  • C. A CRM helps firms that sell to other businesses. — The practices apply to consumer and business customers alike, and both are served by these systems.
  • D. CRM is a strategy; policies and training must change too. — correct. Without customer-focused policies, measures, and trained staff, the software records the old behavior.

30. 8.5 A CRM feature list includes case queues and a searchable knowledge base for agents. Which component is that?

  • A. Customer service management, which handles cases and answers. — correct. Case handling and knowledge bases are the customer service management functions of a CRM.
  • B. Sales force automation, which manages pipelines and accounts. — Sales automation tracks opportunities and pipelines rather than answering service requests.
  • C. Marketing automation, which runs campaigns and nurtures leads. — Marketing automation manages campaigns and routes leads, and it does not resolve a reported problem.
  • D. Analysis and reporting, which segments customers by behavior. — Analytics describes and segments customers instead of handling an individual open case.

31. 8.5 A service center cuts average handling time by transferring harder calls sooner, and callbacks rise. What does first-contact resolution show?

  • A. It rises, because each call is now shorter than before. — A shorter call is not a solved one, and the rising callbacks show the issue coming back.
  • B. It is unaffected, because the same agents answer these calls. — Who answers matters less than whether the customer's problem was actually resolved that time.
  • C. It falls, because the issue is not settled at first contact. — correct. First-contact resolution counts issues settled during the first contact, which transfers push into later calls.
  • D. It cannot be measured once a call has been transferred. — Transfers and repeat contacts are exactly what this measure is designed to capture.

32. 8.5 One buyer appears as three records: a loyalty card, a web account, and a service ticket under a misspelled name. What is the CRM work?

  • A. Delete two records and keep whichever was created first. — Deleting records throws away purchase and contact history that the surviving record never held.
  • B. Merge the identities so one history informs every contact. — correct. A complete view ties the channels together so anyone who talks to the buyer sees the same picture.
  • C. Ask the buyer to choose which of the three records to keep. — Customers should not be asked to repair a firm's record keeping, and the choice discards real history.
  • D. Keep them apart so each channel reports its own totals. — Channel totals are easy to produce from merged data, and separate records hide that one person is involved.

33. 8.5 A retailer's model infers a shopper's health from purchases and emails offers based on the inference. Which objection is strongest?

  • A. The inference is unhelpful because purchase records are complete. — Purchase records are rarely complete, and completeness would not settle whether the inference is appropriate.
  • B. The inference is harmless because the data was gathered lawfully. — Lawful collection does not answer the objection that personalization can reach too far into private life.
  • C. The offers are wasted because email campaigns rarely convert. — Conversion rates are a marketing question and say nothing about the treatment of the shopper.
  • D. The inference may be wrong, and intrusive when it is right. — correct. Profiling can miscategorize people, and an accurate guess about health can still cost the firm the relationship.

34. 8.6 Two rivals buy the same supply chain suite in the same quarter. What should a manager conclude about advantage?

  • A. The edge lies in the processes and data, not the license. — correct. A purchasable product is available to rivals, so advantage rests on what a rival cannot quickly copy.
  • B. The first firm to finish installing holds a permanent lead. — Finishing first helps briefly, but an installation date is not a durable barrier to imitation.
  • C. Neither firm can gain, since the software is identical. — The same tool can still produce different results depending on the processes and data behind it.
  • D. The suite raises entry barriers for both firms equally. — Owning software that anyone may buy does not by itself obstruct a firm entering the industry.

35. 8.6 Corporate buyers can now compare specifications, lead times, and prices from a dozen suppliers within minutes. Which force strengthens?

  • A. The bargaining power of the suppliers now being compared. — Easy comparison weakens a supplier's position rather than strengthening it against informed buyers.
  • B. The bargaining power of the buyers doing the comparing. — correct. Buyers with quick access to alternatives and prices gain leverage over price and terms.
  • C. The threat that substitutes will replace the product. — A substitute meets the need in a different way, and comparing the same product does not create one.
  • D. Rivalry falls, because prices are visible to everyone. — Transparent prices usually sharpen competition between existing rivals instead of calming it.

36. 8.6 A proposal claims a new CRM will end the late deliveries that are caused by stockouts at the plant. What is the flaw?

  • A. A CRM cannot record a complaint about a late delivery. — A CRM records complaints well, and recording them is not the same as removing the cause.
  • B. Late delivery is a marketing problem the CRM would worsen. — Marketing does not create the stockout, and better campaigns would raise demand the plant cannot meet.
  • C. The plant should replace the CRM with a second finance module. — A finance module addresses ledgers and payments rather than shortages of stock at the plant.
  • D. The cause sits in supply and operations, which a CRM does not run. — correct. CRM serves presale and post-sale customer activities, while the stockout belongs to supply and operations.

37. 8.7 A request to a finance committee reads “approve an enterprise system for us.” Which rewrite lets them price what is being asked for?

  • A. Name the class more fully, as one integrated suite on a shared company-wide database. — A fuller description of the class still carries no price, because nobody has said which piece is being switched on.
  • B. Name the vendor and the edition, so the committee can look up a published list price. — A list price covers the license alone, while the year-one bill still has to carry implementation, configuration, and training.
  • C. Name the module being switched on, and the configuration work it needs. — correct. A module is the piece a firm actually switches on, and configuring it to the firm's business rules is the labor a committee must price.
  • D. Name every department that will use it, so the committee sees how far it reaches. — Counting the departments describes how far the change reaches without saying what work the system would do.

38. 8.7 A recommendation sets a review date twelve weeks after go-live. What should it warn the committee to expect in the first weeks?

  • A. A drop in output while experienced people work slowly in an unfamiliar system. — correct. Firms often see a sharp drop in productivity at go-live, so saying it first stops the early figures reading as failure.
  • B. Steady output, because the modules reproduce the processes people already run. — Users have to learn a new set of business processes as well as a new screen, so the work does not simply continue.
  • C. The project's largest gain, before the old habits begin to reassert themselves. — The gain arrives once people are fluent, and calling the first weeks the peak sets the committee up to be disappointed.
  • D. No change of any kind until the last department has finished its training. — Training beforehand shortens the dip rather than postponing it, because the change lands the day the system is switched on.
