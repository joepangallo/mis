Porter’s five forces · the value chain model

Five forces, five activities, and what is inside each one

This board is deliberately two things and nothing else. The five competitive forces: what each one is, the conditions that make it strong, and how to tell the five apart when the evidence is ambiguous. The five primary activities of the value chain: what each one covers, how it reads in a service business, and — for all five — the cost drivers it is judged on and the systems that answer them. Pick a category and a dollar value, decide your answer, then reveal it and grade yourself honestly.

How to play

  • Goal: clear both boards and bank as much money as you can. You choose the category and the value, the same way the real game works.
  • Every clue is a statement. Your answer is phrased as a question — “What is buyer power?” not “Buyer power.”
  • Answer honestly. Nothing is typed and nothing is graded for you. Decide your answer, tap Reveal answer, then say whether you actually had it. The board cannot tell, and a score you did not earn teaches you nothing.
  • No penalty for a miss on the boards. This is a study tool, not an elimination game: right answers add the tile’s value and misses simply move on. Final Jeopardy is the one place a wrong answer costs you, because you chose the wager.
  • Every answer explains itself. Under each answer is a sentence saying why it is that one and not the term next to it, because the near-miss is what the exam tests.
  • The score is not the point. At the end you get a breakdown by course objective and by topic, so a weak area shows up as a named thing to go back to instead of disappearing into one number.

The two boards

Round one · $200–$1,000 · naming each part

Round two · Double Jeopardy · $400–$2,000 · reading them from evidence

Five categories open up one activity each Naming the five activities is the easy half. Inside Inbound Logistics, Inside Operations, Inside Outbound Logistics, Inside Sales and Marketing and Inside Service ask for what actually sits in each one: the cost drivers that activity is judged on, and the systems that answer them. Twenty-five of the fifty tiles are those five categories, because knowing that outbound logistics exists is not the same as knowing that sixty percent of its bill is drivers idling between stops.

Which course objectives this board covers

Every clue is tagged with the course objectives it serves, and the results screen reports them separately. This board is deliberately narrow — two lists and what sits inside one of them, not a chapter — so it claims three objectives and says plainly what it leaves alone.

Double Jeopardy

The first board is clear. Your score is $0. The second board is worth double, and it stops asking what each part is called and starts asking you to recognise it from the evidence.

What changes

  • Values double — $400 through $2,000.
  • The clues become situations. A firm has a symptom and you name the force, or a hotel is doing something and you name the activity, which is the form this material takes once you are the person being asked.
  • Which Force Is It? is built entirely on the five near-misses — rival against substitute, entrant against rival, a demanding buyer against a crowded market — because the near-miss is where the marks go.
  • The last three categories open up the activities round one did not. Outbound logistics, sales and marketing, and service, one cost driver and one system at a time.
  • Your score carries over. Nothing from round one is lost.
