Module 6 · managing data and business intelligence

Fifty-one clues, one to four players

The first board is the vocabulary the chapter cannot be discussed without: what makes data an asset, what the parts of a table are called, the SQL clauses, and the three Vs. The second board is the work — diagnosing which data failure you are actually looking at, naming the report a manager is asking for, choosing where the data should live, reading competitive pressure, and the shape of a recommendation somebody could fund. Play it alone as a study tool, or put two to four people on one screen and race for the buzzer.

Who is playing?

One player is a study session. Two to four is a game, and the rules change accordingly — see How to play below. Names are only used on this screen and are remembered in this browser so the next round can start straight away.

How to play

  • The player in control picks the category and the dollar value, the same way the real game works. Whoever answers correctly takes control of the board; if nobody gets it, control stays where it was. Double Jeopardy opens with the player in last place, which is the show’s own rule and the one that keeps a runaway game interesting.
  • With two or more players, the number keys are the buzzers. Once a clue is up, press 1, 2, 3 or 4 to ring in — first key down wins, exactly as on a shared keyboard it should — or 0 if nobody has it. The buttons do the same thing for a touchscreen.
  • Answer out loud before anything is revealed. The person who rang in says their answer, phrased as a question, and the table decides whether it counts. The printed answer stays hidden until the clue is settled, because a steal is worthless if everyone has already read it.
  • A wrong answer costs the tile’s value and opens the clue to everybody else. That penalty is the only thing that makes ringing in mean anything, so it applies whenever two or more are playing. Alone, nothing is deducted — a study tool that punishes you for attempting a clue teaches you to attempt fewer of them.
  • Every answer explains itself. Under each answer is a sentence saying why it is that one and not the term next to it, because the near-miss is what the exam tests. Read it before moving on; it is the part of the game that is actually the lesson.
  • Final Jeopardy is wagered privately. Each player enters a wager in turn — pass the screen along — then one clue goes to everybody at once and each wager is settled separately. This is the one place a wrong answer costs a solo player anything, because the solo player chose the number.
  • The score is not the point. The results screen breaks the game down by course objective and by section of the module, so a weak area shows up as a named thing to go back to instead of disappearing into one total.

The last category is the recommendation, not more vocabulary Making the Case asks about the deliverable itself: why two reports are not two value chain activities, what a comparison needs before a drop in hours counts as an improvement, why released staff time is not yet a cash saving, and the piece that decides in advance what would stop a pilot from being extended. Those clues are worth playing even if you already know every definition on the first board.

Which course objectives this board covers

Every clue is tagged with the course objectives it serves, and the results screen reports them separately. Module 6 is one chapter, so the board claims five objectives and says plainly which ones it leaves alone.

Course objectiveClues
Not on this board What an information systems department does, internet technology for electronic commerce and working with business partners, and the life cycle of an information system are course objectives this chapter does not teach. Neither is security breaches and computer crime: the module covers responsible access, accidental exposure and recovery, which is a duty of care rather than the recognition of a crime. Objectives 10 and 11 — building a database application and a spreadsheet decision support system — are the course’s separate build work; asking what WHERE does is preparation for it, not a substitute.

What changes

  • Values double — $400 through $2,000, and so does the cost of a wrong answer.
  • The clues become situations. A firm has a problem and you name the failure, the report, the repository, the force or the missing piece of the recommendation — which is the form this material takes once you are the person being asked.
  • The player in last place picks first, which is the show’s own rule.
  • Every score carries over. Nothing from round one is lost.
