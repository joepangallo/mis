Practice case

Harbor & Pine Garden Centers

A regional retailer losing ground while its own stores cannot see each other’s stock. Apply the five competitive forces and the value chain, recommend one initiative, and compare it against a real company you can read about.

Scenario

A garden retailer whose busiest eight weeks are also its worst

Harbor & Pine Garden Centers is not a real company. It is invented for this exercise, the way the practice examples in the lecture modules are, and every organisation, number and quotation in it is hypothetical.

You have been hired as an analyst at Harbor & Pine Garden Centers, a mid-sized regional retailer selling plants, garden supplies, outdoor furniture and seasonal décor. Harbor & Pine operates 14 stores across two states and has relied on in-person sales for more than fifteen years. Over the last three years the company has lost share steadily: online plant and garden sellers now ship living stock directly to a customer’s door, two national chains have enlarged their garden departments, and freight from the growers has risen every season.

Leadership is uneasy. The eight-week spring peak produces almost half the year’s revenue, and during it the checkout queues run long enough that staff watch customers abandon loaded carts. No store can see another store’s stock, so a customer is told an item is unavailable while a store twelve miles away holds forty of them. There is no online ordering, no record of what any customer has bought before, and no way to tell which of the fourteen stores is quietly unprofitable until the year closes. Before investing in new technology, the executive team wants to understand how information systems could strengthen Harbor & Pine’s competitive position and support its long-term strategy.

What the executives have told you

  • Roughly 46 percent of annual revenue arrives in the eight weeks of spring, and about a fifth of spring transactions are abandoned at the queue on the busiest weekends.
  • Stock is counted by hand at each store on a Sunday; by the following Friday the count is “more of a rumour than a number,” in the operations manager’s words.
  • Plants are perishable inventory: a tray unsold at the wrong end of the season is scrapped, and last year scrapped stock came to about 7 percent of purchases.
  • Deliveries arrive from eleven growers on eleven schedules, and freight has risen faster than the prices Harbor & Pine can charge.
  • Landscapers and small contractors buy in volume, ask for terms, and account for a growing share of revenue — but nobody can say how much, because there is no customer record.
  • The company has a website with photographs, opening hours and a telephone number.
Nothing in this scenario is drawn from any real company. If a detail sounds familiar for reasons outside this exercise, that is a coincidence — analyse this Harbor & Pine and only this one, using the evidence above plus any assumption you are willing to state in writing.
Before you start

Choose a company to compare against

Choose one real retail or e-commerce company to use as a point of comparison with Harbor & Pine. Look at its competitive landscape, what it has done with technology, and the difficulties it is working through now. The goal is not admiration; it is to gather enough understanding to draw a clear parallel, and then to use that parallel to sharpen your view of where technology would make the greatest strategic difference at Harbor & Pine.

Pick a company whose relevant practice is actually documented somewhere you can read. A famous company whose inner workings are undescribed gives you a name and nothing else, and a lesson that only works at that company’s budget or footprint is not a lesson that transfers to fourteen garden centres.
Your tasks

Four tasks, one analysis

Work them in order. Each one depends on the one before it, which is also the order a real analyst is obliged to work in: name the pressure before naming the fix, and name the activity before naming the product.

6 pts

Task 1 · Apply the five competitive forces

Evaluate the competitive pressures facing Harbor & Pine. For each of the five forces, write one or two sentences saying whether it is weak, moderate or strong, and give the evidence from the scenario — or a reasonable assumption you state plainly — that supports the rating. Finish with one sentence naming the single strongest force and saying why it beats the other four.

6 pts

Task 2 · Use the value chain to find the opportunities

Using the value chain, identify at least two internal areas — primary activities or support bands — where information systems could create an operational or customer-focused advantage. For each area: name the activity, describe the specific weakness the scenario points to, and say briefly what a system would change there. Two is a floor rather than a target: the second area is what shows you scanned the chain instead of stopping at your first idea, and it becomes the alternative your recommendation was preferred over.

8 pts

Task 3 · Recommend exactly one IT initiative

Recommend one initiative, drawn from one of the areas you named, that would directly support Harbor & Pine’s strategic goals. Do not list options and let the reader choose — one initiative is a decision, three are a wish list. Cover all four parts:

  • What it does — concrete enough that somebody could cost it and later judge whether it shipped. Not “improve our digital capabilities.”
  • Which activity it belongs to, and where the saving appears — these are frequently not the same activity, and naming both is what stops the obvious objection.
  • Which force it answers — tie it back to Task 1 explicitly. A recommendation that cannot be traced to a pressure is a preference.
  • How you would know it worked — one measure, with the number you expect it to move, and the date you would look at it.
5 pts

Task 4 · Compare against the real company, in 150 to 200 words

Write a short summary explaining how the two companies complement each other and what Harbor & Pine can draw from the comparison. Say what the company you chose actually did, what makes it worth comparing even though the two are almost certainly a different size, and the one lesson that would still make sense at fourteen stores. Stay inside the word band: shorter usually means the evidence went missing, longer usually means history and admiration crept in.

0 words Aim for 150–200 words.
Before you hand it in

Check the shape of what you wrote

This checks structure, not correctness — it cannot tell whether your reasoning is any good, and a full row of ticks is not a grade. What it can catch is the handful of omissions that cost marks on this kind of analysis every single time.

    Where to practise the two frameworks first

    • Five Forces and the Value Chain — a working review: rate an industry force by force against an analyst’s reading, sort evidence to the force it actually demonstrates, place a proposed system on the chain, and work three case studies that need both models at once.

    One habit worth copying from the worked cases. Write the sentence “the strongest force is X, and the evidence is Y” before you write anything about technology. Nearly every weak analysis of this kind is a product recommendation that was decided first and given a diagnosis afterwards.
